'''Tajikistan''',{{efn|{{IPAc-en|audio=En-us-Tajikistan.ogg|t|ɑː|ˈ|dʒ|iː|k|ɪ|s|t|ɑː|n}}, {{IPAc-en|t|ə|-|,_|t|æ|-}}; {{langx|tg|Тоҷикистон|Tojikiston|Land of the [[Tajiks]]}}, {{IPA|tg|tʰɔdʒɪkʰɪsˈtʰɔn|pron}}. Alternatively spelled as ''Tadzhikistan''; {{langx|ru|Таджикистан}}}} officially the '''Republic of Tajikistan''',{{efn|{{langx|tg|Ҷумҳурии Тоҷикистон|Jumhurii Tojikiston|engvar=gb}}, {{IPA|tg|dʒʊmhʊˈɾijɪ tʰɔdʒɪkʰɪsˈtʰɔn|pron}}}} is a [[landlocked country]] located in [[Central Asia]]. [[Dushanbe]] is the [[capital city|capital]] and largest city of the country, with a population of over 1.2 million.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Dušanbe (City, Tajikistan) - Population Statistics, Charts, Map and Location |url=https://www.citypopulation.de/en/tajikistan/admin/4__du%C5%A1anbe/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241113003542/https://www.citypopulation.de/en/tajikistan/admin/4__du%C5%A1anbe/ |archive-date=13 November 2024 |access-date=2026-02-15 |website=www.citypopulation.de |language=en |url-status=live }}</ref> Tajikistan borders [[Afghanistan]] to the [[Afghanistan–Tajikistan border|south]], [[Uzbekistan]] to the [[Tajikistan–Uzbekistan border|west]], [[Kyrgyzstan]] to the [[Kyrgyzstan–Tajikistan border|north]], and [[China]] to the [[China–Tajikistan border|east]]; it is narrowly separated from [[Pakistan]] by Afghanistan's [[Wakhan Corridor]]. Its total population exceeds 11{{nbsp}}million.<ref>{{Cite web |title=World Bank Open Data |url=https://data.worldbank.org/ |access-date=2026-03-07 |website=World Bank Open Data}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=Tajikistan Population (2025) |url=http://www.worldometers.info/world-population/tajikistan-population/ |access-date=2025-06-25 |website=Worldometer |language=en}}</ref>

The territory was previously home to cultures of the [[Neolithic]] and the [[Bronze Age]], including the [[Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex|Oxus civilisation]] in the west. Parts of the country were part of the [[Sogdia]]n and [[Bactria]]n civilisations, and were ruled by the [[Achaemenid Empire|Achaemenids]], [[Alexander the Great]], the [[Greco-Bactrian Kingdom|Greco‑Bactrians]], the [[Kushan Empire|Kushans]], the [[Kidarites]], the [[Hephthalites]], the [[First Turkic Khaganate]], the [[Umayyad Caliphate|Umayyad]] and [[Abbasid Caliphate]]s, the [[Samanid Empire]], the [[Kara-Khanid Khanate|Kara‑Khanids]], the [[Seljuk Empire|Seljuks]], the [[Khwarazmian Empire|Khwarazmians]], the [[Mongol Empire|Mongols]], the [[Timurid Empire|Timurids]], and the [[Khanate of Bukhara]]. The region was [[Russian conquest of Central Asia|later conquered]] by the [[Russian Empire]], before becoming part of the [[Soviet Union]]. Within the Soviet Union, the [[National delimitation in the Soviet Union#National delimitation in Central Asia|country's borders were drawn]] when it was part of [[Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic|Uzbekistan]] as an [[Tajik Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic|autonomous republic]], before becoming a [[Tajik Soviet Socialist Republic|constituent republic]] of the Soviet Union on 5 December 1929.<ref>Bergne, Paul (2007) ''The Birth of Tajikistan: National Identity and the Origins of the Republic'', IB Taurus & Co Ltd, pg. 39–40</ref>

On 9 September 1991, Tajikistan declared itself an independent sovereign state as [[Dissolution of the Soviet Union|the Soviet Union was disintegrating]]. A [[Tajikistani Civil War|civil war]] was fought after independence, lasting from May 1992 to June 1997. Since the end of the war, newly established political stability and foreign aid have allowed the country's economy to grow. The country has been led since 1994 by [[Emomali Rahmon]], who heads an [[Authoritarianism|authoritarian regime]] and whose [[Human rights in Tajikistan|human rights record]] has been criticised.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2019/country-chapters/tajikistan|title=World Report 2019: Rights Trends in Tajikistan|date=15 January 2019|work=Human Rights Watch|access-date=13 May 2020|language=en|archive-date=24 February 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190224074509/https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2019/country-chapters/tajikistan|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|last=Welle (www.dw.com)|first=Deutsche|title=Tajikistan's eternal ruler Emomali Rakhmon {{!}} DW {{!}} 12 October 2020|url=https://www.dw.com/en/tajikistans-eternal-ruler-emomali-rakhmon/a-55234401|access-date=5 July 2021|website=DW.COM|language=en-GB|archive-date=6 July 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210706140240/https://www.dw.com/en/tajikistans-eternal-ruler-emomali-rakhmon/a-55234401|url-status=live}}</ref>

Tajikistan is a [[Presidential system|presidential republic]] consisting of [[Regions of Tajikistan|four provinces]]. [[Tajiks]] form the ethnic majority in the country,<ref>{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Ethnic groups – Demographics |url=https://www.indexmundi.com/tajikistan/ethnic_groups.html |website=www.indexmundi.com |language=en |access-date=30 May 2019 |archive-date=30 May 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190530140927/https://www.indexmundi.com/tajikistan/ethnic_groups.html |url-status=live }}</ref> and their national language is [[Tajik language|Tajik]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=People of Tajikistan |url=https://www.britannica.com/place/Tajikistan/People#ref1265422 |access-date=16 November 2023 |archive-date=25 February 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225231634/https://www.britannica.com/place/Tajikistan/People#ref1265422 |url-status=live }}</ref> [[Russian language|Russian]] is used as the official inter‑ethnic language.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tajik-people | title=Tajik &#124; Central Asian, Persian, Pamiri, & Iranian Language &#124; Britannica | date=18 September 2024 }}</ref> While the state is [[Secular state|constitutionally secular]], [[Islam in Tajikistan|Islam]] is adhered to by 97.5% of the population. In the [[Gorno-Badakhshan]] [[oblast]], there is considerable linguistic diversity, with languages such as [[Rushani dialect|Rushani]], [[Shughni language|Shughni]], [[Ishkashimi language|Ishkashimi]], [[Wakhi language|Wakhi]], and Tajik being spoken. Mountains cover more than 90% of the country. It is a [[developing country]] with a [[Transition economy|transitional economy]] that is dependent on [[remittance]]s and on the production of [[aluminium]] and [[cotton]]. Tajikistan is a member of the [[United Nations]], [[Commonwealth of Independent States|CIS]], [[Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe|OSCE]], [[Organisation of Islamic Cooperation|OIC]], [[Economic Cooperation Organization|ECO]], [[Shanghai Cooperation Organisation|SCO]], and [[Collective Security Treaty Organization|CSTO]], and is a [[NATO]] [[Partnership for Peace|PfP partner]].

== Etymology {{anchor|Etymology}} ==
{{Main|Tajik people}}

The term '[[Tajik (word)|Tajik]]' ultimately derives from the [[Middle Persian]] ''Tāzīk'', the [[Turkic languages|Turkic]] rendition of the [[Arabic]] ethnonym ''[[Tayy|Ṭayyi’]]'', denoting a [[Qahtanite]] [[Arab tribe]] that migrated to the [[Transoxiana]] region of [[Central Asia]] in the 7th century AD.<ref>Ali Shir Nava'i ''Muhakamat al-lughatain'' tr. & ed. Robert Devereaux (Leiden: Brill) 1966 p6</ref> Before 1991, Tajikistan appeared in English as ''Tadjikistan'' or ''Tadzhikistan''. This is owing to a transliteration from the {{langx|ru|"Таджикистан"}}. In Russian, there is no single letter 'j' to represent the phoneme {{ipa|/d͡ʒ/}}; instead, {{script|Cyrl|дж}} (transliterated as ''dzh'') is used. ''Tadzhikistan'' is an alternative spelling and is found in English‑language literature derived from Russian sources.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Gleason |first1=Gregory W. |title=The Central Asian States: Discovering Independence |date=8 October 2018 |publisher=Routledge |isbn=978-0-429-96532-6 |page=xv |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=9_7EDwAAQBAJ |language=en}}</ref>

Cultures in the region have been dated back to at least the fourth millennium BC, including the [[Bronze Age]] [[Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex]], the [[Andronovo culture]] and the pro-urban site of [[Sarazm]], a UNESCO [[World Heritage Site]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1141|title=Proto-urban Site of Sarazm – UNESCO World Heritage Centre|first=UNESCO World Heritage|last=Centre|work=unesco.org|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304033948/http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1141|archive-date=4 March 2016}}</ref>

The earliest recorded history of the region dates back to about 500 BC when most, if not all, of Tajikistan was a part of the [[Achaemenid Empire]].<ref name="lcweb2.loc.gov">[https://lccn.loc.gov/97005110 A Country Study: Tajikistan, Ethnic Background] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200517164239/https://catalog.loc.gov/vwebv/search?searchCode=LCCN&searchArg=97005110&searchType=1&permalink=y |date=17 May 2020 }}. See: Geographical and Economic Studies in the Mahābhārata: Upāyana Parva, 1945, p 11–13, Moti Chandra – India; ''Geographical Data in the Early Purāṇas: A Critical Study'', 1972, p 165/66, M. R. These Kambojas were of [[Iranian peoples|Iranian]] affinities who lived in [[Pamir Mountains|Pamir]]s and [[Badakshan]]. [[Xuanzang]] calls this region ''Kiumito'' which is thought to be ''Komdei'' of [[Ptolemy]] and ''Kumadh'' or ''Kumedh'' of [[Muslim]] writers (See: Studies in Indian History and Civilization, Agra, p 351; India and the World, 1964, p 71, Dr Buddha Prakash; India and Central Asia, 1955, p 35, P. C. Bagch).</ref> before they became part of the Achaemenid Empire.<ref>See: The Deeds of Harsha: Being a Cultural Study of Bāṇa's Harshacharita, 1969, p 199, Dr Vasudeva Sharana Agrawala; Proceedings and Transactions of the All-India Oriental Conference, 1930, p 118, Dr J. Vidyalankara; Prācīna Kamboja, jana aura janapada =: Ancient Kamboja, people and country, 1981, Dr Jiyālāla Kāmboja, Dr Satyavrat Śāstrī – Kamboja (Pakistan).</ref> After the region's conquest by [[Alexander the Great]] it became part of the [[Greco-Bactrian Kingdom]], a successor state of Alexander's empire. Northern Tajikistan (the cities of [[Khujand]] and [[Panjakent]]) was a part of [[Sogdiana]], a collection of city-states which were overrun by [[Scytho-Siberians]] and [[Yuezhi]] nomadic tribes around 150 BC. The [[Silk Road]] passed through the region and following the expedition of Chinese explorer [[Zhang Qian]] during the reign of [[Emperor Wu of Han|Wudi]] (141 BC – 87 BC) commercial relations between [[Han Empire]] and Sogdiana flourished.<ref>[http://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=18006 C. Michael Hogan, "Silk Road, North China", The Megalithic Portal, ed. Andy Burnham] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131002140921/http://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=18006 |date=2 October 2013 }}. Megalithic.co.uk. Retrieved on 20 January 2017.</ref><ref>[[Records of the Grand Historian|Shiji]], trans. Burton Watson</ref> Sogdians played a role in facilitating trade and worked in other capacities, as farmers, carpetweavers, glassmakers, and woodcarvers.<ref>[[Frances Wood]] (2002) ''The Silk Road: Two Thousand Years in the Heart of Asia''. University of California Press. p. 66. {{ISBN|978-0-520-23786-5}}.</ref><!--an unsourced Indian assertion? However, the Sogdian traders were then still less important in the Silk Road trade than their southern neighbors, Indians and Bactrians.-->

The [[Kushan Empire]], a collection of Yuezhi tribes, took control of the region in the first century AD and ruled until the fourth century AD during which time [[Buddhism]], [[Nestorian Christianity]], [[Zoroastrianism]], and [[Manichaeism]] were practised in the region.<ref name="Congress Call Number DK851?">[https://www.loc.gov/rr/frd/cs/profiles/Tajikistan.pdf Tajikistan] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161221014613/https://loc.gov/rr/frd/cs/profiles/Tajikistan.pdf |date=21 December 2016 }}. loc.gov.</ref> Later the [[Hephthalite Empire]], a collection of nomadic tribes, moved into the region, and the [[Arabs]] [[Spread of Islam|propagated Islam]] in the eighth century.<ref name="Congress Call Number DK851?" />

=== Samanid Empire ===
{{Main|Samanid Empire}}
[[File:MansurISamanidPaintingHistoryofIran.jpg|thumb|The [[Samanid]] ruler [[Mansur I]] (961–976)]]
Four brothers – Nuh, Ahmad, Yahya, and Ilyas – founded the [[Samanid Empire]]. Each of them ruled territory under [[Abbasid]] suzerainty. In 892, [[Ismail Samani]] (892–907) united the Samanid state under a single ruler, thereby putting an end to the feudal system that had previously prevailed. It was under his rule that the Samanids became independent of Abbasid authority. The Samanid Empire (819–999) restored [[Persian people|Persian]] control of the region and expanded the cities of [[Samarkand]] and [[Bukhara]], which became the cultural centres of the [[Greater Iran|Iranian world]]; the region was known as [[Khorasan]]. The empire was centred on Khorasan and [[Transoxiana]], and at its greatest extent encompassed Afghanistan, parts of Iran, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, parts of Kazakhstan, and Pakistan.

The Samanids revived [[Persian culture]] by patronising [[Rudaki]],<ref>{{cite book |author=J. Calmard |title=The Encyclopedia of Islam |publisher=Brill |year=1993 |editor1=C. E. Bosworth |volume=VII |page=18 |chapter=Mihragan |editor2=E. van Donzel |editor3=W. P. Heinrichs |editor4=C. Pellat}}</ref> [[Bal'ami]], and [[Daqiqi]].<ref>{{cite book |last=Bosworth |first=C.E. |title=The Ghaznavids: 994–1040 |publisher=Edinburgh University Press |year=1963 |page=131}}</ref> The Samanids actively promoted Sunni Islam and suppressed [[Ismaili]] Shi'ism,<ref>An Ismaili Heresiography: The "Bab Al-Shaytan" from Abu Tammam's Kitab Al ... By Wilferd Madelung, Paul Ernest Walker, p. 5.</ref> but were more tolerant of [[Twelver]] Shi'ism. Islamic architecture and Perso‑Islamic culture were spread deep into the heartlands of Central Asia by the Samanids. Following the first complete translation of the Qur'an into Persian in the 9th century, populations under the Samanid Empire began to accept Islam in significant numbers.

The Karluk Turkic [[Kara-Khanid Khanate]] conquered Transoxania (which corresponded approximately to what would later become Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, southern Kyrgyzstan, and south‑west Kazakhstan) and ruled there from 999 to 1211.<ref name="encyclo">{{cite web|url=http://search.eb.com/eb/article-9062103|title=Encyclopedia – Britannica Online Encyclopedia|work=eb.com|access-date=2 June 2014|archive-date=2 December 2008|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081202055513/http://search.eb.com/eb/article-9062103|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |author=Grousset, Rene |title=The Empire of the Steppes |publisher=Rutgers University Press |year=2004 |isbn=978-0-8135-1304-1 }}</ref> Their arrival in Transoxania marked a definitive shift from Iranian to Turkic predominance in Central Asia.<ref name="svat">{{cite book |title=A history of Inner Asia |author=Svatopluk Soucek |chapter=Chapter 5 – The Qarakhanids |publisher=Cambridge University Press |year=2000 |isbn=978-0-521-65704-4 |chapter-url-access=registration |chapter-url=https://archive.org/details/historyofinneras00souc }}</ref> Over time, the Kara‑Khanids became assimilated into the Perso‑Arab Muslim culture of the region.<ref>[http://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ilak-khanids ilak-khanids] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150909080737/http://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ilak-khanids |date=9 September 2015 }}: Iranica. accessed May 2014.</ref>

The region was later ruled by the [[Ghurid dynasty|Ghurid]] and [[Kart dynasty|Kart dynasties]],<ref>Daʿadli, Tawfiq (9 April 2019). Esoteric Images: Decoding the Late Herat School of Painting. BRILL. p. 7. ISBN 978-90-04-39841-2.</ref> both of [[Tajiks|Tajik]] origin.<ref name="RE2">{{Cite book |author=[[Richard M. Eaton|Richard Eaton]] |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=tLXXAAAAMAAJ&q=Rajputs |title=Essays on Islam and Indian History |date=2000 |publisher=Oxford University Press |isbn=978-0-19-565114-0 |page=100 |language=en |quote=The dynamics of north Indian politics changed dramatically, however, when the Ghurids, a dynasty of Tajik (eastern Iranian), origin arrived from central Afghanistan towards the end of twelfth century}}</ref><ref>[[Encyclopaedia of Islam]], "Ghurids", C.E. Bosworth, Online Edition, 2006: ''"... The Shansabānīs were, like the rest of the <u>Gh</u>ūrīs, of eastern Iranian Tājik stock ..."''</ref>{{sfn|Wink|2020|p=78}}<ref>Cynthia Talbot, ''The Last Hindu Emperor: Prithviraj Chauhan and the Indian Past, 1200–2000'', (Cambridge University Press, 2016), 36.</ref> While the Kart dynasty ruled in present‑day Afghanistan, [[Muhammad of Ghor]], a ruler of the Ghurid dynasty, [[Ghurid campaigns in India|conquered large parts of India]].

In the 13th century, the [[Mongol Empire]] swept through Central Asia, invaded the [[Khwarezmian Empire]], and sacked its cities, looting and massacring their inhabitants. The [[Turco-Mongol tradition|Turco‑Mongol]] conqueror [[Timur|Tamerlane]] founded the [[Timurid Empire]], becoming the first ruler of the [[Timurid dynasty]] in what later became Tajikistan and Central Asia.<ref name="auto"/>

=== Bukharan rule ===
{{Main|Khanate of Bukhara}}
[[File:Thomas Edward Gordon Lake Victoria, Great Pamir, May 2nd, 1874.png|thumb|19th-century painting of lake [[Zorkul]] and a local [[Tajik people|Tajik]] inhabitant]]
The territory that later became Tajikistan came under the rule of the [[Khanate of Bukhara]] during the 16th century. Following the khanate's collapse in the 18th century, the region fell under the control of the [[Emirate of Bukhara]] and the [[Khanate of Kokand]]. The Emirate of Bukhara remained intact until the 20th century.

=== Imperial Russia ===
{{Main|Russian conquest of Turkestan}}

{{Further|Russian Turkestan|The Great Game}}

During the 19th century, the Russian Empire began to conquer parts of the region.<ref>{{Cite web|title=History of Central Asia – Under Russian rule|url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Central-Asia|access-date=5 January 2021|website=Encyclopedia Britannica|language=en|archive-date=7 May 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210507015604/https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Central-Asia|url-status=live}}</ref> Russian [[Imperialism]] led to the [[Russian Empire]]'s conquest of Central Asia during the 19th century's [[Great Game|Imperial Era]]. Between 1864 and 1885, Russia gradually took control of the entire territory of [[Russian Turkestan]], the Tajikistan portion of which had been controlled by the Emirate of Bukhara and Khanate of Kokand. Russia was interested in gaining access to a supply of [[cotton]] and in the 1870s attempted to switch cultivation in the region from grain to cotton (a strategy later copied and expanded by the Soviets).<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Whitman |first1=John |title=Turkestan Cotton in Imperial Russia |journal=American Slavic and East European Review |date=1956 |volume=15 |issue=2 |publisher=Association for Slavic and Eurasian Studies |location=Cambridge University |pages=190–205 |doi=10.2307/3000976 |jstor=3000976 |url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/3000976 |access-date=5 February 2021 |archive-date=18 August 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210818153542/https://www.jstor.org/stable/3000976 |url-status=live  |issn=1049-7544|url-access=subscription }}</ref>

During the 19th century, the [[Jadid]]ists established themselves as an Islamic social movement throughout the region. While the Jadidists were pro-modernisation and not necessarily anti-Russian, the Russians viewed the movement as a threat because the Russian Empire was predominantly Christian.<ref>{{cite web |last1=Khalid |first1=Adeeb |title=Jadidism in Central Asia: Origins, Development, and Fate Under the Soviets |url=https://mesbar.org/jadidism-in-central-asia-origins-development-and-fate-under-the-soviets/ |website=Al Mesbar Studies and Research Centre |date=10 April 2018 |access-date=5 February 2021 |archive-date=1 March 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210301165850/https://mesbar.org/jadidism-in-central-asia-origins-development-and-fate-under-the-soviets/ |url-status=live }}</ref> Russian troops were required to restore order during uprisings against the Khanate of Kokand between 1910 and 1913. Further violence occurred in July 1916 when demonstrators attacked Russian soldiers in Khujand over the threat of forced [[conscription]] during [[World War I]]. While Russian troops brought Khujand back under control, clashes continued throughout the year in various locations in Tajikistan.<ref>{{cite web |title=Tajikistan – The Russian Conquest |url=http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/4.htm |website=Country Studies |access-date=5 February 2021 |archive-date=26 October 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211026092613/http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/4.htm |url-status=live }}</ref>

=== Soviet period ===
{{Main|Basmachi movement|Tajik Soviet Socialist Republic}}

[[File:Negotiations with basmachs Fergana, 1921.jpg|thumb|Soviet negotiations with ''[[Basmachi movement|basmachi]]'', 1921]]

After the [[Russian Revolution]] of 1917, guerrillas throughout Central Asia, known as ''[[basmachi]]'', waged [[Kokand Autonomy|a war]] against [[Bolshevik]] armies in an attempt to maintain independence.<ref name="auto">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan profile – Timeline |url=https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-16201087 |access-date=31 January 2021 |work=BBC News |publisher=BBC |date=31 July 2018 |archive-date=2 May 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210502174022/https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-16201087 |url-status=live }}</ref> The Bolsheviks prevailed after a four‑year war, during which [[mosque]]s and villages were burned down and the population was suppressed.

Between 1928 and 1941, Soviet authorities began an [[USSR anti-religious campaign (1928–1941)|anti‑religious campaign]] of [[secularization|secularisation]]. Practising [[Christianity]], [[Islam]], or [[Judaism]] was discouraged and repressed; under [[Soviet anti-religious legislation]], several [[Church (building)|churches]], [[mosque]]s, and [[synagogue]]s were closed.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Pipes|first1=Richard|title=Muslims of Soviet Central Asia: Trends and Prospects (Part I)|journal=Middle East Journal|date=1955|volume=9|issue=2|pages=149–150|jstor=4322692}}</ref> As a consequence of the conflict and Soviet agricultural policies, Central Asia, including Tajikistan, suffered a famine that claimed many lives.<ref>{{cite web|title=A Country Study: Tajikistan, Impact of the Civil War|url=http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/6.htm |website=Country Studies US |publisher=U.S. Library of Congress|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304210235/http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/6.htm|archive-date=4 March 2016}}</ref>

Tajikistan was first created in 1924 as the [[Tajik Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic]] (abbreviated as the Tajik ASSR), an autonomous republic within the [[Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic]].{{sfn|Heathershaw|Herzig|2013|p=1}} Despite their historical status as Tajik cities and their largely Tajik populations, none of the regional urban centres – such as [[Bukhara]], [[Samarkand]], [[Tirmiz]], [[Qarshi]], and Khojand – were included in Tajikistan. Instead, the Tajikistani capital was established in [[Dushanbe]], a mountain town with approximately 1,000 residents.{{sfn|Foltz|2019|p=119}}

A specific Persian dialect, with vocabulary and pronunciation distinct from formal written Persian, was deliberately made the national language of Tajikistan by the Soviets and renamed 'Tajiki' in order to forcibly separate it from the common variant of Persian spoken from [[Istanbul]] to [[Calcutta]]. The Tajiks were also excluded from the enormous global world of Persian literature, both past and present, by the creation of a new phonetic alphabet – first Latin‑based in 1928 and later Cyrillic in 1940.{{sfn|Foltz|2019|p=118}} With Persian confined to Tajikistan and replaced by Russian as Central Asia's common language, the unity of the [[Persianate]] world was broken, steering Soviet and post‑Soviet Central Asia on to a developmental path distinct from that of Iran and Afghanistan.{{sfn|Arjomand|2025|p=344}} In 1929, the Tajik ASSR was elevated to its own full [[Republics of the Soviet Union|union republic]], the [[Tajik Soviet Socialist Republic]]. The [[Sughd Region]] was added to Tajikistan in the same year.{{sfn|Foltz|2019|p=120}}

Between 1927 and 1934, [[collectivization of agriculture|collectivisation of agriculture]] and an expansion of cotton production took place, especially in the southern region.<ref name="countrystudies.us">{{cite web|url=http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/9.htm|title=Tajikistan – Collectivization|work=Country Studies US|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121014202805/http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/9.htm|archive-date=14 October 2012}}</ref> Soviet collectivization policy brought violence against farmers and peasants, classified as [[anti-Soviet]] categories of "[[enemies of the people]]", and [[Population transfer in the Soviet Union|forced resettlement]] occurred throughout Tajikistan. Consequently, some peasants fought collectivisation and revived the [[Basmachi movement]]. Some industrial development occurred during this time along with the expansion of irrigation infrastructure.<ref name="countrystudies.us" />

[[File:Hammond Slides Central Asia Unlabeled 15.jpg|thumb|Soviet Tajikistan in 1964]]
Two rounds of [[Great Purge|Stalin's purges]] (1927–1934 and 1937–1938) resulted in the expulsion of nearly 10,000 people from all levels of the [[Communist Party of Tajikistan]].<ref name="The Purges 1996">{{cite web|url=http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/10.htm|title=Tajikistan – The Purges|work=Country Studies US|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121014204552/http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/10.htm|archive-date=14 October 2012}}</ref> Ethnic [[Russians]] were sent in to replace those expelled and subsequently Russians dominated party positions at all levels, including the top position of first secretary.<ref name="The Purges 1996"/> Between 1926 and 1959 the proportion of Russians among Tajikistan's population grew from less than 1% to 13%.<ref>{{cite web |url-status=live |url=http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/23.htm |title=Tajikistan – Ethnic Groups |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101207115900/http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/23.htm |archive-date=7 December 2010 |agency=U.S. Library of Congress |website=Country Studies US }}</ref> [[Bobojon Ghafurov]], [[First Secretary of the Communist Party of Tajikistan]] from 1946 to 1956, was the only Tajik politician of significance outside of the republic during the Soviet Era.<ref name="ReferenceB"/>

Tajiks began to be conscripted into the [[Red Army]] in 1939, and during the [[Second World War]] around 260,000 Tajik citizens fought against [[Nazi Germany]], [[Finland in World War II|Finland]], and the [[Empire of Japan]]. Between 60,000 (4%)<ref>Kamoludin Abdullaev and Shahram Akbarzaheh (2010) ''Historical Dictionary of Tajikistan'', 2nd ed. p. 383. {{ISBN|0810860619}}.</ref> and 120,000 (8%)<ref>Vadim Erlikman (2004). ''Poteri narodonaseleniia v XX veke''. Moscow. pp. 23–35. {{ISBN|5-93165-107-1}}</ref> of Tajikistan's 1,530,000 citizens were killed during the war.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://ww2db.com/country/tajikistan|title=Tajikistan in World War II|author=C. Peter Chen|work=WW2DB|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140726045015/http://ww2db.com/country/tajikistan|archive-date=26 July 2014|access-date=15 June 2014}}</ref>

Following the war and the end of Stalin's rule, attempts were made to further expand the agriculture and industry of Tajikistan.<ref name="ReferenceB">{{cite web|url=http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/11.htm|title=Tajikistan – The Postwar Period|work=Country Studies US|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121014205135/http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/11.htm|archive-date=14 October 2012}}</ref> During 1957–58, [[Nikita Khrushchev]]'s [[Virgin Lands Campaign]] focused attention on Tajikistan, where living conditions, education, and industry lagged behind those of the other [[Republics of the Soviet Union|Soviet republics]].<ref name="ReferenceB"/> In the 1980s, Tajikistan had the lowest household saving rate in the USSR,<ref>Boris Rumer (1989) ''Soviet Central Asia: A Tragic Experiment'', Unwin Hyman, London. p. 126. {{ISBN|0044451466}}.</ref> the lowest percentage of households in the two top per capita income groups,<ref>''Statistical Yearbook of the USSR 1990'', Goskomstat, Moscow, 1991, p. 115 {{in lang|ru}}.</ref> and the lowest rate of university graduates per 1,000 people.<ref>''Statistical Yearbook of the USSR 1990'', Goskomstat, Moscow, 1991, p. 210 {{in lang|ru}}.</ref>

By the 1980s, Tajik nationalists were calling for increased rights. Real disturbances did not occur within the republic until 1990. The following year, the [[Soviet Union]] collapsed, and Tajikistan declared its independence on 9 September 1991, a day which is celebrated as the country's [[Independence Day (Tajikistan)|Independence Day]].<ref>{{cite news |url=https://frontnews.eu/news/en/12776/Tajikistan-celebrates-Independence-Day |title=Tajikistan celebrates Independence Day |work=Front News International |date=9 September 2017 |access-date=7 September 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180907221425/https://frontnews.eu/news/en/12776/Tajikistan-celebrates-Independence-Day |archive-date=7 September 2018 |url-status=dead }}</ref>

=== Independence ===
{{multiple image
|image1=RIAN archive 699861 Dushanbe riots, February 1990.jpg
|caption1=[[1990 Dushanbe riots]]
|image2=RIAN archive 466493 Rally on Ozodi square.jpg
|caption2=Tajik men and women rally on [[Dousti Square|Ozodi square]] in Dushanbe after independence, 1992.
}}
In February 1990, [[1990 Dushanbe riots|riots and strikes]] in Dushanbe and other cities began because of the difficult socio‑economic situation, a shortage of housing, and youth unemployment.<ref name="auto"/> The nationalist and democratic opposition, along with supporters of independence, joined the strikes and began to demand the independence of the republic and democratic reforms. [[Islamism|Islamists]] also began to hold strikes to demand respect for their rights and independence. The Soviet leadership introduced Internal Troops in Dushanbe to put an end to the unrest.<ref name="auto"/>

Following independence, the nation fell into [[Tajikistani Civil War|civil war]] among factions distinguished by clan loyalties.<ref name="United Nations">{{cite web|title=Tajikistan: rising from the ashes of civil war|url=https://www.un.org/events/tenstories/06/story.asp?storyID=600|publisher=United Nations|access-date=10 August 2014|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140812220732/http://www.un.org/events/tenstories/06/story.asp?storyID=600|archive-date=12 August 2014}}</ref> Regional groups from the [[Gharm]] and [[Gorno-Badakhshan]] regions of Tajikistan, led by a combination of [[liberal democracy|liberal democratic]] reformers<ref>{{Cite web|last=Dubovitsky|first=Viktor|url=http://www.analitika.org/article.php?story=20060307230526550&mode=print|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080411201135/http://www.analitika.org/article.php?story=20060307230526550&mode=print|title=Features of the ethnic and confessional situation in the Republic of Tajikistan|date=February 2003|archive-date=11 April 2008|url-status=usurped|language=ru}}</ref> and [[Islamists]], who eventually became the [[United Tajik Opposition]], rose up against the newly formed government of President [[Rahmon Nabiyev]], which was dominated by people from the Khujand and [[Kulob]] regions.

The war lasted until 1997. More than 500,000 residents fled during this time because of persecution and increased poverty, seeking better economic opportunities in the West or in other former Soviet republics.<ref>{{cite web|title=Human Rights Watch World Report 1994: Tajikistan|url=https://www.hrw.org/reports/1994/WR94/Helsinki-20.htm|publisher=Human Rights Watch|access-date=10 August 2014|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150924164340/http://www.hrw.org/reports/1994/WR94/Helsinki-20.htm|archive-date=24 September 2015}}</ref> The estimated number of dead exceeded 100,000. Around 1.2 million people were [[refugee]]s, both inside and outside the country.<ref name="United Nations" />

[[Emomali Rahmon]] came to power in the early part of this conflict in 1992,<ref name="auto"/> after Nabiyev was forced at gunpoint on 7 September 1992 to resign from office.<ref>{{Cite web|date=17 November 1992|title=Ситуация в Таджикистане|url=https://www.kommersant.ru/doc/30157|access-date=17 May 2021|website=www.kommersant.ru|language=ru}}</ref> Rahmon defeated the former prime minister [[Abdumalik Abdullajanov]] in a presidential election in November with 58% of the vote.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.wrmea.com/backissues/0695/9506053.htm |title=Telling the truth for more than 30 years – Tajikistan After the Elections: Post-Soviet Dictatorship |work=Washington Report on Middle East Affairs |date=June 1995 |access-date=13 September 2013 |archive-date=22 June 2006 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20060622035642/http://www.wrmea.com/backissues/0695/9506053.htm |url-status=live }}</ref>

In 1997, a [[ceasefire]] was reached between Rahmon and opposition parties under the guidance of Gerd D. Merrem,<ref name="auto"/> Special Representative to the Secretary General, a result praised as a successful United Nations peacekeeping initiative. The ceasefire guaranteed 30% of ministerial positions would go to the [[United Tajik Opposition|opposition]].<ref>{{cite web|author1=Jim Nichol|title=Central Asia's Security: Issues and Implications for U.S. Interests|url=https://fas.org/sgp/crs/row/RL30294.pdf|publisher=Federation of American Scientists|access-date=10 August 2014|page=8|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120520055426/http://www.fas.org/sgp/crs/row/RL30294.pdf|archive-date=20 May 2012}}</ref> [[Tajikistani presidential election, 1999|Elections]] were held in 1999 and were criticised by opposition parties and foreign observers as unfair; Rahmon was re-elected with 98% of the vote.<ref name="auto"/> [[Tajik presidential election, 2006|Elections in 2006]] were again won by Rahmon (with 79% of the vote) and he began his third term in office. Opposition parties boycotted the 2006 election and the [[Organization for Security and Cooperation in Europe]] (OSCE) criticised it, while observers from the [[Commonwealth of Independent States]] claimed the elections were legal and transparent.<ref>{{cite web|title=REPUBLIC OF TAJIKISTAN PRESIDENTIAL ELECTION|url=http://www.osce.org/odihr/elections/tajikistan/24664?download=true|publisher=Organization for Security and Cooperation in Europe|access-date=10 August 2014|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140810080751/http://www.osce.org/odihr/elections/tajikistan/24664?download=true|archive-date=10 August 2014}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|title=OSCE and CIS Observers Disagree on Presidential Election in Tajikistan|url=http://www.neweurasia.net/politics-and-society/osce-and-cis-observers-disagree-on-presidential-election-in-tajikistan/|website=New Eurasia|access-date=10 August 2014|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150722001344/https://www.neweurasia.net/politics-and-society/osce-and-cis-observers-disagree-on-presidential-election-in-tajikistan/|archive-date=22 July 2015}}</ref>

[[File:Spetsnaz troopers during the 1992 Tajik war.jpg|thumb|left|[[Spetsnaz]] soldiers during the [[Tajikistani Civil War|civil war]], 1992]]
Rahmon's administration came under further criticism from OSCE in October 2010 for its censorship and repression of the media. OSCE claimed that the Tajik Government censored Tajik and foreign websites and instituted tax inspections on independent printing houses that led to the cessation of printing activities for a number of independent newspapers.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://af.reuters.com/article/worldNews/idAFTRE69H2FD20101018|work=Reuters|title=OSCE urges Tajikistan to stop attacks on free media|date=18 October 2010|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170628001735/http://af.reuters.com/article/worldNews/idAFTRE69H2FD20101018|archive-date=28 June 2017}}</ref>

Russian border troops were stationed along the Tajik–Afghan border until summer 2005. Since the [[September 11, 2001 attacks]], French troops have been stationed at [[Dushanbe Airport]] in support of air operations of [[NATO]]'s [[International Security Assistance Force]] in Afghanistan. [[United States Army]] and [[United States Marine Corps|Marine Corps]] personnel periodically visit Tajikistan to conduct joint training missions of up to several weeks' duration. The [[Government of India]] rebuilt the [[Ayni Air Base]], a military airport located 15&nbsp;km southwest of Dushanbe, at a cost of $70 million, completing the repairs in September 2010.<ref>{{cite web|last=Kucera|first=Joshua|title=Tajikistan's Ayni airbase opens – but who is using it?|work=The Bug Pit – The military and security in Eurasia|publisher=The Open Society Institute|date=7 September 2010|url=http://www.eurasianet.org/node/61866|access-date=12 September 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130905071620/http://www.eurasianet.org/node/61866|archive-date=5 September 2013}}</ref> It is the main base of the Tajikistan Air Force. There have been talks with Russia concerning use of the Ayni facility,<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.eurasianet.org/node/62187|title=Tajikistan: Dushanbe Dangling Ayni Air Base Before Russia|publisher=EurasiaNet.org|date=19 October 2010|access-date=13 September 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130905123258/http://www.eurasianet.org/node/62187|archive-date=5 September 2013}}</ref> and Russia continues to maintain a base on the outskirts of Dushanbe.<ref>{{cite web|title=Ratification of Russian military base deal provides Tajikistan with important security guarantees|url=http://www.janes.com/article/27898/ratification-of-russian-military-base-deal-provides-tajikistan-with-important-security-guarantees|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140803133141/http://www.janes.com/article/27898/ratification-of-russian-military-base-deal-provides-tajikistan-with-important-security-guarantees|url-status=dead|archive-date=3 August 2014|website=Jane's|access-date=10 August 2014}}</ref>

In 2010, there were concerns among Tajik officials that Islamic militarism in the east of the country was on the rise following the escape of 25 militants from a Tajik prison in August, an ambush that killed 28 Tajik soldiers in the [[Rasht Valley]] in September,<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-asia-pacific-11565443|work=BBC News|title=Tajikistan says restive east is under control|date=18 October 2010|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131115193229/http://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-asia-pacific-11565443|archive-date=15 November 2013}}</ref> and another ambush in the valley in October that killed 30 soldiers,<ref>{{cite news|title=Tajikistan Says Kills Three Suspected Islamist Militants|publisher=Radio Free Europe Radio Liberty|date=18 October 2010|url=https://www.rferl.org/a/Tajikistan_Says_Kills_Three_Suspected_Islamist_Militants/2193377.html|access-date=12 September 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131106020716/http://www.rferl.org/content/Tajikistan_Says_Kills_Three_Suspected_Islamist_Militants/2193377.html|archive-date=6 November 2013}}</ref> followed by fighting outside [[Gharm]] that left three militants dead. The country's Interior Ministry insisted that the central government maintained full control over the country's east, and the military operation in the Rasht Valley was concluded in November 2010.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://enews.fergananews.com/news.php?id=1903&mode=snews |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120415112458/http://enews.fergananews.com/news.php?id=1903&mode=snews |url-status=dead |archive-date=15 April 2012 |title=Tajikistan: The government withdraws troops from the Rasht valley|publisher=Ferghana Information agency, Moscow|date=3 November 2010|access-date=13 September 2013}}</ref>

[[2012 Gorno-Badakhshan clashes|Fighting erupted]] again, this time in and around Gorno-Badakhshan, in July 2012.<ref>{{cite web|author=Khayrullo Fayz|url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-asia-18965366|title=Tajikistan clashes: 'Many dead' in Gorno-Badakhshan|publisher=BBC Uzbek|date=24 July 2012|access-date=13 September 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121218140917/http://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-asia-18965366|archive-date=18 December 2012}}</ref> In 2015, Russia sent more troops to Tajikistan.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.stratfor.com/analysis/why-russia-will-send-more-troops-central-asia|title=Why Russia Will Send More Troops to Central Asia|work=Stratfor|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150927163915/https://www.stratfor.com/analysis/why-russia-will-send-more-troops-central-asia|archive-date=27 September 2015}}</ref>

In May 2015, Tajikistan's national security underwent a setback when Colonel [[Gulmurod Khalimov]], commander of the special-purpose police unit (OMON) of the Interior Ministry, defected to the [[Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant|Islamic State]].<ref name="auto"/><ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mideast-crisis-tajikistan-idUSKBN0OD1AP20150528|work=Reuters|title=Commander of elite Tajik police force defects to Islamic State|date=28 May 2015|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151016042543/http://www.reuters.com/article/2015/05/28/us-mideast-crisis-tajikistan-idUSKBN0OD1AP20150528|archive-date=16 October 2015}}</ref> Khalimov was allegedly killed on 8 September 2017 during a Russian airstrike near [[Deir ez-Zor]], [[Syria]],<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mideast-crisis-syria-russia/russia-says-its-air-strike-kills-several-top-islamic-state-commanders-in-syria-idUSKCN1BJ0KJ|title = Russia says its air strike kills several top Islamic State commanders in Syria|newspaper = Reuters|date = 8 September 2017}}</ref> although Tajikistan authorities express doubts whether he has died.

In 2021, following the [[Fall of Kabul (2021)|Fall of Kabul]], Tajikistan allegedly got involved in the [[Panjshir conflict]] against the [[Taliban]] on the side of the [[National Resistance Front of Afghanistan]].<ref>{{Cite web|date=4 October 2021|title=Afghan resistance has sanctuary in Tajikistan, but fighting Taliban a 'non-viable prospect'|url=https://www.france24.com/en/asia-pacific/20211004-afghan-resistance-has-sanctuary-in-tajikistan-but-fighting-taliban-a-non-viable-prospect|access-date=17 December 2021|website=France 24|language=en|archive-date=13 October 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211013170556/https://www.france24.com/en/asia-pacific/20211004-afghan-resistance-has-sanctuary-in-tajikistan-but-fighting-taliban-a-non-viable-prospect|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|title=Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan diverge on approaches to Afghanistan {{!}} Eurasianet|url=https://eurasianet.org/kyrgyzstan-tajikistan-diverge-on-approaches-to-afghanistan|access-date=17 December 2021|website=eurasianet.org|language=en|archive-date=7 December 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211207155858/https://eurasianet.org/kyrgyzstan-tajikistan-diverge-on-approaches-to-afghanistan|url-status=live}}</ref>

In September 2022 [[2022 Kyrgyzstan–Tajikistan clashes|armed clashes]], including the use of artillery, erupted along most of the border between Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan.<ref>{{cite web |title=Armed clashes erupted all along the border of Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, Kyrgyz government sources reported on Sept. 16. |url=https://english.nv.ua/nation/fighting-breaks-out-between-kyrgyzstan-and-tajikistan-central-asia-news-50270517.html |access-date=17 September 2022 |archive-date=24 January 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230124200452/https://english.nv.ua/nation/fighting-breaks-out-between-kyrgyzstan-and-tajikistan-central-asia-news-50270517.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

== Geography ==
{{Main|Geography of Tajikistan}}

[[File:Tajikistan satellite photo.jpg|thumb|Satellite photograph]]
[[File:Koppen-Geiger_Map_TJK_present.svg|thumb|Map of [[Köppen climate classification]]]]
[[File:Tajik mountains edit.jpg|thumb|Mountains]]
Tajikistan is [[landlocked country|landlocked]] and is the smallest nation in Central Asia by area. It lies mostly between latitudes [[36th parallel north|36°]] and [[41st parallel north|41° N]], and longitudes [[67th meridian east|67°]] and [[75th meridian east|75° E]]. It is covered by mountains of the [[Pamir Mountains|Pamir]] range, and most of the country is over {{convert|3000|m|ft}} above [[sea level]]. The areas of lower land are in the north (part of the [[Fergana Valley]]) and in the southern valleys of the [[Kofarnihon River|Kofarnihon]] and [[Vakhsh River|Vakhsh]] rivers, which form the Amu Darya. [[Dushanbe]] is located on the southern slopes above the Kofarnihon valley.

The [[Amu Darya]] and [[Panj River|Panj]] rivers mark the border with Afghanistan, and the glaciers in Tajikistan's mountains are the source of [[runoff (water)|runoff]] for the [[Aral Sea]]. There are over 900 rivers in Tajikistan longer than 10 kilometres.

[[File:Dushanbe Presidential Palace 01.jpg|thumb|The Palace of Nations in [[Dushanbe]]]]After independence, Tajikistan was plunged into a civil war. Factions were supported by foreign countries including [[Afghanistan]], Iran, Pakistan, [[Uzbekistan]] and Russia. Russia and Iran focused on keeping peace in the warring nation to decrease the chances of [[United States|U.S.]] or [[Turkey|Turkish]] involvement. Russia backed the pro-government faction and deployed troops from the [[Commonwealth of Independent States]] to guard the Tajikistan-Afghan border.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.c-r.org/accord-article/tajik-civil-war-causes-and-dynamics|title=The Tajik civil war: Causes and dynamics|date=30 December 2011|website=Conciliation Resources|language=en|access-date=10 March 2019|archive-date=12 April 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190412223027/https://www.c-r.org/accord-article/tajik-civil-war-causes-and-dynamics|url-status=live}}</ref> All but 25,000 of the more than 400,000 ethnic Russians, who were mostly employed in industry, fled to Russia. By 1997, the war had ended after a [[Tajikistani Civil War|peace agreement]] between the government and the Islamist-led opposition, and a central government began to take form, with peaceful elections in 1999.<ref>{{Cite web|title=How The Tajik President Has Managed To Stay In Power For Nearly Three Decades|url=https://www.rferl.org/a/30884643.html|access-date=5 January 2021|website=RadioFreeEurope/RadioLiberty|language=en|archive-date=4 January 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210104055854/https://www.rferl.org/a/30884643.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

"Longtime observers of Tajikistan often characterize the country as profoundly averse to risk and skeptical of promises of reform, a political passivity they trace to the country's ruinous civil war," Ilan Greenberg wrote in a news article in ''[[The New York Times]]'' before the country's November 2006 presidential election.<ref name=greenberg>Greenberg, Ilan, "Media Muzzled and Opponents Jailed, Tajikistan Readies for Vote", ''[[The New York Times]]'', 4 November 2006 (article dateline 3 November 2006), page A7, New York edition</ref>

[[File:Tajik Parliament House, Dushanbe, Tajikistan.JPG|thumb|[[Supreme Assembly (Tajikistan)|Supreme Assembly]] in [[Dushanbe]].]]

The parliamentary elections of 2005 aroused accusations from opposition parties and international observers that President [[Emomali Rahmon]] corruptly manipulated the election process and unemployment. Elections in February 2010 saw the ruling PDPT lose four seats in Parliament, yet still maintain a majority. The [[Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe]] election observers said the 2010 polling "failed to meet many key OSCE commitments" and that "these elections failed on many basic democratic standards."<ref name="economistchange" /><ref name="BBC2010election" /> The government insisted that only minor violations had occurred, which would not affect the will of the Tajik people.<ref name="economistchange">{{cite news|title=Change you can't believe in|newspaper=[[The Economist]]|date=4 March 2010|url=http://www.economist.com/world/asia/displayStory.cfm?story_id=15612447&source=hptextfeature|access-date=5 March 2010|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161230010535/http://www.economist.com/world/asia/displayStory.cfm?story_id=15612447&source=hptextfeature|archive-date=30 December 2016}}</ref><ref name="BBC2010election">{{cite news|title=Tajikistan elections criticised by poll watchdog|newspaper=[[BBC]]|date=1 March 2010|access-date=5 March 2010|url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/asia-pacific/8543214.stm|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100304194250/http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/asia-pacific/8543214.stm|archive-date=4 March 2010}}</ref>

The Tajik government has reportedly clamped down on facial hair as part of a crackdown on Islamic influence and due to its perceived associations with [[Islamic extremism]], which is evident in bordering [[Afghanistan]].<ref>{{cite AV media|title=Dictatorland: Tajikistan|date=20 April 2017|medium=Television production|work=BBC Three}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/sep/07/tajikistans-beard-ban-facial-hair-emomali-rahmon|title=The men evading Tajikistan's de-facto beard ban|website=The Guardian|date=7 September 2015|access-date=22 March 2021|archive-date=25 February 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225034114/https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/sep/07/tajikistans-beard-ban-facial-hair-emomali-rahmon|url-status=live}}</ref>

The presidential election held on 6 November 2006 was boycotted by "mainline" opposition parties, including the 23,000-member [[Islamic Renaissance Party of Tajikistan|Islamic Renaissance Party]]. Four remaining opponents "all but endorsed the incumbent", Rahmon.<ref name=greenberg />

Freedom of the press is officially guaranteed by the government, and independent press outlets remain restricted, as does an amount of web content.<ref>{{Cite web|last=Welle (www.dw.com)|first=Deutsche|title=Press freedom in Tajikistan: Going from bad to worse {{!}} DW {{!}} 5 June 2020|url=https://www.dw.com/en/press-freedom-in-tajikistan-going-from-bad-to-worse/a-53697778|access-date=5 January 2021|website=DW.COM|date=5 June 2020 |language=en-GB|archive-date=15 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200615162514/https://www.dw.com/en/press-freedom-in-tajikistan-from-bad-to-worse/a-53697778|url-status=live}}</ref> According to the [[Institute for War & Peace Reporting]], access to local and foreign websites is blocked, and journalists are sometimes obstructed from reporting on some events. In practice, no public criticism of the regime is tolerated and all direct protest is suppressed and denied coverage in the local media.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://iwpr.net/report-news/tajik-government%E2%80%99s-fury-over-conflict-reporting|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140926061259/http://iwpr.net/report-news/tajik-government%E2%80%99s-fury-over-conflict-reporting|url-status=dead|archive-date=26 September 2014|title=Tajik Government's Fury Over Conflict Reporting|publisher=Iwpr.net|date=22 October 2010|access-date=14 January 2011}}</ref>

In the 2020 ''[[Democracy Index]]'' by the [[Economist Intelligence Unit]], Tajikistan was ranked 160th, after [[Saudi Arabia]], while receiving the designation of "authoritarian regime".<ref>{{Cite news|title=Global democracy has another bad year|newspaper=The Economist|url=https://www.economist.com/graphic-detail/2020/01/22/global-democracy-has-another-bad-year|access-date=27 July 2020|issn=0013-0613|archive-date=23 March 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210323135217/https://www.economist.com/graphic-detail/2020/01/22/global-democracy-has-another-bad-year|url-status=live}}</ref>

In July 2019, UN ambassadors of 37 countries, including Tajikistan, signed a joint letter to [[UNHRC]] defending China's [[Xinjiang re-education camps|treatment of Uyghurs]] in the [[Xinjiang]] region.<ref>{{cite news |title=Which Countries Are For or Against China's Xinjiang Policies? |url=https://thediplomat.com/2019/07/which-countries-are-for-or-against-chinas-xinjiang-policies/ |work=[[The Diplomat (magazine)|The Diplomat]] |date=15 July 2019 |access-date=18 July 2019 |archive-date=16 July 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190716160658/https://thediplomat.com/2019/07/which-countries-are-for-or-against-chinas-xinjiang-policies/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

In October 2020, President Emomali Rahmon was [[2020 Tajik presidential election|re-elected]] for another seven-year term with 90% of the vote, following a largely ceremonial election.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2020/10/12/tajikistan-leader-rakhmon-wins-election-with-over-90-percent|title=Tajikistan re-elects leader Rahmon with overwhelming majority|website=www.aljazeera.com|access-date=3 May 2021|archive-date=20 November 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201120060537/https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2020/10/12/tajikistan-leader-rakhmon-wins-election-with-over-90-percent/|url-status=live}}</ref>

In April 2021, a [[Water conflict|conflict over water]] with [[Kyrgyzstan]] escalated into one of the [[2021 Kyrgyzstan–Tajikistan conflict|border clashes]] between the two countries since independence.<ref>{{Cite news|date=29 April 2021|title=Kyrgyz, Tajik security forces clash at border in water dispute|work=[[Reuters]]|url=https://www.reuters.com/article/kyrgyzstan-tajikistan-border-clashes-idUSL8N2MM5JM|access-date=19 July 2021|archive-date=2 May 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210502053222/https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/kyrgyz-tajik-security-forces-clash-border-water-dispute-2021-04-29/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite news |title=After Kyrgyzstan-Tajikistan Border Conflict, Time For a Human Rights Agenda |url=https://www.hrw.org/news/2021/05/21/after-kyrgyzstan-tajikistan-border-conflict-time-human-rights-agenda |work=Human Rights Watch |date=21 May 2021 |access-date=19 July 2021 |archive-date=21 May 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210521140543/https://www.hrw.org/news/2021/05/21/after-kyrgyzstan-tajikistan-border-conflict-time-human-rights-agenda |url-status=live }}</ref>

In July 2021, Tajikistan appealed to members of a Russian-led [[Collective Security Treaty Organization]] (CSTO) of ex-Soviet states for help in dealing with security challenges emerging from neighbouring [[Afghanistan]].<ref>{{cite news |title=Tajikistan asks Russia-led bloc for help on Afghan border |url=https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/russia-says-afghan-situation-can-swiftly-worsen-pledges-help-if-needed-2021-07-07/ |work=Reuters |date=7 July 2021 |access-date=4 August 2021 |archive-date=4 August 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210804184603/https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/russia-says-afghan-situation-can-swiftly-worsen-pledges-help-if-needed-2021-07-07/ |url-status=live }}</ref> The safety concerns emerged as foreign troops such as the US and British army exited the country, causing over 1,000 Afghan civilians and servicemen to flee to neighbouring Tajikistan after [[Taliban]] insurgents [[2021 Taliban offensive|took control of parts]] of Afghanistan.<ref>{{Cite news|title=Tajikistan Reportedly Calls On Allies For Help With Security Challenges From Afghanistan|url=https://www.rferl.org/a/tajikistan-csto-help-afghanistan/31346198.html|access-date=8 July 2021|newspaper=Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty|date=7 July 2021 |language=en|archive-date=22 November 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211122165303/https://www.rferl.org/a/tajikistan-csto-help-afghanistan/31346198.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Administrative divisions ===
{{Main|Regions of Tajikistan|Districts of Tajikistan}}

{{stack|{{Tajikistan Provinces Image Map}}}}

Tajikistan consists of four administrative divisions at the first level. These are the provinces ([[viloyat]]) of [[Sughd]] and [[Khatlon]], the autonomous province of [[Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Province|Gorno-Badakhshan]] (abbreviated as GBAO), and the [[Regions of Republican Subordination]] (RRP – Raiony Respublikanskogo Podchineniya in [[Romanization of Russian|transliteration]] from Russian; {{langx|tg|Ноҳияҳои тобеи ҷумҳурӣ|Nohiyahoi tobei jumhurii}}). The capital city, [[Dushanbe]], is administered separately as a city of republican subordination.

Each region is divided into [[Districts of Tajikistan|districts]] ({{langx|tg|Ноҳия|nohiya}} or ''raion''). {{As of|2024}}, there are 47 districts in Tajikistan (excluding the 4 city districts within Dushanbe). The districts are further subdivided into ''[[Jamoats of Tajikistan|jamoats]]'' (village-level self-governing units), which are then divided into villages (''[[Qyshloqs of Tajikistan|qyshloqs]]''). {{As of|2024}}, there are approximately 406 jamoats.

== Economy ==
{{Main|Economy of Tajikistan}}
{{See also|Agriculture in Tajikistan}}

[[File:Young Tajikistani dry fruit seller.jpg|thumb|right|A Tajik dry fruit seller]]

Tajikistan's economy has experienced significant growth in recent years, with real GDP expanding by 8.4% in 2024 and maintaining a growth rate of 8.4% in 2025, reaching a nominal GDP of 176.9 billion somoni. Growth in the first half of 2025 averaged 8.1%. The World Bank projects growth to moderate to 6.2% in 2026 and 4.7% in 2027, while the IMF forecasts 6.0% for 2026. The government's own target for 2026 remains 8.5%.<ref name="wb-tajikistan-2025">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Economic Update, Spring 2025 |url=https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/tajikistan/publication/tajikistan-economic-update-spring-2025 |publisher=World Bank |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref><ref name="imf-weo-2025">{{cite web |title=World Economic Outlook Database, April 2025 – Tajikistan |url=https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WEO/weo-database/2025/April |publisher=International Monetary Fund |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="asiaplus-gdp-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan's GDP grows 8.4% in 2024 |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250225/tajikistans-gdp-grows-84-in-2024 |work=Asia-Plus |date=25 February 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

In 2024, Tajikistan remained one of the world's most [[remittance]]-dependent economies, with remittances accounting for approximately 49% of GDP, up from 39% in 2023. The World Bank estimated remittance inflows at $5.8 billion in 2024, representing a 27% increase. In the first quarter of 2025, remittances surged by 62.9% in dollar terms compared to the same period in 2024, driven primarily by higher wages for migrant workers in Russia. However, the World Bank has warned that a downturn in the Russian economy could lead to returning migrants facing limited economic prospects at home.<ref name="wb-remittances-2024">{{cite web |title=Remittance Flows to Central Asia, 2024 Update |url=https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/migrationremittancesdiasporaissues/brief/migration-and-remittances-data |publisher=World Bank |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="asiaplus-remittances-2025">{{cite news |title=Remittances to Tajikistan surge by 62.9% in Q1 2025 |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250618/remittances-to-tajikistan-surge-by-629-in-q1-2025 |work=Asia-Plus |date=18 June 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

Inflation has declined to historic lows, averaging 3.6% in 2024 and 3.5% in 2025—the lowest since independence. Food prices rose by 4.5%, services by 5.7%, and non-food goods by 1.5% in 2025. The National Bank of Tajikistan targets an inflation range of 3–7%, while the World Bank projects inflation at 4.5% for 2026.<ref name="asiaplus-inflation-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan records lowest inflation since independence |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250714/tajikistan-records-lowest-inflation-since-independence |work=Asia-Plus |date=14 July 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

Poverty has declined substantially, with the national poverty rate falling from 56% in 2010 to approximately 20% in 2024. The middle class grew from 8% to 33% over the same period. Under the lower-middle-income country poverty line ($4.20 per day in 2017 PPP), 16.9% of the population lived in poverty in 2024. Despite these gains, the World Bank notes that poverty reduction has been driven primarily by wage growth and remittances rather than domestic job creation. Labour force participation remains low at only 40%—the lowest in Central Asia—with female participation at just 21%.<ref name="wb-poverty-2025">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Poverty Assessment, 2025 |url=https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/tajikistan/publication/tajikistan-poverty-assessment-2025 |publisher=World Bank |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

[[File:Talco.jpg|thumb|The [[TadAZ]] aluminium smelting plant, in Tursunzoda, is Tajikistan's chief industrial asset and one of the largest aluminium plants in Central Asia.]]

The primary sources of income in Tajikistan remain aluminium production, cotton growing, and remittances from migrant workers. The state-owned [[Tajik Aluminum Company]] (TadAZ) produced 83,000 tonnes of aluminium in 2024, representing a 24% increase, with a target of 180,000 tonnes for 2025. Aluminium exports reached 71,110 tonnes in 2024, a 36.5% increase, though the sector accounts for only 1.33% of industrial output.<ref name="asiaplus-aluminium-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan's aluminium production up 24% in 2024 |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250120/tajikistans-aluminium-production-up-24-in-2024 |work=Asia-Plus |date=20 January 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

Cotton production, however, has declined sharply from 404,700 tonnes in 2022 to 253,200 tonnes in 2024. Fibre exports totalled 100,500 tonnes worth $170.1 million in 2024, with Iran receiving 67.9%, Turkey 15%, and China 8.4%. Cotton no longer accounts for 60% of agricultural output as previously reported, reflecting a significant restructuring of the agricultural sector.<ref name="asiaplus-cotton-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan's cotton production continues to decline |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250210/tajikistans-cotton-production-continues-to-decline |work=Asia-Plus |date=10 February 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

Gold production grew by 18.8% in 2024, though exports of precious metals fell by 69.6% due to reduced gold shipments.<ref name="asiaplus-gold-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan's gold production up 18.8% in 2024 |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250315/tajikistans-gold-production-up-188-in-2024 |work=Asia-Plus |date=15 March 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

Tajikistan's hydropower potential remains a key focus, with the [[Rogun Dam]] project progressing. The World Bank approved $350 million in financing in late 2024, and the OPEC Fund provided a $25 million loan for construction. Once completed, the 335-metre dam will be the tallest in the world, with a capacity of 3,780 MW. The [[Nurek Dam]], currently the second highest dam in the world, remains operational. Other hydroelectric projects include the Sangtuda-1 and Sangtuda-2 stations. Russia's [[RAO UES]] completed Sangtuda-1 (670 MW capacity) which commenced operations in January 2008. Iran has been involved in Sangtuda-2, and Chinese company SinoHydro has worked on the Zerafshan project.<ref name="wb-rogun-2024">{{cite news |title=World Bank approves $350 million for Rogun Dam |url=https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2024/12/10/world-bank-approves-350-million-for-rogun-hydropower-project-in-tajikistan |publisher=World Bank |date=10 December 2024 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref><ref name="asiaplus-rogun-2025">{{cite news |title=Rogun HPP: physical progress and funding update |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250701/rogun-hpp-physical-progress-and-funding-update |work=Asia-Plus |date=1 July 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

The planned [[CASA-1000]] project aims to transmit 1,000 MW of surplus electricity from Tajikistan to Pakistan via Afghanistan. The 750&nbsp;km transmission line is being developed as a public-private partnership with support from the World Bank, IFC, ADB, and IDB, with an estimated cost of approximately US$865 million.<ref name="casa1000-2025">{{cite web |title=CASA-1000 Project Update |url=https://www.casa-1000.org/ |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Other energy resources include coal deposits and smaller reserves of natural gas and petroleum.<ref name="iea-tajikistan">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan – Energy Profile |url=https://www.iea.org/countries/tajikistan |publisher=International Energy Agency |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

The unemployment rate remains a subject of debate, with official registered unemployment at approximately 50,900 people (June 2025), while international estimates from the World Bank place the rate at approximately 11.6% (2024). This discrepancy reflects the large informal economy and underemployment not captured in official figures.<ref name="worldbank-unemployment">{{cite web |title=World Bank Open Data - Unemployment, total (% of total labor force) - Tajikistan |url=https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SL.UEM.TOTL.ZS?locations=TJ |website=World Bank |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

Drug trafficking remains an illegal source of income, with Tajikistan serving as a transit country for Afghan narcotics bound for Russian and European markets. The UNODC continues to work with Tajik authorities to strengthen border controls, provide training, and establish joint interdiction teams. The Tajikistani Drug Control Agency has been established with international support. However, according to some experts, drug money has contributed to corruption at various levels of government. Tajikistan ranks among the leading countries globally for heroin and raw opium confiscations, though recent figures have not been publicly updated from earlier records.<ref name="unodc-tajikistan-2025">{{cite web |title=UNODC Country Programme: Tajikistan |url=https://www.unodc.org/centralasia/en/countries/tajikistan.html |publisher=UNODC |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

China has become a significant economic partner under the [[Belt and Road Initiative]], with total Chinese investment in Tajikistan reaching approximately $4.1 billion between 2007 and 2023. Over 700 Chinese companies operate in the country, spanning infrastructure, mining, energy, and manufacturing sectors.<ref name="vakulchuk-overland-2019">Vakulchuk, Roman and Indra Overland (2019) "China's Belt and Road Initiative through the Lens of Central Asia", in Fanny M. Cheung and Ying-yi Hong (eds) ''Regional Connection under the Belt and Road Initiative. The Prospects for Economic and Financial Cooperation''. London: Routledge, pp. 115–133.</ref><ref name="asiaplus-china-2025">{{cite news |title=China's economic presence in Tajikistan |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250320/chinas-economic-presence-in-tajikistan |work=Asia-Plus |date=20 March 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

The World Bank's 2025 assessment notes that while Tajikistan has made significant progress in economic growth and poverty reduction, the economy remains vulnerable to external shocks, particularly fluctuations in remittance flows from Russia and changes in global commodity prices. Diversification away from reliance on remittances, aluminium, and cotton remains a key policy challenge.<ref name="wb-tajikistan-2025" />

=== Telecommunications ===
{{Main|Telecommunications in Tajikistan}}

Tajikistan's communications market grew 13.7% in 2025 to 4.9 billion somoni.<ref name="TrendTelecom">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan's telecom sector growth |url=https://www.trend.az/business/4137956.html |website=Trend |date=5 January 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

Average mobile internet speed increased from 7.2 Mbps at the beginning of 2025 to 35.4 Mbps in 2026. Fixed internet speed increased from 30 Mbps to over 40 Mbps in 2025, with plans to reach 100 Mbps within two years. Average internet consumption per user has increased tenfold over the past five years, from 3 to 30 GB. Fixed internet prices have decreased by an average of 55%.

Tajikistan is in the early stages of 5G deployment. A memorandum on the establishment of a national 5G consortium was signed between the Communications Service and the country's mobile operators—Indigo Tajikistan, TT Mobile, Babilon M, Tacom, and Tojiktelecom—to coordinate the rollout of next-generation mobile technologies.<ref name="5GConsortium">{{cite web |title=5G consortium established in Tajikistan |url=https://asiaplustj.info/ |work=Asia-Plus |date=15 July 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Currently, 84 5G base stations are active.

In October 2025, a licensing agreement was signed with Starlink Tajikistan (SpaceX), enabling the launch of Starlink operations in the country.<ref name="StarlinkTajikistan">{{cite web |title=Starlink licensing agreement signed in Tajikistan |url=https://asiaplustj.info/ |work=Asia-Plus |date=15 October 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

=== Transportation ===
{{Main|Transport in Tajikistan}}

As a [[landlocked country]], Tajikistan has no ports, and the majority of transportation is via road, air, and rail. In 2025, the country had an ambitious agenda to modernize its transport sector, focusing on infrastructure upgrades and stronger international connections.<ref name="trend-transport-2025">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan's transport sector overview in 2025 |url=https://www.trend.az/business/4137956.html |website=Trend |date=5 January 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="dhapress-transport-2025">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan's transport sector overview in 2025 |url=https://www.dhapress.com/en/news/world/38360 |website=DHAPress |date=5 January 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> As of 2026, the country is implementing 11 investment projects in the road and transport sector with a total value of USD 1.193 billion.<ref name="khovar-11projects-2026">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Implements 11 Transport Infrastructure Projects Worth Over $1.1 Billion |url=https://eng.khovar.tj/2026/07/tajikistan-implements-11-transport-infrastructure-projects-worth-over-1-1-billion/ |publisher=NIAT Khovar |date=13 July 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Under the State Budget for 2025, nearly 3 billion somoni ($274.35 million) was allocated to the transport and communications sector.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" /><ref name="dhapress-transport-2025" />

The government has pursued agreements with Iran, Afghanistan, and Pakistan to gain access to ports and establish regional transit corridors. In July 2026, Iran, Afghanistan, and Tajikistan signed a trilateral transit corridor agreement to activate a new road transit route.<ref name="trilateral-transit-2026">{{cite news |title=Taliban, Iran and Tajikistan Sign Transit Corridor Deal |url=https://en.didpress.com/34353/ |work=DID Press Agency |date=16 July 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="tehrantimes-transit-2026">{{cite news |title=Iran, Tajikistan emphasize transport, transit, logistics co-op expansion |url=https://www.tehrantimes.com/news/514853/Iran-Tajikistan-emphasize-transport-transit-logistics-co-op-expansion |work=Tehran Times |date=14 July 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref> Afghanistan, Iran, and Turkey agreed to jointly construct a railway between Herat and Mazar-i-Sharif in 2025.<ref name="herat-mazar-rail-2025">{{cite news |title=Afghanistan, Iran, Turkey sign railway agreement |url=https://grc.net/ |work=GRC |date=October 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Tajikistan has also been exploring participation in the [[China–Kyrgyzstan–Uzbekistan railway]] project.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" /><ref name="dhapress-transport-2025" />

In 2025, a total of 154.8 million tons of cargo were transported by all modes of transport, which is 15.2 percent more compared to 2024.<ref name="stat-freight-2025">{{cite web |title=Freight Transportation Volume in Tajikistan Increased by 15.2 Percent |url=https://www.stat.tj/news/freight-transportation-volume-in-tajikistan-increased-by-15-2-percent/ |publisher=Agency on Statistics under the President of the Republic of Tajikistan |date=17 February 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> In 2025, road transport carried 147.3 million tons of cargo and 1,129.5 million passengers.<ref name="asiaplus-2025-statistics">{{cite news |title=Over 2.9 mln passengers served at Tajikistan's airports in 2025 |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20260203/over-29-mln-passengers-served-at-tajikistans-airports-in-2025 |work=Asia-Plus |date=3 February 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

==== Road ====
{{Main|Roads in Tajikistan}}

[[File:Tajikistan International Roads numbered map.png|thumb|Map of Highways of International importance in Tajikistan]]
[[Road transport]] continues to dominate Tajikistan's transport system, accounting for more than 90% of passenger transportation and over 80% of domestic freight.<ref name="tajikistanmission.ch">{{cite web |url=http://www.tajikistanmission.ch/business-and-investment/infrastructure.html |title=Tajikistan Mission – Infrastructure |work=tajikistanmission.ch |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140531142944/http://www.tajikistanmission.ch/business-and-investment/infrastructure.html |archive-date=31 May 2014}}</ref> As of 2025, the total length of roads in the country is approximately 27,800 kilometres.<ref name="tajikistanmission.ch" /> In 2025, through state investment projects, 236 kilometers of roads and other transport infrastructure facilities were built and put into operation, with an investment of 5.4 billion somoni (about $580 million). Currently, there are 16 transport sector projects under construction nationwide, with a total investment of about 11.3 billion somoni (about $1.22 billion).<ref name="mofcom-2026-roads">{{cite web |title=2026年塔吉克斯坦将修复和建设公路300公里 |url=http://www.mofcom.gov.cn/tjsj/gbdqmytj/oygjdqmytj/art/2026/art_d300b14285ca45c190a0a3075f7b05e7.html |publisher=Ministry of Commerce of China |date=16 January 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

Major road projects completed or underway include:

The construction of the [[Rogun Dam|Rogun reservoir]] affected the Obigarm–Nurobod section of the M41 highway. The 75-kilometer Rogun–Obigarm–Nurobod International Highway was opened in August 2025, bypassing the Rogun reservoir. The project includes three tunnels—"35th Anniversary of Independence" (2,605 m), "Vahdat" (1,700 m), and "Oriyono" (1,600 m)—which shorten the route by 24&nbsp;km, as well as 14 bridges spanning approximately 1,150 meters.<ref name="akn-rogun-highway-2025">{{cite web |title=Commissioning of the Rogun-Obigarm-Nurobod International Highway and its three tunnels |url=https://akn.tj/en/2025/08/26/commissioning-of-the-rogun-obigarm-nurobod-international-highway-and-its-three-tunnels/ |publisher=AKN |date=26 August 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="khovar-rogun-highway-2025">{{cite web |title=Глава государства Эмомали Рахмон дал старт работе дороги Рогун–Оби Гарм–Нуробод |url=https://khovar.tj/rus/2025/08/glava-gosudarstva-emomali-rahmon-dal-start-rabote-dorogi-rogun-obi-garm-nurobod/ |publisher=NIAT Khovar |date=26 August 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="trend-rogun-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan inaugurates ADB-supported Obigarm-Nurobod road |url=https://www.trend.az/business/4149480.html |work=Trend |date=26 August 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> This route connects the capital with the administrative centers of the Nurobod, Rasht, Sangvor, Tojikobod and Lakhsh districts, expanding communication in all directions, including trade turnover between Tajikistan and [[Kyrgyzstan]] and regional transit.<ref name="akn-rogun-highway-2025" /> In June 2025, the [[Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank]] approved a $2.04 million grant—its first under a Project-Specific Window—to support restoration of the 75-kilometer stretch.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" />

The launch of the Dangara–Guliston Green Corridor project (49&nbsp;km) began in March 2025, upgrading the road from two to four lanes and adding bike lanes, sidewalks, lighting, crossings, and electric vehicle charging stations. Financing comes from a mixture of international sources, including a [[European Bank for Reconstruction and Development]] (EBRD) loan of up to €38 million and an [[Asian Development Bank]] (ADB) grant of up to $86.7 million, with completion expected by 2030.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" /><ref name="dhapress-transport-2025" />

The Dushanbe–Chanak highway (358&nbsp;km), connecting the capital to the [[Uzbekistan]] border, continues to undergo maintenance and reconstruction. As of August 2025, approximately 347,000 tons of asphalt had been used in its repair since 2012. The route includes three tunnels—"Maikhura" (897 m), "Istiklol" (4,998 m), and "Shakhristan" (5,253 m)—as well as 38 bridges and 24 avalanche galleries.<ref name="avesta-chanak-asphalt-2025">{{cite news |title=About 347,000 tons of asphalt were used to repair the Dushanbe-Chanak road |url=https://en.avesta.tj/2025/08/12/about-347000-tons-of-asphalt-were-used-to-repair-the-dushanbe-chanak-road/ |work=Avesta |date=12 August 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="avesta-chanak-bridges-2025">{{cite news |title=38 bridges and tunnels are being renovated on the Dushanbe-Chanak highway |url=https://en.avesta.tj/2025/05/07/38-bridges-and-tunnels-are-being-renovated-on-the-dushanbe-chanak-highway/ |work=Avesta |date=7 May 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

The Qalaikhumb–Vanj–Rushan section of the Dushanbe–Kulob–Khorog–Kulma highway was completed in 2025, funded by a $230 million grant from the Chinese government.<ref name="asiaplus-qvr-2025">{{cite news |title=Rehabilitation of the Qalaikhumb-Vanj-Rushan border road to be completed by June |url=https://old.asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250130/rehabilitation-of-the-qalaikhumb-vanj-rushan-border-road-to-be-completed-by-june |work=Asia-Plus |date=30 January 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> President [[Emomali Rahmon]] inaugurated a section of the Kulob–Khorog–Kulma–China highway in 2025.<ref name="khovar-kulob-khorog-2025">{{cite web |title=President Emomali Rahmon inaugurated a section of Kulob-Khorug-Kulma-China highway |url=https://eng.khovar.tj/ |publisher=NIAT Khovar |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In February 2025, Tajikistan signed an agreement with China's Zhejiang Communications Construction Group to build [[Central Asia]]'s longest road bridge over the Surkhab River. The bridge will span 920 meters (1,500 meters including access roads) and is expected to be completed within four years at a cost of nearly $60 million.<ref name="trend-bridge-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan, China sign agreement to build longest bridge in Central Asia |url=https://en.trend.az/casia/tajikistan/4002392.html |work=Trend News Agency |date=26 February 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="khovar-bridge-2025">{{cite web |title=President Emomali Rahmon becomes acquainted with construction of road bridge project in Nurobod |url=https://eng.khovar.tj/2025/08/president-emomali-rahmon-becomes-acquainted-with-construction-of-road-bridge-project-in-nurobod/ |publisher=NIAT Khovar |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The bridge will be the largest road bridge in the republic in terms of design and style.<ref name="khovar-bridge-2025" />

Tajikistan and China have agreed to build 93&nbsp;km of roads and two tunnels (total length 5.2&nbsp;km) as part of a broader infrastructure partnership. The project includes five avalanche corridors totaling 602 meters, 15 bridges totaling 633 meters, 21,850 meters of protective walls, and 151 artificial structures.<ref name="avesta-china-roads-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan and China have agreed to build 93 km of roads and two tunnels |url=https://en.avesta.tj/2025/08/22/tajikistan-and-china-have-agreed-to-build-93-km-of-roads-and-two-tunnels/ |work=Avesta |date=22 August 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> In September 2025, the governments of Tajikistan and China signed an agreement on international road transportation, along with seven other supplementary instruments in Beijing, encompassing various domains of bilateral collaboration.<ref name="qazinform-china-road-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan, China sign deal on international road transportation |url=https://qazinform.com/news/tajikistan-china-sign-deal-on-international-road-transportation-3738b9 |work=Qazinform |date=3 September 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="asiaplus-china-road-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan and China sign Agreement on International road transport |url=https://old.asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250903/tajikistan-and-china-sign-agreement-on-international-road-transport |work=Asia-Plus |date=3 September 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

China has resumed construction of a strategic 109-kilometer section of the Dushanbe–Kulma Highway near the Afghan border after several months of suspension, following new security guarantees provided by the Tajik government. Construction resumed in April 2026 after Tajik authorities introduced enhanced security measures to protect Chinese engineers and workers. The project had been suspended in late 2025 following a series of violent attacks on Chinese nationals.<ref name="didpress-china-resume-2026">{{cite news |title=China Resumes Tajikistan Highway Project After New Security Guarantees |url=https://en.didpress.com/33339/ |work=DID Press Agency |date=1 July 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In December 2025, Tajikistan and Kuwaiti consulting company NARCO signed an agreement for the rehabilitation of the 162-kilometer Ayni–Kuhistoni Mastchoh road in Sughd province. The project includes road surface modernization, widening of the roadway, the construction of 27 bridges and 405 culverts, and the updating of engineering systems.<ref name="asiaplus-ayni-2026">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan's MoT and Kuwaiti consulting company sign contract for rehabilitation of Ayni – Kuhistoni Mastchoh road |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/node/356885 |work=Asia-Plus |date=14 January 2026 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In 2026, eight new road projects with a total length of nearly 286.9&nbsp;km and a combined value of USD 334.9 million are scheduled to begin, including the rehabilitation of the Guliston–Kulob road (32.2&nbsp;km, USD 80 million), Baljuvon–Sarikhosor road (58&nbsp;km, USD 58 million), the Murghab–Kulma section (67&nbsp;km, USD 78 million), construction of three bridges on the Labijar–Sangvor route (USD 17.1 million), construction of the Kulob bypass road (11&nbsp;km, USD 30 million), rehabilitation of the Vahdat–Obigarm road (70&nbsp;km, USD 28.8 million), construction of the Khistevarz–Konibodom (Arka) road (9.7&nbsp;km, USD 4.5 million), and the Khorugh–Roshtqala road (39&nbsp;km, USD 38.5 million).<ref name="khovar-11projects-2026" />

==== Rail ====
{{Main|Rail transport in Tajikistan}}

[[File:ТЭ33А-0175.jpg|thumb|Tajikistan's rail infrastructure.]]
The railroad system consists of approximately 680 kilometres of {{RailGauge|1520mm}} [[Russian gauge|broad gauge]], non-electrified single-track lines.<ref name=CIA>{{Cite web|title=Tajikistan – World Factbook|url=https://www.cia.gov/the-world-factbook/countries/tajikistan/|access-date=8 May 2021|website=www.cia.gov|archive-date=20 August 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210820040637/https://www.cia.gov/the-world-factbook/countries/tajikistan/|url-status=dead}}</ref> According to Tajikistan's Ministry of Transport, the total length exceeds 980 kilometres, including approach and technical tracks.<ref name="mofcom-rail-2025">{{cite web |title=塔吉克斯坦计划实现铁路电气化 |url=http://tj.mofcom.gov.cn/jmxw/art/2025/art_8bec32c74bf44d08b6cf667d0dc5904e.html |publisher=Ministry of Commerce of China |date=30 July 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="asiaplus-rail-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan plans to electrify the country's railways |url=https://old.asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250724/tajikistan-plans-to-electrify-the-countrys-railways |work=Asia-Plus |date=24 July 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> In the first half of 2025, rail accounted for approximately 70% of international freight traffic.<ref name="mofcom-rail-2025" /> In 2025, railway transport (including loading, dispatch, and transit shipments) carried 7.4 million tons of cargo, which is 10.9 percent more than in 2024.<ref name="stat-freight-2025" /> The network consists of three disconnected segments (northern, central, and southern), which require transit through Uzbekistan and [[Turkmenistan]] for interconnection.<ref name="mofcom-rail-2025" />

The principal segments are in the southern region, connecting the capital with the industrial areas of the Hisor and Vakhsh valleys, and with Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kazakhstan, and Russia.<ref>{{cite AV media |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BBSardpSH0E |title=Migrant Express Part 1: Good-bye Dushanbe |date=1 September 2009 |work=YouTube |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151016042543/https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BBSardpSH0E |archive-date=16 October 2015}}</ref> The [[Bokhtar]]–[[Kulab, Tajikistan|Kulob]] railway connects the Kulob District with the central area of the country.<ref name="tajikistanmission.ch" /> The Vahdat–Yavan railway (48.65&nbsp;km) is another important line, connecting the Districts of Republican Subordination with Khatlon region.<ref name="mofcom-rail-2025" />

Railway development in 2025 centered on electrification plans and regional integration. Tajik Railways began preliminary work on electrifying the 62-kilometer Vahdat–Pakhtaobod line, which links eastern Dushanbe with the Uzbek border. This section of the line serves a commuter train that runs daily between Dushanbe-1 station and Pakhtaobod, with 15 intermediate stops. The travel time is approximately one hour.<ref name="asiaplus-rail-2025" /><ref name="trend-transport-2025" /> Authorities also explored electrification of the Kanibadam–Bekabad route. Feasibility studies continued for a proposed 51-kilometer railway connecting Jaloliddini Balkhi with Nizhniy Pyandzh, discussed within cooperation frameworks involving South Korea.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" /> Tajikistan has also been examining possible participation in the China–Kyrgyzstan–Uzbekistan railway project, which is expected to span more than 530 kilometers and include 20 stations.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" />

Iran and Tajikistan have commenced a new chapter aimed at enhancing railway cooperation through the signing of a memorandum of understanding (MoU), seeking to strengthen and expand railway trade.<ref name="irandaily-rail-2025">{{cite news |title=Tehran-Dushanbe railway transit breaks Tajikistan's landlocked status |url=https://newspaper.irandaily.ir/ |work=Iran Daily |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> In July 2025, Afghanistan, Uzbekistan, and Pakistan signed an agreement to begin a feasibility study for the "Afghan-Trans" railway project.<ref name="afghan-trans-2025">{{cite web |title=Afghan-Trans railway feasibility study agreement |url=https://csrsaf.org/ |publisher=CSRSAF |date=16 July 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

==== Electric vehicles and green mobility ====
Tajikistan has seen a dramatic increase in electric vehicle (EV) adoption. As of June 2025, the number of registered EVs exceeded 34,000 units, representing more than 5% of the country's total vehicle fleet. This shift has contributed to a reduction of 18,500 tons in liquefied gas imports in the first half of 2025.<ref name="asiaplus-ev-2025">{{cite news |title=Number of electric vehicles in Tajikistan exceeds 34,000 |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250619/number-of-electric-vehicles-in-tajikistan-exceeds-34000 |work=Asia-Plus |date=19 June 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref> Since April 2025, Tajikistan has exempted new EVs (less than one year old) from import customs duties.<ref name="asiaplus-ev-2025" />

==== Urban transport ====
Urban transport modernization gained momentum in 2025, particularly in the capital Dushanbe. Tajikistan announced plans to begin work on a light metro system in Dushanbe, with a feasibility study scheduled for completion in 2025 and construction planned between 2028 and 2033. The first underground line will span 10.5 kilometres, linking the southern gate of the capital (Darvozai) with central areas.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" /><ref name="asiaplus-metro-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajik transport minister orders urgent measures to tackle traffic congestion in Dushanbe |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/society/20251021/tajik-transport-minister-orders-urgent-measures-to-tackle-traffic-congestion-in-dushanbe |work=Asia-Plus |date=21 October 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

The EBRD is supporting the reconstruction of Dushanbe's road infrastructure through a newly established Road Reconstruction Project Implementation Group under the Ministry of Transport.<ref name="avesta-ebrd-dushanbe-2025">{{cite news |title=The EBRD will support the reconstruction of Dushanbe's road infrastructure |url=https://en.avesta.tj/2025/08/30/the-ebrd-will-support-the-reconstruction-of-dushanbes-road-infrastructure/ |work=Avesta |date=30 August 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Tajikistan and China have also agreed to step up work on the construction of two-level road interchanges in Dushanbe.<ref name="asiaplus-interchanges-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan and China to strengthen work on two-level overpasses in Dushanbe |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20250823/tajikistan-and-china-to-strengthen-work-on-two-level-overpasses-in-dushanbe |work=Asia-Plus |date=23 August 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

In the Sughd region, a new 12.7-kilometre section of the Dehmoy–Konibodom road is planned at a cost of over $8 million, with 11 road bridges and 17 bus stops already constructed along the route.<ref name="asiaplus-dehmoy-2025">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan's president opens repaired road sections and new flight maintenance complex at KIA |url=https://asiaplustj.info/en/news/tajikistan/economic/20251121/tajikistans-president-opens-repaired-road-sections-and-new-flight-maintenance-complex-at-kia |work=Asia-Plus |date=21 November 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

==== Regional and international cooperation ====
Tajikistan has been actively pursuing regional transport integration. In July 2026, Iran, Afghanistan, and Tajikistan signed a trilateral transit corridor agreement in Mashhad, establishing a joint road transit corridor aimed at boosting regional trade. Under the agreement, a new transit route connecting the three countries will be activated, with the first trial cargo shipment expected within one month. The agreement also includes the formation of a joint working group tasked with addressing administrative obstacles, facilitating visas for drivers, reducing border delays and developing shared insurance mechanisms.<ref name="trilateral-transit-2026" /><ref name="tehrantimes-transit-2026" />

China has become a significant economic partner under the [[Belt and Road Initiative]], with total Chinese investment in Tajikistan reaching approximately $4.1 billion between 2007 and 2023. Over 700 Chinese companies operate in the country, spanning infrastructure, mining, energy, and manufacturing sectors.<ref name="vakulchuk-overland-2019"/> Following the signing of a friendship treaty between China and Tajikistan in May, approximately $8 billion in economic and infrastructure agreements were signed, expanding cooperation in transport, infrastructure and security.<ref name="didpress-china-resume-2026" />

Tajikistan and Kazakhstan are also expanding transport cooperation, with road freight transportation between the two countries increasing during the first four months of 2026, with total cargo volumes rising 5.8% and the number of vehicles increasing 17.9% compared with the same period last year.<ref name="khovar-kazakhstan-2026">{{cite web |title=Road Transport Between Tajikistan and Kazakhstan Rises 5.8% in Early 2026 |url=https://eng.khovar.tj/ |publisher=NIAT Khovar |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

==== Challenges and vulnerabilities ====
Tajikistan's transport sector faces several significant challenges. According to the Asian Transport Observatory, the country faces estimated average annual losses of $14.5 million to transport infrastructure, with 91% of these losses concentrated in the road sector.<ref name="green-roads-2025" /> Greenhouse gas emissions from road transport are growing at an annual rate of 17.9%.<ref name="green-roads-2025" />

Security concerns have impacted infrastructure projects, as demonstrated by the suspension of the Dushanbe–Kulma Highway project following attacks on Chinese nationals.<ref name="didpress-china-resume-2026" /> Out of 225 registered transport companies, only 107 have their own material and technical base, with the rest operating through leasing arrangements, affecting service quality.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" />

Traffic congestion in Dushanbe remains a major concern, with the Minister of Transport ordering urgent measures in October 2025, including the completion of a comprehensive road infrastructure inventory, the development of a digital road map, the use of digital technology and artificial intelligence, and the transition to cashless payment systems across all transport services by the end of 2025.<ref name="asiaplus-metro-2025" /> Proposed solutions include restrictions on personal vehicle use, congestion charges on central streets, installation of smart traffic lights, development of paid parking, construction of multi-storey parking facilities, stricter regulations on taxis, and dedicated bus lanes.<ref name="asiaplus-metro-2025" />

The transport sector's budget allocation has been reduced by 24% in the 2025–2027 program, potentially limiting the pace of infrastructure development.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" /> Additionally, the Istiklol Tunnel project faced suspension due to a $2.16 million funding shortfall from Iran.<ref name="trend-transport-2025" />

Despite these challenges, Tajikistan continues to pursue an ambitious transport modernization agenda, with multiple international partners supporting road rehabilitation, railway electrification, airport upgrades, and the development of sustainable urban transport solutions.

=== Languages ===
{{main|Languages of Tajikistan|Tajik language|Russian dialects#Tajik Russian|l2 = Tajik Russian|}}

The official [[language]]s of Tajikistan are [[Tajik language|Tajik]] as the state language and [[Russian language|Russian]] as the interethnic language, as understood in Article 2 of the Constitution: "The state language of Tajikistan shall be Tajik. Russian shall be the language of international communication."<ref name="auto1">{{cite web |url=http://www.unesco.org/education/edurights/media/docs/ae8c0f7576f3d9f63ff2055592a9cb6b7f95227a.pdf |title=Constitution of Tajikistan |publisher=UNESCO |access-date=8 April 2021 |archive-date=10 May 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210510033938/http://www.unesco.org/education/edurights/media/docs/ae8c0f7576f3d9f63ff2055592a9cb6b7f95227a.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref>

The state (national) language ({{langx|tg|забони давлатӣ|zaboni davlatí|links=no|engvar=gb}}, {{langx|ru|государственный язык|gosudarstvennyy yazyk|engvar=gb}}) of the Republic of Tajikistan is [[Tajik language|Tajik]], which is written in the [[Tajik alphabet|Tajik Cyrillic alphabet]]. Millions of native Tajik speakers live in neighbouring [[Uzbekistan]] and in Russia.<ref>{{cite web |title=Tajik language |url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tajik-language |website=Britannica |access-date=5 February 2021 |archive-date=27 February 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210227074700/https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tajik-language |url-status=live }}</ref>

According to article 2 of the Constitution of the Republic of Tajikistan,<ref name="auto1"/> Russian is recognised as the second official language of Tajikistan; the official language of inter-ethnic communication ({{langx|ru|язык межнационального общения}}; {{langx|tg|забони муоширати байни миллатҳо}}) in the country.<ref name="RIA-RU">{{cite web|url=https://ria.ru/culture/20091022/190107839.html|title=The status of the Russian language in Tajikistan remains unchanged – Rahmon|publisher=RIA – RIA.ru|date=22 October 2009|access-date=30 September 2016|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161002141721/https://ria.ru/culture/20091022/190107839.html|archive-date=2 October 2016}}</ref><ref name="lenta.ru/news/2011/06/09">{{cite web|url=http://lenta.ru/news/2011/06/09/russian|title=В Таджикистане русскому языку вернули прежний статус|publisher=Lenta.ru|access-date=13 September 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130905014016/http://lenta.ru/news/2011/06/09/russian/|archive-date=5 September 2013}}</ref>

Approximately 90% of the population of Tajikistan speaks Russian at varying levels.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://celcar.indiana.edu/materials/language-portal/tajiki/index.html | title=Tajiki | access-date=25 October 2024 | archive-date=12 June 2024 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240612222820/https://celcar.indiana.edu/materials/language-portal/tajiki/index.html | url-status=dead }}</ref> The varieties of Russian spoken in Tajikistan are referred to by scholars as ''[[Russian dialects#Tajikistani Russian|Tajik(istani) Russian]]''<ref>{{cite web |title=Russian: A Monocentric or Pluricentric Language |url=https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=739986 |publisher=Colloquia Humanistica |access-date=28 February 2021 |archive-date=17 April 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210417154701/https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=739986 |url-status=live }}</ref> and it shares some similarities with ''Uzbek(istani) Russian'', such as morphological differences and lexical differences such as the use of word ''урюк''<ref>{{cite web |title=Урюк |url=http://forum.lingvolive.com/thread/l132954/?order=all#comment-96f1bb5a-2004-46a4-85a3-a7e80086b86b |website=Lingvolive |publisher=ABBYY Lingvo |access-date=28 February 2021 }}{{Dead link|date=October 2022 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref> for a wild apricot or ''кислушка'' for rhubarb.<ref>{{cite web |title=Кислушка (ревень) |url=http://forum.lingvolive.com/thread/l131195/?order=all#comment-2c008487-0fcb-480f-9742-a7e80086723c |website=Lingolive |publisher=ABBYY Lingvo |access-date=28 February 2021 }}{{Dead link|date=March 2022 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref>

Both Russian and Tajik speakers in the country use the following words in common in addressing unfamiliar people and acquaintances.<ref>{{cite web |last1=Карина |first1=Рахим-заде |title=Взаимопроникновение русского и таджикского языков в разговорной речи населения Душанбе |url=https://studentlib.com/chitat/kursovaya_rabota_teoriya-32870-vzaimoproniknovenie_russkogo_i_tadzhikskogo_yazykov_v_razgovornoy_rechi_naseleniya_dushanbe.html |website=Studentlib.com |publisher=ГОСУДАРСТВЕННЫЙ ИНСТИТУТ РУССКОГО ЯЗЫКА имени А.С.ПУШКИНА |access-date=28 November 2021 |archive-date=28 November 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211128110606/https://studentlib.com/chitat/kursovaya_rabota_teoriya-32870-vzaimoproniknovenie_russkogo_i_tadzhikskogo_yazykov_v_razgovornoy_rechi_naseleniya_dushanbe.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

The "highly educated" part of the population of Tajikistan, and the [[intelligentsia]], prefer to speak Russian and Persian, the pronunciation of which in Tajikistan is called the "Iranian style".<ref name="RFE/RL">{{cite web |url=https://www.rferl.org/a/Tajikistan_Drops_Russian_As_Official_Language/1846118.html |title=Tajikistan Drops Russian As Official Language |publisher=RFE/RL – Rferl.org |date=7 October 2009 |access-date=13 September 2013 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304055202/http://www.rferl.org/content/Tajikistan_Drops_Russian_As_Official_Language/1846118.html |archive-date=4 March 2016 }}</ref><ref name="RIA-RU"/><ref name="lenta.ru/news/2011/06/09"/>

Native Uzbek speakers live in the north and west of Tajikistan. In fourth place (after Tajik, Russian and Uzbek) by number of native speakers are [[Pamir languages]], whose native speakers live in [[Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Region|Kuhistani Badakshshan Autonomous Region]]. The majority of [[Zoroastrians]] in Tajikistan speak one of the Pamir languages. Native speakers of the [[Kyrgyz language]] live in the north of Kuhistani Badakshshan Autonomous Region. [[Yagnobi language]] speakers live in the west of the country. The [[Parya language]] of local [[Romani people]] ([[:ru:Среднеазиатские цыгане|Central Asian Gypsies]]) is spoken in Tajikistan. Tajikistan has communities of native speakers of [[Persian language|Persian]], [[Arabic]], [[Pashto]], [[Armenian language|Armenian]], [[Azerbaijani language|Azerbaijani]], [[Tatar language|Tatar]], [[Turkmen language|Turkmen]], [[Kazakh language|Kazakh]], [[Chinese language|Chinese]], and [[Ukrainian language|Ukrainian]].<ref>{{cite web |last1=Sen Nag |first1=Oishimaya |title=What Languages Are Spoken In Tajikistan |url=https://www.worldatlas.com/articles/what-languages-are-spoken-in-tajikistan.html |website=World Atlas |date=August 2017 |access-date=5 February 2021 |archive-date=25 February 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225025005/https://www.worldatlas.com/articles/what-languages-are-spoken-in-tajikistan.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

Tajikistan is a [[secular state]] with a constitution providing for freedom of religion, but nevertheless it heavily regulates the practices of its Muslim majority.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://thediplomat.com/2015/04/tajikistan-no-hajj-no-hijab-and-shave-your-beard/|title=Tajikistan: No Hajj, No Hijab, and Shave Your Beard|first=Catherine|last=Putz|website=thediplomat.com|access-date=12 September 2021|archive-date=21 October 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191021054613/https://thediplomat.com/2015/04/tajikistan-no-hajj-no-hijab-and-shave-your-beard/|url-status=live}}</ref> Sunni Islam of the [[Hanafi]] school has been officially recognised by the government since 2009.<ref>{{cite web|author=Avaz Yuldashev|url=http://asiaplus.tj/news/16/47964.html|script-title=ru:«Ханафия» объявлена официальным религиозным течением Таджикистана|trans-title="Hanafi" declared the official religious movement in Tajikistan|language=ru|date=5 March 2009|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100825055806/http://asiaplus.tj/news/16/47964.html|archive-date=25 August 2010}}</ref> The government has declared two Islamic holidays, [[Eid ul-Fitr]] and [[Eid al-Adha]], as state holidays. According to a [[US State Department]] release and Pew research group, the population of Tajikistan is 98% [[Muslim]]. Approximately 87–95% of them are [[Sunni Islam|Sunni]] and roughly 3% are [[Shia]] and roughly 7% are [[non-denominational Muslims]].<ref>[http://www.pewforum.org/2012/08/09/the-worlds-muslims-unity-and-diversity-1-religious-affiliation/#identity Pew Forum on Religious & Public life, Chapter 1: Religious Affiliation] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161226113158/http://www.pewforum.org/2012/08/09/the-worlds-muslims-unity-and-diversity-1-religious-affiliation/ |date=26 December 2016 }} retrieved 29 October 2013.</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=https://2009-2017.state.gov/r/pa/ei/bgn/5775.htm |title=Background Note: Tajikistan |publisher=State.gov |access-date=2 October 2009 |archive-date=13 May 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210513180616/https://2009-2017.state.gov/r/pa/ei/bgn/5775.htm |url-status=live }}</ref> The Shia part of the population predominantly live in the [[Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Region]] and are followers of the [[Isma'ilism|Ismaili]] branch of Shia Islam.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Keeping religion alive: performing Pamiri identity in Central Asia {{!}} IIAS |url=https://www.iias.asia/the-newsletter/article/keeping-religion-alive-performing-pamiri-identity-central-asia |access-date=26 May 2022 |website=www.iias.asia |archive-date=11 August 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220811135252/https://www.iias.asia/the-newsletter/article/keeping-religion-alive-performing-pamiri-identity-central-asia |url-status=live }}</ref> The remaining 2% of the population are followers of [[Russian Orthodox Church|Russian Orthodoxy]], [[Protestantism]], [[Zoroastrianism]] and [[Buddhism]]. Muslims fast during Ramadan, while about one third in the countryside and 10% in the cities observe daily prayer and dietary restrictions.<ref>{{cite web |title=U.S. Department of State Annual Report on International Religious Freedom for 1999: Tajikistan |url=https://1997-2001.state.gov/global/human_rights/irf/irf_rpt/1999/irf_tajikist99.html |website=U.S. Department of State Archive |publisher=U.S. Department of State |access-date=29 November 2021 |archive-date=29 November 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211129065456/https://1997-2001.state.gov/global/human_rights/irf/irf_rpt/1999/irf_tajikist99.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

As of January 2016, as part of an "anti-radicalisation campaign", police in the Khatlon region reportedly shaved the beards of 13,000 men and shut down 160 shops selling the [[hijab]]. Shaving beards and discouraging women from wearing hijabs is part of a government campaign targeting trends that are deemed "alien and inconsistent with Tajik culture", and "to preserve secular traditions".<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-35372754 |title=Tajikistan's battle against beards |access-date=27 January 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160124221542/http://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-35372754 |archive-date=24 January 2016 |work=BBC News |date=21 January 2016 |last1=Sarkorova |first1=Anora }}</ref>

There is a concern for religious institutions becoming active in the political sphere. The [[Islamic Renaissance Party of Tajikistan|Islamic Renaissance Party]] (IRP), a combatant in the [[Tajikistan Civil War|1992–1997 Civil War]] and then-proponent of the creation of an [[State religion#Islamic states|Islamic state]] in Tajikistan, constitutes no more than 30% of the government by statute. Membership in [[Hizb ut-Tahrir]], a militant Islamic party which aims for an overthrow of secular governments and the unification of Tajiks under one Islamic state, is illegal and members are subject to arrest and imprisonment.<ref>{{cite news | title =Hizb ut Tahrir | newspaper =BBC News | publisher =BBC | date =27 August 2003 | url =https://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/programmes/newsnight/3182271.stm | access-date =12 September 2013 | url-status =live | archive-url =http://archive.wikiwix.com/cache/20130913234029/http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/programmes/newsnight/3182271.stm | archive-date =13 September 2013 | df =dmy-all }}</ref>

By law, religious communities must register with the State Committee on Religious Affairs (SCRA) and with local authorities. Registration with the SCRA requires a charter, a list of ten or more members, and evidence of local government approval for the prayer site location. Religious groups that do not have a physical structure are not allowed to gather publicly for prayer. Failure to register can result in fines and closure of a place of worship. There are reports that registration at the local level is sometimes difficult to obtain.<ref>[http://www.forum18.org/Archive.php?article_id=190 TAJIKISTAN: Religious freedom survey, November 2003] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100613013715/http://forum18.org/Archive.php?article_id=190 |date=13 June 2010 }} -[[Forum 18]] News Service, 20 November 2003</ref> People under the age of 18 are barred from public religious practice.<ref>{{Cite web|title=U. S. Department of State International Religious Freedom Report for 2013, Executive Summary.|url=https://2009-2017.state.gov/j/drl/rls/irf/religiousfreedom/index.htm#wrapper|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170207121457/https://2009-2017.state.gov/j/drl/rls/irf/religiousfreedom/index.htm#wrapper|archive-date=7 February 2017|access-date=2 August 2014}}</ref>

Approximately 1.6% of the population in Tajikistan is [[Christianity|Christian]], mostly [[Russian Orthodox Church|Orthodox Christians]].<ref name="pewforum.org">[http://www.pewforum.org/2015/04/02/religious-projection-table/2010/percent/all/ Religious Composition by Country, 2010–2050 | Pew Research Center] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170802041823/http://www.pewforum.org/2015/04/02/religious-projection-table/2010/percent/all/ |date=2 August 2017 }}. Pewforum.org (2 April 2015). Retrieved on 20 January 2017.</ref><ref name="globalreligiousfutures.org">[http://www.globalreligiousfutures.org/countries/tajikistan#/?affiliations_religion_id=0&affiliations_year=2010&region_name=All%20Countries&restrictions_year=2013 Tajikistan – Pew-Templeton Global Religious Futures Project] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170209010624/http://www.globalreligiousfutures.org/countries/tajikistan |date=9 February 2017 }}. Globalreligiousfutures.org. Retrieved on 20 January 2017.</ref> The territory of Tajikistan is part of the [[:ru:Душанбинская епархия|Dushanbe and Tajikistan Diocese]] of the [[:ru:Среднеазиатский митрополичий округ|Central Asian Metropolitan District]] of the [[Russian Orthodox Church|Russian Orthodox Moscow Patriarchate]]. The country is home to communities of [[Catholic Church|Catholics]], [[Armenian Apostolic Church|Armenian Christians]], [[Protestantism|Protestants]], [[Lutheranism|Lutherans]], [[Jehovah's Witnesses]], [[Baptists]], [[Mormons]], and [[Adventists]].<ref>{{cite web |title=Tajikistan – Religion |url=http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/25.htm |website=Country Studies |access-date=5 February 2021 |archive-date=15 May 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210515173642/http://countrystudies.us/tajikistan/25.htm |url-status=live }}</ref>

[[Bukharan Jews]] have lived in Tajikistan since the 2nd century BC. In the 1940s, the Jewish community of Tajikistan numbered nearly 30,000 people. Most were Persian‑speaking Bukharan Jews who had lived in the region for millennia, along with Ashkenazi Jews from Eastern Europe who resettled there in the Soviet era. As of 2011, the Jewish population was estimated at fewer than 500, with roughly half living in [[Dushanbe]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.tabletmag.com/jewish-news-and-politics/54896/home-stand|title=Home Stand|work=Tablet Magazine|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151211090933/http://www.tabletmag.com/jewish-news-and-politics/54896/home-stand|archive-date=11 December 2015|date=4 January 2011}}</ref>

=== Education ===
{{Main|Education in Tajikistan}}

[[File:Tajik National University (Main Building).jpg|thumb|[[Tajik National University]] in Dushanbe]]
An estimated 99.8% of the population of Tajikistan have the ability to read and write.<ref name=CIA />

Public education in Tajikistan consists of 11 years of primary and secondary education and the government planned to implement a 12-year system in 2016.<ref name="scholaro.com">{{cite web|url=http://www.scholaro.com/db/Countries/Tajikistan/Education-System|title=Tajikistan Education System|website=www.scholaro.com|access-date=4 August 2024}}</ref> There are a number of [[List of universities in Tajikistan|tertiary education institutions]] including [[Khujand State University]], which has 76 departments in 15 faculties,<ref name="scholaro.com" /> [[Tajikistan State University of Law, Business, & Politics]], [[Khorugh State University]], [[Agricultural University of Tajikistan]], [[Tajik National University]], and other institutions. Universities were established during the Soviet Era. {{As of|2008}} tertiary education enrollment was 17%, below the sub-regional average of 37%,<ref name="unicef.org">[http://www.unicef.org/ceecis/Tajikistan.pdf Education in Tajikistan] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131106003014/http://www.unicef.org/ceecis/Tajikistan.pdf |date=6 November 2013 }}. unicef.org</ref> while higher than any other [[low-income country]] after [[Syria]].<ref>{{Cite web|title=School enrollment, tertiary (% gross) – Low income {{!}} Data|url=https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SE.TER.ENRR?locations=XM&most_recent_value_desc=true|access-date=27 July 2020|website=data.worldbank.org|archive-date=27 July 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200727191035/https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SE.TER.ENRR?locations=XM&most_recent_value_desc=true|url-status=live}}</ref> Tajiks left the education system due to "low" demand in the labour market for people with "extensive" educational training or professional skills.<ref name="unicef.org" />

Public spending on education was relatively constant between 2005–2012 and fluctuated from 3.5% to 4.1% of [[GDP]]<ref>[http://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SE.XPD.TOTL.GD.ZS/countries/TJ?display=graph Tajikistan, Public spending on education, total (% of GDP)] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140714214007/http://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SE.XPD.TOTL.GD.ZS/countries/TJ?display=graph |date=14 July 2014 }} World Bank</ref> below the [[OECD]] average of 6%.<ref name="unicef.org" /> The [[United Nations]] reported that the level of spending was "severely inadequate to meet the requirements of the country's high-needs education system."<ref name="unicef.org" />

According to a UNICEF-supported survey, about 25% of girls in Tajikistan fail to complete compulsory primary education because of poverty and gender bias,<ref>{{cite web | title =Tajikistan hosts education forum | work =News note | publisher =UNICEF | date =9 June 2005 | url =http://www.unicef.org/media/media_27308.html | access-date =12 September 2013 | url-status =live | archive-url =https://web.archive.org/web/20131104204553/http://www.unicef.org/media/media_27308.html | archive-date =4 November 2013 | df =dmy-all }}</ref> while [[literacy]] is "generally high" in Tajikistan.<ref name="hdrstats.undp.org" /> Estimates of out of school children range from 4.6% to 19.4% with the majority being girls.<ref name="unicef.org" />

In September 2017, the [[University of Central Asia]] launched its second campus in Khorog, Tajikistan, offering majors in Earth & Environmental Sciences and Economics.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.ucentralasia.org/|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161129122624/http://www.ucentralasia.org/|url-status=dead|title=University of Central Asia – University of Central Asia|archive-date=29 November 2016|website=www.ucentralasia.org}}</ref> Tajikistan was ranked 108th in the [[Global Innovation Index]] in 2025.<ref>{{Cite web |title=GII Innovation Ecosystems & Data Explorer 2025 |url=https://www.wipo.int/gii-ranking/en/tajikistan |access-date=2025-10-16 |website=WIPO}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last1=Dutta |first1=Soumitra |url=https://www.wipo.int/web-publications/global-innovation-index-2025/en/index.html |title=Global Innovation Index 2025: Innovation at a Crossroads |last2=Lanvin |first2=Bruno |publisher=[[World Intellectual Property Organization]] |year=2025 |isbn=978-92-805-3797-0 |page=19 |language=en |doi=10.34667/tind.58864 |access-date=2025-10-17}}</ref>

Science in the territory of Tajikistan achieved "success" in the Middle Ages, and scientific organisations were created in the Soviet period. During the period of independence, the scientific sphere has experienced a "crisis": the annual number of patent applications for inventions decreased in 1994–2011 from 193 to 5.<ref>''Султанова Л. Ш., Айдинова М. А.'' Значение канала трансфера новых технологий для Узбекистана // Актуальные вопросы современной науки. — 2014. — № 1 (2,3). — С. 87.</ref> A contribution to science is made by universities, where in 2011 6707 researchers worked, of which 2450 had academic degrees.<ref>''Шарипов М. М.'' Роль вузов в формировании и развитии инновационной экономики в Республике Таджикистан // Современные проблемы науки и образования. — 2014. — № 6. — С. 627.</ref>

Arab News. 21 May 2007.</ref> More than 70% of the female population lives in traditional villages.<ref>{{cite news|last1=Azimova|first1=Aigul|last2=Abazbekova|first2=Nazgul|title=Millennium Development Goals: Saving women's Lives|newspaper=D+C|page=289|date=27 July 2011|url=http://www.dandc.eu/en/article/tough-labour-conditions-central-asias-female-health-workers|access-date=12 September 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140217124220/http://www.dandc.eu/en/article/tough-labour-conditions-central-asias-female-health-workers|archive-date=17 February 2014}}</ref>

=== Health ===
{{Main|Health in Tajikistan}}

[[File:Big Hospital.jpg|thumb|A hospital in Dushanbe]]
The state's Ministry of Labour and Social Welfare reported that 104,272 disabled people were registered in Tajikistan in 2000. == Culture ==
{{Main|Culture of Tajikistan}}
[[File:Одежда жителей Гиссара.JPG|thumb|Tajik traditional dress]]

The culture of Tajikistan has developed over several thousand years. Tajik culture can be divided into two areas, Metropolitan and Kuhiston (Highland). Modern city centres include [[Dushanbe]] (the capital), [[Khujand]], [[Kulob]], and [[Panjakent]].

The [[Tajik language]] is the mother tongue of around 80% of the citizens of Tajikistan.<ref name="BritannicaPeople">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan – People |url=https://www.britannica.com/place/Tajikistan/People |website=Britannica |access-date=5 February 2021 |archive-date=25 February 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225231634/https://www.britannica.com/place/Tajikistan/People |url-status=live}}</ref> There are [[Uzbeks|Uzbek]], [[Kyrgyz people|Kyrgyz]] and [[Russians|Russian]] minorities.<ref name="BritannicaPeople" /> The [[Yaghnobis|Yaghnobi people]] live in areas of northern Tajikistan. The estimated number of Yaghnobis is about 25,000. Forced migrations in the 20th century decimated their numbers. They speak the [[Yaghnobi language]], which is the only direct descendant of the [[Sogdian language]] and has often been called Neo-Sogdian in academic literature.<ref name="YaghnobiUN">{{Cite web|title=Award-winning Yaghnobi-Czech dictionary captures dying language|url=http://cuni.cz/UKEN-300.html|access-date=5 January 2021|website=Charles University|language=en|archive-date=11 April 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210411004322/https://cuni.cz/UKEN-300.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

Tajikistan artisans created the [[Dushanbe Tea House]], which was presented in 1988 as a gift to the sister city of [[Boulder, Colorado]].<ref>[https://web.archive.org/web/20120527123614/http://www.boulder-dushanbe.org/teahouse.html The Dushanbe-Boulder tea house]. boulder-dushanbe.org</ref>

In the country, especially among women from the indigenous population, the wearing of traditional national clothing is preserved. The seamstresses and embroiderers of regions of Tajikistan use factory fabrics and local needlework embroidery for home decoration and women's clothing. The practice of [[Chakan embroidery]] is preserved among women in certain areas, passing the knowledge down from one generation to the next.<ref name="Chakan Embroidery">{{cite web |title=UNESCO - Chakan, embroidery art in the Republic of Tajikistan |url=https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/chakan-embroidery-art-in-the-republic-of-tajikistan-01397 |website=Intangible Cultural Heritage |access-date=25 April 2022 |language=en |archive-date=10 December 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201210130159/https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/chakan-embroidery-art-in-the-republic-of-tajikistan-01397 |url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Festivals and national holidays ===
{{Main|Public holidays in Tajikistan}}
Tajikistan celebrates several ancient national holidays that form the core of its cultural identity. These include [[Nowruz]], [[Mehregan|Mehrgon]], [[Sadeh|Sada]], and [[Tirgan|Tirgon]], which are recognized as the most valuable elements of Tajik intangible cultural heritage.

'''Nowruz''' is the most important holiday in Tajikistan, marking the spring equinox, the beginning of the solar year, and the New Year on the [[Iranian calendars|Iranian calendar]]. Nowruz, meaning "New Day," heralds the arrival of spring and is observed annually on March 21, the vernal equinox. The celebration traces back over 6,000 years, with historical ties to the [[Silk Road]]. In 2026, Dushanbe was selected as the main center for international Nowruz celebrations. The International Festival "Nowruz – Cultural and Global Heritage" is held annually during the third decade of March. The holiday is celebrated from 21 to 24 March, with government employees receiving a total of 10 days off.

'''Mehrgon''' is an ancient harvest festival rooted in [[Zoroastrianism|Zoroastrian]] traditions, dedicated to the deity Mehr ([[Mithra]]). In the ancient Persian calendar, Mehrgon fell in mid-autumn, symbolizing the end of agricultural work and a time to thank the gods for prosperity. It was revived in 2009 by presidential decree and added to Tajikistan's national list of intangible cultural heritage in 2017.

'''Sada''' is one of the oldest holidays of the Tajik people, symbolizing the approach of Nowruz and the arrival of spring. According to scholars, Sada falls fifty nights and fifty days before Nowruz and serves as a reminder to farmers to prepare for the spring sowing. The name "Sada" is usually associated with the number one hundred, or fifty days and fifty nights, before Nowruz. In December 2023, the joint nomination of Tajikistan and Iran for the "Sada Festival" was inscribed on [[UNESCO|UNESCO's]] Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.<ref name="UNESCO_Sada">{{cite web |title=Sada Festival inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List |url=https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/sada-festival-02056 |website=UNESCO |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

'''Tirgon''' is another ancient festival that, along with Sada, Navruz, and Mehrgon, is celebrated as part of Tajikistan's rich cultural heritage.

=== Literature ===
{{Main|Tajik literature}}
Tajik literature has a rich history dating back over a thousand years. [[Rudaki]] (858–941 CE), known as the "Adam of Poets," is considered a father of classical Persian literature and takes centre stage in Tajikistan's literary heritage. He is regarded as the first major poet to write in New Persian. Although only a small portion of his work has survived, his influence on Persian and Tajik poetry remains profound.

[[Sadriddin Ayni|Sadriddin Aini]] (1878–1954) is regarded as the founder of modern Tajik literature. He was a poet, writer, historian, translator, scholar, and political figure. Aini excelled in research and literary criticism, and his papers on the lives and works of Rudaki are particularly notable.

In 2025, Tajikistan participated in the 36th Tehran International Book Fair with more than 400 newly published titles. Works by classical poets such as Rudaki, [[Ferdowsi]], Hafez, Saadi, [[Abdul-Qādir Bedil|Bidel]], and Jami, as well as contemporary writers like [[Mirzo Tursunzoda]], [[Layeq Sherali|Layeq Shir-Ali]], [[Mu'min Qanoat]], [[Gulrukhsor Safieva|Gulrukhsor]], and [[Muhammad Ghoyib]], were presented. The Persian translation of Sadriddin Aini's novel ''Odina'' was published in Iran in 2025. A collection of poems titled ''Kamandi Zulf'' by [[Zulfiya Atoulloeva|Zulfiya Atoi]], a folk poet of Tajikistan, was published by the ECO Cultural Institute in [[Tehran]].

=== Visual arts and handicrafts ===
Chakan embroidery is a distinctive form of Tajik embroidery that was inscribed in 2018 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.<ref name="UNESCO_Chakan">{{cite web |title=Chakan, embroidery art in the Republic of Tajikistan |url=https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/chakan-embroidery-art-in-the-republic-of-tajikistan-01397 |website=UNESCO |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The art of Chakan embroidery involves sewing ornaments, images of flowers and symbolic drawings with colourful threads on cotton or silk fabrics. The National Festival "Sad Gul-i Chakan" was held on 5 March 2025 at the Dushanbe Philharmonic Hall, with craftswomen from Sughd, Khatlon, Badakhshan, and the Rasht valleys displaying their works.

Suzani embroidery is another important Tajik handicraft. Tajik masters embroidered suzani wall panels, zardevor ceiling friezes, borpush, kars, ruidjo covers and blankets, joynamaz prayer mats, as well as chakan dresses. The city of [[Panjakent]] was designated as a "World Craft City for Panjakent Suzani Embroidery" by the World Crafts Council on 21 October 2025.<ref name="WCC_Panjakent">{{cite web |title=World Craft City designation for Panjakent |url=https://worldcraftscouncil.org/ |website=World Crafts Council |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In September 2025, the exhibition "The World of Tajik Embroidery" opened at the Azerbaijan National Carpet Museum, featuring 56 unique pieces from the Golden Treasury of the National Museum of Tajikistan. The exhibition included suzani embroidery, chakan dresses, popurduzi embroidery, blankets, prayer rugs, embroidered men's waistbands (miyonband), mirror covers (oinakhalta), wall embroidery, and tablecloths.

=== Music and performing arts ===
{{Main|Music of Tajikistan}}
Tajikistan has a rich musical heritage, with two genres – [[Shashmaqam|Shashmaqom]] and [[Falak]] – recognized as masterpieces of intangible cultural heritage.

'''Shashmaqom''' is the basis of Tajik traditional music and the highest manifestation of the musical mind of the people. Shashmaqom, meaning "six maqoms", constitutes a fusion of vocal and instrumental music, melodic and rhythmic idioms and poetry. The system of musical grammar of professional traditional Tajik music has been formed on the basis of maqoms. The six maqoms are Buzurg, Rost, Navo, Dugoh, Segoh, and Iroq. The poetic texts of the vocal sections are drawn from the works of classical Persian-Tajik poets such as [[Hafez]], [[Saadi Shirazi|Saadi]], and [[Jami]]. Traditional instruments accompanying Shashmaqom include the [[tanbur]], [[dutar]], and [[Daf|doira]]. The musical genre emerged in the pre-Islamic period in [[Bukhara]] and later spread throughout the Eastern world. In 2003, Shashmaqom was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.<ref name="UNESCO_Shashmaqom">{{cite web |title=Shashmaqom music - UNESCO |url=https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/shashmaqom-music-00063 |website=UNESCO |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> May 12 is celebrated in Tajikistan as "Shashmaqom Day." In June 2025, a Shashmaqom cultural program was held in Paris to mark the 700th anniversary of Hafez Sherazi and the 60th anniversary of UNESCO's water sciences.

'''Falak''' is a musical genre consisting of a cycle of folk songs, accompanied by string musical instruments such as the dumbra, tablak, and ghizhak. Falak, meaning 'heaven', 'fortune' and 'universe', is the traditional folklore music of the mountain people of Tajikistan. The expressive and philosophical musical genre may be performed by a male or female soloist, either a cappella, or with a single instrumental accompaniment or an ensemble and dancers. It is performed in the Khatlon region, Badakhshan, and the Rasht valley. In October 2025, an international symposium titled "Falak and State Independence – from Revival to Global Recognition" was held in Dushanbe to comprehensively examine Falak as a unique phenomenon in Tajikistan's musical culture and its role in strengthening national identity.

Other traditional musical genres include '''Rubobnavozi''' (skill of playing the rubab), '''Qonunnavozi''' (skill of playing the qonun), and '''Amiri''' (folk songs and melodies performed at celebrations and concerts).

=== Cuisine ===
{{Main|Tajik cuisine}}

Tajik cuisine is hearty, filling, and rooted in Central Asian and Persian influences.

[[Pilaf|Oshi Palav]] (pilaf) is the national dish in Tajikistan. It is a rice dish made with shredded yellow turnip or carrots, pieces of meat, all fried together in vegetable oil or mutton fat in a special qazan (a wok-shaped cauldron) over an open flame. There are up to 200 varieties of the dish. Oshi palav is prepared for regular mealtimes, as well as social gatherings, celebrations, and rituals. In 2016, Oshi Palav was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.<ref name="UNESCO_Palav">{{cite web |title=Oshi Palav, traditional pilaf in Tajikistan |url=https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/oshi-palav-traditional-pilaf-in-tajikistan-01168 |website=UNESCO |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

[[Qurutob]] is a traditional dish made from pieces of flatbread soaked in yoghurt or dried curd sauce, topped with onions and herbs. Other traditional dishes include [[Manti (food)|mantu]] (steamed dumplings), [[Chorba|shurbo]] and piti (meat and vegetable soups), [[Laghman (food)|laghman]] and ugro (noodle soups), and sambusa (stuffed pastries). Traditional meals typically begin with dried fruits, nuts, and halva, proceed to soups and meat dishes, and conclude with plov.

=== Sport ===

Another traditional sport is [[buzkashi]], a game played on horseback, similar to [[polo]]. Buzkashi may be played as an individual sport and as a team sport. It is played at [[Nowruz]] celebrations.<ref>{{cite book|last1=Abdullaev|first1=Kamoludin|last2=Akbarzaheh|first2=Shahram|title=Historical Dictionary of Tajikistan|publisher=Scarecrow Press|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=PB5xgFRuYPUC&pg=PA85|isbn=978-0-8108-7379-7|page=85|date=27 April 2010|access-date=8 November 2015|archive-date=24 January 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230124200453/https://books.google.com/books?id=PB5xgFRuYPUC&pg=PA85|url-status=live}}</ref> In 2025, buzkashi was featured prominently during the Nowruz celebrations in Dushanbe.

At the [[2025 Asian Winter Games]] in Harbin, China, Tajikistan was represented by four athletes (all male) in alpine skiing, though the country did not secure any medals. The country is actively preparing for the [[2026 Asian Games]] in Aichi-Nagoya, Japan, and the [[2028 Summer Olympics]]. The Tajikistani team is expected to participate in 41 sports disciplines at the 2026 Asian Games.

The [[Tajikistan Football Federation]] is the governing body of [[Association football|football]] in Tajikistan. The men's national team made its historic debut at the [[2023 AFC Asian Cup]] in Qatar, where it advanced to the quarter-finals.<ref>{{cite web|title=Segrt: Asian Cup euphoria can spark bright Tajikistan future|url=https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/petar-segrt-interview-tajikistan|website=[[FIFA]]|date=13 February 2024|access-date=6 January 2025|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240909224039/https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/petar-segrt-interview-tajikistan|archive-date=9 September 2024|url-status=live}}</ref> The team finished second in Group A behind hosts and eventual champions Qatar, ahead of China and Lebanon, before defeating the United Arab Emirates on penalties in the round of 16.<ref name="AsianCup2023">{{cite news |title=Tajikistan make stellar debut at the AFC Asian Cup Qatar 2023 |url=https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/ |work=The Peninsula |date=27 January 2024 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="AsianCup2023-2">{{cite news |title=AFC Asian Cup 2023: Tajikistan march into RO16 to create history |url=https://khelnow.com/ |work=Khel Now |date=22 January 2024 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The team participated in the [[AFC Challenge Cup]] on four occasions, winning the inaugural edition in [[2006 AFC Challenge Cup|2006]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.the-afc.com/en/challenge-cup-previous-competitions/afc-challenge-cup-2006/5657|title=Tajikistan lift inaugural AFC Challenge Cup|website=[[Asian Football Confederation]]|date=19 April 2006|archive-url=https://archive.today/20130203153915/http://www.the-afc.com/en/challenge-cup-previous-competitions/afc-challenge-cup-2006/5657|archive-date=3 February 2013|url-status=dead}}</ref>

The men's team finished 2025 with a FIFA ranking of 101st.<ref name="FIFA2025">{{cite news |title=Национальная сборная Таджикистана по футболу завершает 2025 год на 101-м месте в рейтинге ФИФА |url=https://khovar.tj/ |work=NIAT Khovar |date=23 December 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Among Asian Football Confederation members, the team ranks 17th.<ref name="FIFA2025-2">{{cite web |title=Национальная сборная Таджикистана завершает 2025 год на 101-м месте в рейтинге ФИФА |url=https://fft.tj/ |website=Football Federation of Tajikistan |date=22 December 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> During 2025, the team played 10 official matches, recording six wins, two draws, and two losses.<ref name="FIFA2025" /> The team's next official match is scheduled for 31 March 2026 in Hisar.<ref name="FIFA2025" />

The women's side has twice participated in the [[CAFA Women's Championship]], finishing third in the [[2018 CAFA Women's Championship|2018 edition]] and [[2022 CAFA Women's Championship|hosting the tournament in 2022]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.the-afc.com/en/more/news/uzbekistan_inaugural_cafa_womens_championship_winners.html|title=Uzbekistan inaugural CAFA Women's Championship winners|website=[[Asian Football Confederation]]|date=2 December 2018|access-date=6 January 2025|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250106150312/https://www.the-afc.com/en/more/news/uzbekistan_inaugural_cafa_womens_championship_winners.html|archive-date=6 January 2025|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://turkmenistan.gov.tm/index.php/en/post/64765/turkmen-football-players-tied-team-tajikistan-cafa-2022-championship-dushanbe|title=Turkmen football players tied with the team of Tajikistan at the CAFA-2022 championship in Dushanbe|website=[[Government of Turkmenistan|Turkmenistan.gov.tm]]|date=10 July 2022|access-date=6 January 2025|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250106151301/https://turkmenistan.gov.tm/index.php/en/post/64765/turkmen-football-players-tied-team-tajikistan-cafa-2022-championship-dushanbe|archive-date=6 January 2025|url-status=live}}</ref>

The youth teams have also achieved success. The under-17 team won the CAFA U17 Championship in 2025 in Tashkent, and the under-18 team secured a bronze medal at the CAFA U20 Championship in 2025. The under-17 team also participated in the 2025 FIFA U-17 World Cup in Qatar with players from the FIFA Talent Academy.<ref name="U17WorldCup">{{cite web |title=Tajik U-17 Football Team Arrives in Chonburi to Participate in the 2023 Asian Cup |url=https://eng.khovar.tj/ |website=NIAT Khovar |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

The 2026 season of the [[Tajikistan Higher League]] began on 6 March 2026 with 12 teams. The league has implemented a foreign player quota of a maximum of 7 players, with only 5 allowed on the field simultaneously. Each team is also required to include at least one player born in 2008 or later in the starting lineup.<ref name="League2026">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Higher League 2026 |url=https://fft.tj/ |website=Football Federation of Tajikistan |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The Super Cup is scheduled for 23 May 2026 at the Hisar Central Stadium, and the Cup final will be held at the Vakhsh Arena in Bokhtar. A women's team will become mandatory for all clubs from 2027.

A new National Stadium of Tajikistan is under construction in Dushanbe on a 9.5-hectare site with a capacity of more than 30,000 seats.<ref name="NationalStadium1">{{cite web |title=President Emomali Rahmon examined construction progress of National Stadium of Tajikistan |url=https://eng.khovar.tj/ |website=NIAT Khovar |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref><ref name="NationalStadium2">{{cite news |title=President Emomali Rahmon visits construction sites of national teahouse and stadium in Dushanbe |url=https://old.asiaplustj.info/ |work=Asia-Plus |date=3 June 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The stadium will feature 45 office rooms and a conference hall, 34 high-level VIP viewing areas, 9 medical facilities, 36 canteens, and a Tajik football museum built to international standards.<ref name="NationalStadium1" /><ref name="NationalStadium3">{{cite news |title=Emomali Rahmon inspects construction work at Tajikistan National Stadium in Dushanbe |url=https://en.avesta.tj/ |work=Avesta |date=3 June 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> A fire broke out at the construction site in August 2025, but the project continues.<ref name="NationalStadiumFire">{{cite news |title=A football stadium under construction in Dushanbe, which is to become the largest in Tajikistan, caught fire (UPDATED) |url=https://asiaplustj.info/ |work=Asia-Plus |date=20 August 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The Vasi' Stadium in the Vasi' district of Khatlon region was opened on 22 October 2025 with a capacity of 21,000 seats, featuring a 105×68 meter natural grass football pitch and an athletics track. The construction cost approximately 69 million somoni and includes two mini-football fields, volleyball and basketball courts.<ref name="VasiStadium">{{cite web |title=Vasi' Stadium Opening |url=https://khovar.tj/ |website=NIAT Khovar |date=22 October 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> It is part of the 2022–2026 football development program, which aims to build 130 sports projects in Khatlon province.

Several other sports are also actively pursued. The [[Tajikistan Cricket Federation]] was formed in 2012 as the governing body for the sport of [[cricket]] in Tajikistan. It was granted affiliate membership of the [[Asian Cricket Council]] in the same year.<ref>{{cite web |title=Cricket in Tajikistan |url=https://emergingcricket.com/teams/tajikistan/ |website=Emerging Cricket |access-date=5 February 2021 |archive-date=23 January 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210123003712/https://emergingcricket.com/teams/tajikistan/ |url-status=live }}</ref> At the CAFA Nations Cup 2025, Tajikistan is placed in Group B alongside India, Afghanistan, and Iran.

In 2008, rugby union was officially registered with the [[Ministry of Justice (Tajikistan)|Ministry of Justice]], and there are three men's clubs.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Таъсиси нахустин тими духтаронаи рогбӣ дар Тоҷикистон|trans-title=Establishment of the first girls' rugby team in Tajikistan|url=http://www.bbc.com/tajik/institutional/2012/04/120414_zm_regy_dushanbe.shtml|access-date=5 October 2020|website=BBC Tajik/Persian|language=tg}}{{Dead link|date=March 2022 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref> In November 2025, Asia Rugby held a meeting with the Minister of Sports and the National Olympic Committee of Tajikistan with the aim of building a solid foundation for the Rugby Federation of Tajikistan through technical training, educational programs, and financial assistance.<ref name="Rugby2025">{{cite web |title=Asia Rugby Meeting with Tajikistan |url=https://www.asiarugby.com/ |website=Asia Rugby |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

[[Khorugh]], capital of [[Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Region]], is the location of highest altitude where [[bandy]] has been played.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://translate.google.com/translate?hl=en&sl=ru&u=http%3A%2F%2Fbandynet.ru%2Fv1%2Fnode%2F14931&prev=search|title=Google Translate|work=google.co.uk|access-date=13 November 2016|archive-date=30 January 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170130025448/https://translate.google.com/translate?hl=en&sl=ru&u=http%3A%2F%2Fbandynet.ru%2Fv1%2Fnode%2F14931&prev=search|url-status=live}}</ref>

Tajikistan has one [[ski resort]], called [[Safed Dara]] (formerly ''Takob''), near the town of [[Varzob]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.tripadvisor.ca/Hotel_Review-g811255-d9792233-Reviews-Safed_Dara-Varzob_Karotegin_Province.html|title=Safed Dara|work=Trip Advisor|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170811060158/https://www.tripadvisor.ca/Hotel_Review-g811255-d9792233-Reviews-Safed_Dara-Varzob_Karotegin_Province.html|archive-date=11 August 2017}}</ref> Safed Dara, meaning "White Valley" in Tajik, is located approximately 50 kilometers from Dushanbe, about an hour's drive.<ref name="SafedDara1">{{cite news |title=«Сафеддара» — гармония природы, культуры и активного отдыха |url=https://khovar.tj/ |work=NIAT Khovar |date=16 February 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The resort sits among the snowy peaks of Tajikistan at an elevation reaching up to 3,020 meters.<ref name="SafedDara2">{{cite web |title=Safed Dara Ski Resort |url=https://www.tripadvisor.com.my/ |website=TripAdvisor |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> In 2025, the resort added an additional 1.5&nbsp;km gondola run, doubling the size of the skiable terrain.<ref name="SafedDara3">{{cite web |title=SAFED DARA (2025) All You Should Know BEFORE You Go (w/ Reviews) |url=https://www.tripadvisor.com.my/ |website=TripAdvisor |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The resort features beginner-friendly slopes, a few lifts, and untouched mountain terrain for more adventurous skiers and snowboarders.<ref name="SafedDara4">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan - Best Places To Ski And Snowboard |url=https://skimapmaster.com/ |website=Ski Map Master |date=12 November 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> A one-day tour package is available for $180 per person, offering skiing, snowboarding, snowtubing, and cable car rides over 8 to 10 hours.<ref name="SafedDara5">{{cite web |title=SAFED-DARA: Skiing, snowboarding, One-day tour |url=https://happytovisit.com/ |website=Happy To Visit |date=2 December 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

The Tajikistan national volleyball team hosted the CAVA U16 Girls' Volleyball Championship in July 2025 at the Olympic Palace in Dushanbe, marking the country's first international volleyball event.<ref name="Volleyball2025">{{cite web |title=CAVA U16 Girls' Volleyball Championship |url=https://www.asianvolleyball.net/ |website=Asian Volleyball Confederation |access-date=16 July 2026 |archive-date=24 December 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181224205516/http://asianvolleyball.net/ |url-status=dead }}</ref> In November 2025, the women's national team lost to Iran 3-0 at the Islamic Solidarity Games.<ref name="Volleyball2025-2">{{cite web |title=Islamic Solidarity Games 2025 |url=https://www.islamicsolidaritygames.com/ |access-date=16 July 2026 |archive-date=9 December 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211209095613/http://islamicsolidaritygames.com/ |url-status=dead }}</ref>

In tennis, the 15th edition of the President's Cup of Tajikistan was held in September 2025 with the participation of more than 100 athletes.<ref name="Tennis2025">{{cite web |title=President's Cup of Tajikistan 2025 |url=https://www.itftennis.com/ |website=ITF Tennis |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The J30 Dushanbe 2026 tournament is registered on the ITF calendar for June 2026.<ref name="Tennis2026">{{cite web |title=J30 Dushanbe 2026 |url=https://www.itftennis.com/ |website=ITF Tennis |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In badminton, Tajikistani athletes won bronze medals in the doubles under-15 category at the Central Asian Badminton Championships in Tashkent in September 2025.<ref name="Badminton2025">{{cite web |title=Central Asian Badminton Championships 2025 |url=https://badmintonasia.org/ |website=Badminton Asia |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In kurash, the Tajikistan national team won 8 medals across various age categories at the Asian Kurash Championships in South Korea in August 2025.<ref name="Kurash2025">{{cite web |title=Asian Kurash Championships 2025 |url=https://www.kurash-ika.org/ |website=International Kurash Association |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In boxing, Behruz Umarov won Tajikistan's first gold medal at the 2025 CIS Games in Azerbaijan in October 2025.<ref name="Boxing2025-1">{{cite web |title=CIS Games 2025 |url=https://www.cisgames.org/ |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref> The Tajikistan national boxing championship was held in Khujand in October 2025 with the participation of 112 male and 19 female boxers.<ref name="Boxing2025-2">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan National Boxing Championship 2025 |url=https://www.iba.sport/ |website=International Boxing Association |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The national boxing team also participated in the 2025 World Championships in Dubai in December 2025.<ref name="Boxing2025-3">{{cite web |title=World Boxing Championships 2025 |url=https://www.iba.sport/ |website=International Boxing Association |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In judo, Tajikistan hosted the Dushanbe Grand Slam 2025 from 2–4 May 2025, one of the most important judo events in the world.<ref name="Judo2025-1">{{cite web |title=Dushanbe Grand Slam 2025 |url=https://www.ijf.org/ |website=International Judo Federation |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Temur Rakhimov (+100&nbsp;kg) won a bronze medal at the World Championships in Budapest in July 2025.<ref name="Judo2025-2">{{cite web |title=World Judo Championships 2025 |url=https://www.ijf.org/ |website=International Judo Federation |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> In November–December 2025, Muhiddin Asadullaev (under 73&nbsp;kg) won a silver medal and Sunnatullah Musavi (under 81&nbsp;kg) won a bronze medal at the Abu Dhabi Grand Slam.<ref name="Judo2025-3">{{cite web |title=Abu Dhabi Grand Slam 2025 |url=https://www.ijf.org/ |website=International Judo Federation |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Tajikistan also hosted the Asian Junior and Youth Cup in Dushanbe in 2025, and the men's national judo championship was held in Dushanbe in December 2025.<ref name="Judo2025-4">{{cite web |title=Asian Junior and Youth Cup Dushanbe 2025 |url=https://www.ijf.org/ |website=International Judo Federation |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In powerlifting, the Tajikistan national team won 5 gold medals at the Asian Championships in Vietnam in July 2025.<ref name="Powerlifting2025-1">{{cite web |title=Asian Powerlifting Championships 2025 |url=https://www.worldpowerlifting.com/ |website=World Powerlifting |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> In December 2025, Davlatmand Adinaev won a gold medal at the WRPF World Championships in Moscow in the 110&nbsp;kg category with a total of 735&nbsp;kg.<ref name="Powerlifting2025-2">{{cite web |title=WRPF World Championships 2025 |url=https://www.worldpowerlifting.com/ |website=World Powerlifting |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In taekwondo, Tajikistani athletes won 2 bronze medals (in the junior and youth divisions) at the Asian Championships in Koching, Malaysia in July 2025.<ref name="Taekwondo2025-1">{{cite web |title=Asian Taekwondo Championships 2025 |url=https://www.worldtaekwondo.org/ |website=World Taekwondo |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> In September 2025, Abduljabbor Bahador (under 54&nbsp;kg) and Nurullah Muradov (under 74&nbsp;kg) won bronze medals at the CIS Games 2025.<ref name="Taekwondo2025-2">{{cite web |title=CIS Games 2025 Taekwondo Results |url=https://www.cisgames.org/ |access-date=16 July 2026 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2026 |bot=InternetArchiveBot }}</ref>

In karate, Tajikistani athletes won 32 medals (13 gold, 12 silver, 7 bronze) at the WFKS-2025 World Cup in Moscow in April 2025.<ref name="Karate2025">{{cite web |title=WFKS World Cup 2025 |url=https://www.wfks.org/ |website=World Federation of Karate Styles |access-date=16 July 2026 |archive-date=18 July 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250718214424/http://wfks.org/ |url-status=dead }}</ref>

In wushu, the Tajikistan national team won 19 medals at the World Wushu-Kungfu Championships in China in November 2025.<ref name="Wushu2025">{{cite web |title=World Wushu-Kungfu Championships 2025 |url=https://www.iwuf.org/ |website=International Wushu Federation |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

In para sports, an international seminar on "Mastering Advanced Techniques in Wheelchair Basketball" was held in Dushanbe in October 2025 with the support of TİKA.<ref name="ParaSports2025">{{cite web |title=Wheelchair Basketball Seminar in Dushanbe |url=https://www.tika.gov.tr/ |website=TİKA |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

Esports is an emerging sector in Tajikistan. The President's Cup esports tournament was held in November 2025, featuring competitions in Counter-Strike 2, Dota 2, PUBG M, Mobile Legends: Bang Bang, and FC 25.<ref name="Esports2025-1">{{cite news |title=В Душанбе проходит киберспортивный турнир «Кубок Президента» |url=https://asiaplustj.info/ |work=Asia-Plus |date=26 November 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The first intercompany esports tournament in Dushanbe was held in June 2025, with teams from Dushanbe City Bank, Humo Lab, MegaFon Tajikistan, and Accelerate Prosperity participating.<ref name="Esports2025-3">{{cite news |title=New corporate team-building format launched in Tajikistan |url=https://old.asiaplustj.info/ |work=Asia-Plus |date=12 June 2025 |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> The esports market in Tajikistan is steadily growing, with increasing interest from both players and spectators.<ref name="Esports2025-4">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Esports Market (2025-2031) |url=https://www.6wresearch.com/ |website=6Wresearch |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> A strategic partnership between Tcell and Esports Championship Services (ESCS) was announced to launch a new era of interactive gaming in Tajikistan.<ref name="Esports2025-5">{{cite news |title=Tcell и ESCS объявляют о стратегическом партнерстве |url=https://tj.vesti.news/ |work=Vesti |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Tajikistan also participates in the CIS Youth Esports Games.

In total, over 2024–2025, Tajikistani athletes won 958 medals (274 gold, 268 silver, 406 bronze) in international competitions.<ref name="TotalMedals">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Sports Achievements 2024-2025 |url=https://www.olympic.tj/ |website=National Olympic Committee of Tajikistan |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

Tajikistan has signed sports cooperation agreements with several countries. In March 2025, Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan signed a 2025–2027 cooperation program in the field of sports. In April 2025, Tajikistan and Iran signed a sports cooperation agreement. Tajikistan also cooperates with China in martial arts (wushu).<ref name="Cooperation">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan International Sports Cooperation |url=https://www.mfs.tj/ |website=Ministry of Sports of Tajikistan |access-date=16 July 2026 |archive-date=29 January 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200129160705/http://mfs.tj/ |url-status=dead }}</ref>

The government has prioritized sports as a social policy, allocating 1,787,500 somoni (approximately $164,000) from the presidential reserve fund for bonuses to medalists of international competitions.<ref name="GovernmentSupport">{{cite web |title=Presidential Decree on Sports Bonuses |url=https://www.president.tj/ |website=President of Tajikistan |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Companies such as Parimatch Tajikistan, MegaFon Tajikistan, and Tojiktelecom sponsor the football league and national teams.<ref name="Sponsors">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Football Sponsors |url=https://fft.tj/ |website=Football Federation of Tajikistan |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>

Despite progress, Tajikistan faces challenges in sports development. The country has limited winter sports development, participating only in alpine skiing. Doping remains a concern; in 2025, Lazizbek Mullajanov (an Olympic champion from Uzbekistan) was suspended for doping, which also affected Davlat Boltaev, who shared training sessions with him. The government has established a National Anti-Doping Center to address this issue.<ref name="AntiDoping">{{cite web |title=National Anti-Doping Center of Tajikistan |url=https://www.wada-ama.org/ |website=World Anti-Doping Agency |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref> Despite infrastructure improvements, further investment in sports facilities is needed.<ref name="Challenges">{{cite web |title=Tajikistan Sports Infrastructure Challenges |url=https://www.olympic.tj/ |website=National Olympic Committee of Tajikistan |access-date=16 July 2026}}</ref>
