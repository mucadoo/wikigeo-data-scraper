'''Indonesia''',{{efn|{{IPAc-en|UK|ˌ|ɪ|n|d|ə|ˈ|n|iː|z|i|ə|,_|-|ʒ|ə}} {{Respell|IN|də|NEE|zee|ə|,_|-|zhə}} {{IPAc-en|US|audio=En-us-Indonesia.ogg|ˌ|ɪ|n|d|ə|ˈ|n|iː|ʒ|ə|,_|-|ʃ|ə}} {{Respell|IN|də|NEE|zhə|,_|-|shə}};<ref>{{Cite web |title=INDONESIA Definition & Meaning |url=https://www.dictionary.com/browse/Indonesia |access-date=7 May 2022 |website=Dictionary.com |language=en |archive-date=9 May 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220509110222/https://www.dictionary.com/browse/indonesia |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite Merriam-Webster|Indonesia|access-date=7 May 2022}}</ref> {{IPA|id|ɪndoˈnesia}}}} officially the '''Republic of Indonesia''',{{efn|name=fn1|{{lang|id|Republik Indonesia}} ({{IPA|id|reˈpublik ɪndoˈnesia||audio=Id-Indonesia.ogg}}) is the most used official name, though the name [[Names of Indonesia|Unitary State of the Republic of Indonesia]] ({{lang|id|Negara Kesatuan Republik Indonesia}}, NKRI) also appears in some official documents, including the constitution.<ref name="UUD45" />}} is a country in [[Southeast Asia]] and [[Oceania]], between the [[Indian Ocean|Indian]] and [[Pacific Ocean|Pacific]] oceans. Comprising between [[List of islands of Indonesia|13,000 and 17,000 islands]], including [[Sumatra]], [[Java]], [[Sulawesi]], and parts of [[Borneo]] and [[New Guinea]], Indonesia is the world's largest [[archipelagic state]] and the [[List of countries and dependencies by area|14th-largest country by area]], at {{convert|1904569|km2|sqmi|abbr=off}}. Indonesia has significant areas of wilderness that support one of the [[Megadiverse countries|world's highest levels of biodiversity]]. It shares [[Template:Borders of Indonesia|land borders]] with [[Papua New Guinea]], [[Timor-Leste]], and [[Malaysia]], as well as [[maritime border]]s with seven other countries, including [[Australia]], [[Singapore]], and the [[Philippines]].

The [[Nusantara (term)|Indonesian archipelago]] has been inhabited since prehistoric times, with early human presence evidenced by fossils of ''[[Homo erectus]]'' and ''[[Homo sapiens]]'', and megalithic sites. By the early second millennium, it had become a crossroads for international [[trade]] linking East and South Asia. Over the centuries, external influences—including [[Hinduism in Indonesia|Hinduism]], [[Buddhism in Indonesia|Buddhism]] and later [[Islam in Indonesia|Islam]]—were absorbed into local societies, which introduced lasting cultural and religious influences. European powers later competed to monopolise trade in the Spice Islands of [[Maluku Islands|Maluku]] during the [[Age of Discovery]], followed by three and a half centuries of [[Dutch East Indies#History|Dutch colonial rule]], before Indonesia [[Proclamation of Indonesian Independence|proclaimed its independence]] in the [[aftermath of World War II]]. 

Since independence, Indonesia has experienced [[separatism|separatist conflicts]], corruption, political upheaval and natural disasters, alongside [[Post-Suharto era in Indonesia|democratisation]] and rapid economic growth. The country today is a [[Presidential system|presidential republic]] with an elected [[People's Consultative Assembly|legislature]] and consists of [[Provinces of Indonesia|38 provinces]], some of which enjoy [[Autonomous administrative division|greater autonomy]] than others. Home to over 280 million people, Indonesia [[List of countries and dependencies by population|ranks fourth in the world by population]] and has the largest [[Islam by country|Muslim population of any country]]. More than half of Indonesians live on [[Java]], the [[List of islands by population|most heavily populated island]] in the world, while the capital [[Jakarta]] is the world's [[List of largest cities|most populous city]].

Indonesian society comprises hundreds of [[Ethnic groups in Indonesia|ethnic]] and [[Languages of Indonesia|linguistic]] groups, with [[Javanese people|Javanese]] forming the largest. National identity is unified under the motto ''[[Bhinneka Tunggal Ika]]'', reflected by a [[Indonesian language|national language]] alongside cultural and religious pluralism. A [[newly industrialised country]], Indonesia has the largest [[economy of Indonesia|national economy]] in Southeast Asia by GDP. The country is active in regional and global affairs as a [[middle power]] and is a member of major multilateral organisations, including the [[United Nations]], [[G20]], the [[Non-Aligned Movement]], [[ASEAN]], and the [[Organisation of Islamic Cooperation]].

== Etymology ==
{{Main|Names of Indonesia}}
The name ''Indonesia'' is usually explained as a compound of the [[Ancient Greek|Greek]] words {{lang|grc-Latn|[[wikt:Indos|Indos]]}} and {{lang|grc|[[wikt:νῆσος|nesos]]}}, together meaning {{gloss|Indian islands}}.<ref name="Tomascik">{{cite book |last1=Tomascik |first1=T. |title=The Ecology of the Indonesian Seas – Part One |last2=Mah |first2=A.J. |last3=Nontji |first3=A. |last4=Moosa |first4=M.K. |date=3 July 1997 |publisher=Oxford Academic |location=Oxford |page=1 |doi=10.1093/oso/9780198501855.001.0001}}</ref> The modern term emerged in the 19th century, well before the formation of independent Indonesia.<ref name="Elson">{{cite book |last=Elson |first=R.E. |url=https://www.cambridge.org/au/universitypress/subjects/history/twentieth-century-regional-history/idea-indonesia-history |title=The Idea of Indonesia: A History |publisher=Cambridge University Press |year=2008 |isbn=978-0-521-12108-8 |pages=1–3 |access-date=24 July 2026}}</ref> In 1850, [[George Windsor Earl]], an English [[ethnology|ethnologist]], proposed the terms ''Indunesians'' and ''Malayunesians'' for the inhabitants of the "Indian Archipelago or [[Malay Archipelago]]", preferring the latter because he considered ''Indunesians'' too general.<ref name="Earl">{{cite journal |last=Earl |first=G.W. |year=1850 |editor-last=Logan |editor-first=J.R. |title=On the Leading Characteristics of the Papuan, Australian, and Malayu-Polynesian Nations |url=https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.283376 |journal=The Journal of the Indian Archipelago and Eastern Asia |volume=IV |page=71 |access-date=5 October 2006 |via=Internet Archive}}</ref><ref name="Elson" /> In the same journal, [[James Richardson Logan]] adopted ''Indonesia'' as a geographical term for the ''Indian Archipelago''.<ref name="Logan">{{cite journal |last=Logan |first=J.R. |year=1850 |title=The Ethnology of the Indian Archipelago: Embracing Enquiries into the Continental Relations of the Indo-Pacific Islanders |url=https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/96568 |journal=Journal of the Indian Archipelago and Eastern Asia |volume=4 |page=254 |access-date=6 June 2026 |archive-date=9 July 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250709012651/https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/96568 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="Elson" />

Usage remained limited for several decades. German ethnographer [[Adolf Bastian]] from the [[Humboldt University of Berlin|University of Berlin]] gave the term greater scholarly currency by using it in the title of his five-volume work {{lang|de|Indonesien oder die Inseln des Malayischen Archipel|italic=yes}}, published from 1884 to 1894.<ref name="Elson" /> Dutch scholars nevertheless tended to use ''Malay Archipelago'' ({{langx|nl|Maleische Archipel}}) or the ''[[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands East Indies]]'' ({{lang|nl|Nederlandsch Oost Indië}}). Popular alternatives included {{lang|nl|Indië}}, {{lang|nl|de Oost}} ("the East"), and, less often, {{lang|nl|Insulinde}}.<ref name="Kroef">{{cite journal |last=van der Kroef |first=J.M. |year=1951 |title=The Term Indonesia: Its Origin and Usage |journal=Journal of the American Oriental Society |volume=71 |issue=3 |page=167–168 |doi=10.2307/595186 |jstor=595186}}</ref>

After 1900, ''Indonesia'' became increasingly common in academic and public usage, and Indonesian nationalist organisations adopted it as an expression of national unity and freedom from colonial rule.<ref name="Kroef" /> Among its early Indonesian promoters was [[Ki Hajar Dewantara|Soewardi Soerjaningrat]] (Ki Hajar Dewantara), who established the {{lang|nl|Indonesisch Persbureau}} in [[The Hague]] in November 1918, a press bureau that used ''Indonesia'' in its name.<ref name="Pranata">{{cite web |last1=Pranata |first1=G. |last2=Priyambodo |first2=U. |date=20 June 2024 |title=Berkat Soewardi, Nama Indonesia Bermula di Den Haag Sejak 1918 |trans-title=Thanks to Soewardi, the Name 'Indonesia' Took Root in The Hague in 1918 |url=https://nationalgeographic.grid.id/read/134107988/berkat-soewardi-nama-indonesia-bermula-di-den-haag-sejak-1918 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240620114215/https://nationalgeographic.grid.id/read/134107988/berkat-soewardi-nama-indonesia-bermula-di-den-haag-sejak-1918?page=all |archive-date=20 June 2024 |access-date=27 November 2025 |publisher=National Geographic Indonesia |language=id}}</ref>

== History ==

=== Prehistory, early states, and Islamisation ===

}}</ref>

Beginning in the third millennium BCE, Austronesian-speaking communities moved south from Taiwan through the Philippines and into the Indonesian archipelago, where related languages eventually spread across most of the islands.{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=6–7}}<ref>{{cite book |last1=Bellwood |first1=P. |url=https://www.jstor.org/content/oa_book_edited/j.ctt2jbjx1 |title=The Austronesians: Historical and Comparative Perspectives |last2=Fox |first2=J.J. |last3=Tryon |first3=D. |date=2006 |publisher=ANU Press |isbn=978-1-920942-85-4 |editor-last1=Bellwood |editor-first1=P. |series=Comparative Austronesian Series |pages=1, 4 |chapter=The Austronesians in History: Common Origins and Diverse Transformations |access-date=29 May 2026 |editor-last2=Fox |editor-first2=J.J. |editor-last3=Tryon |editor-first3=D. |archive-date=18 July 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260718090118/https://www.jstor.org/content/oa_book_edited/j.ctt2jbjx1 |url-status=live }}</ref> Settlement and subsistence patterns varied across the islands, with communities combining fishing, hunting, gathering, and cultivation.{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=7–9}} From around 500 BCE, regional trading networks increasingly connected the archipelago with [[South Asia|South]] and [[East Asia]].{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=10, 15–16}} Imported goods, knowledge, and technologies circulated through these networks, and during the early centuries CE some coastal communities in [[Borneo]], [[Sumatra]], and Java developed into trading ports.{{sfn |Hall |pp=39–40}}{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=15–18}} Indian merchants and religious specialists brought [[Sanskrit]] literacy, [[Hinduism in Indonesia|Hindu]] and [[Buddhism in Indonesia|Buddhist]] practices, and models of kingship that local rulers adopted.{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=18–22}}

[[Srivijaya]], founded in southeastern Sumatra in the late 7th century CE, grew as a maritime trading polity and a centre of Buddhist learning and religious networks.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Manguin |first=P-Y. |date=2021 |title=Srivijaya: Trade and Connectivity in the Pre-modern Malay World |journal=Journal of Urban Archaeology |publisher=Brepols Online |volume=3 |pages=87, 92–94 |doi=10.1484/J.JUA.5.123677}}</ref>{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=24–25}} In Central Java, rulers supported the construction of major Hindu and Buddhist monuments between the 8th and 10th centuries, including [[Borobudur]] and [[Prambanan]].<ref>{{cite book |last=Munandar |first=A.A. |url=https://directory.doabooks.org/handle/20.500.12854/159319 |title=Cultural Dynamics in a Globalized World |date=2017 |publisher=Taylor & Francis Group |isbn=978-1-315-22534-0 |pages=661–662, 665–666 |chapter=Ancient Religious Artworks in Central Java (8th–10th Century AD) |doi=10.1201/9781315225340 |access-date=21 July 2025 |archive-date=29 November 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251129002755/https://directory.doabooks.org/handle/20.500.12854/159319 |url-status=live }}</ref> [[Majapahit]] emerged in eastern Java in the late 13th century after [[Raden Wijaya]] used a [[Mongol invasion of Java|Mongol expedition]] to defeat his rivals and then forced the Mongols to withdraw.{{sfn |Hall |p=257}} Its rulers claimed suzerainty over a wide network of ports across the archipelago, although beyond Java this authority was often exercised indirectly through tributary, ritual, and commercial relationships rather than continuous territorial control.{{sfn |Hall |p=256–258}}

The earliest firm evidence of an Islamic kingdom in the archipelago comes from northern Sumatra. By the end of the 13th century, Muslim rule was established at Perlak and Samudra.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=4, 7}} Majapahit declined during the 15th century amid succession disputes and internal conflict.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=22–23}} By the early 16th century, [[Islam in Indonesia|Islam]] was established most firmly in ports connected to international trade. The [[Demak Sultanate|Sultanate of Demak]] had become the leading Muslim state on Java's north coast and expanded across much of the island.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=10–11, 41–44}} The last Hindu-Buddhist state in eastern Java fell around 1527, and other Muslim states developed in Java and elsewhere in the archipelago.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=41–46}} Later Javanese traditions associated early conversions with the ''[[Wali Sanga]]'',{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=8, 12–14}} and Islamic practice absorbed elements of older Javanese culture, setting Javanese Islam apart from the Islamic traditions of Malaya and Sumatra.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=8–9}}

=== European contact and colonial rule ===
{{Main|Dutch East India Company in Indonesia|Dutch East Indies}}
[[File:Nicolaas Pieneman - The Submission of Prince Dipo Negoro to General De Kock.jpg|thumb|An 1835 painting of the submission of Prince [[Diponegoro]] to [[Hendrik Merkus de Kock|General De Kock]] at the end of the [[Java War]] in 1830|alt=]]Before European arrival, spices from [[Maluku Islands|Maluku]] and the [[Banda Islands]] were already traded through Asian maritime networks by Javanese, Malay, and other merchants.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=27–29}} After [[Capture of Malacca (1511)|capturing Malacca in 1511]], Portuguese expeditions travelled east towards Maluku.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=26–27}} [[Dutch Republic|Dutch]] expeditions followed in the 1590s, and in 1602 competing Dutch companies merged to form the [[Dutch East India Company]] ({{lang|nl|Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie}}, VOC).{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=31}} Its charter authorised it to wage war, build fortresses, and conclude treaties in Asia.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=31}} The VOC made [[Batavia, Dutch East Indies|Batavia]] (present-day [[Jakarta]]) its headquarters in 1619, and the city grew rapidly as Indonesians and especially Chinese migrants arrived for commercial opportunities.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=35}} The VOC was dissolved in 1800, and its territorial possessions passed to the [[Batavian Republic|Dutch]] government.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=144}}

Dutch control expanded during the 19th and early 20th centuries and met prolonged resistance in [[Java War|Java]], [[Padri War|Sumatra]], [[Dutch intervention in Bali (1908)|Bali]], and [[Aceh War|Aceh]].{{sfn |Friend |2003 |p=21}}{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=176–179, 189}} In [[Western New Guinea|western New Guinea]], the Dutch established an outpost in 1828, abandoned it in 1836, and began permanent occupation only after 1898.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=178}} By about 1910, colonial campaigns had roughly established the boundaries of present-day Indonesia.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=189}} The [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands East Indies]] was governed through a combination of direct government and indirect rule by existing local authorities.{{sfn |Friend |2003 |pp=8, 21}}

From 1901, the [[Dutch Ethical Policy]] expanded education and helped create a small Indonesian elite.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=199–203}} Organisations such as [[Budi Utomo]], [[Sarekat Islam]], the [[Communist Party of Indonesia]] (PKI), and the [[Indonesian National Party]] (PNI) were formed. They aimed for social and political reform and independence.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=206–232}} Communist uprisings in 1926–27 and the growth of secular nationalism were followed by tighter colonial repression. [[Sukarno]], [[Mohammad Hatta]], [[Sutan Sjahrir]], and other leaders were imprisoned or exiled.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=227–244}}{{sfn |Friend |2003 |pp=23–26}} The [[Great Depression]] damaged the colony's export economy and caused hardship while open nationalist activity remained restricted during the 1930s.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=233–244}}

=== Emergence of Indonesia and independence ===
{{Main|Indonesian National Awakening|Indonesian National Revolution}}
[[File:Indonesische nationalisten - revolusi.jpg|thumb|Indonesian nationalists riding through the streets, November 1945|alt=]]
A shared national identity began developing through youth associations and a growing literature in [[Malay language|Malay]], which had long been a ''[[lingua franca]]'' that did not favour any single ethnic group.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=232–233}} Nationalists increasingly called the language ''[[Indonesian language|Bahasa Indonesia]]'' and made it a common medium for the emerging nation.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=232–233}} At a Youth Congress in Batavia in October 1928, delegates pledged allegiance to "one homeland" and "one nation", Indonesia, and to ''Bahasa Indonesia'' as the "language of unity".{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=233}} The [[Youth Pledge]] joined the movement's political and cultural aims and expressed a growing identification that crossed regional and religious lines.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=233–234}}

Dutch colonial rule ended when Japan [[Dutch East Indies campaign|conquered the colony in 1942]].{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=241}} The [[Japanese occupation of the Dutch East Indies|occupation]] brought forced labour, rice requisitioning, inflation, shortages, and famine,{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=249–250}} but Japanese authorities also mobilised Indonesians through mass, religious, and youth organisations.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=249–258}}{{sfn |Friend |2003 |pp=26–30}} Semi-military and military units, including [[Defenders of the Homeland|PETA]], trained tens of thousands of Indonesians,{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=255}} and the greater use of Indonesian in propaganda strengthened its position as the national language.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=250}} As Japan's defeat approached in 1945, they allowed Indonesian leaders to prepare for independence and draft a constitution.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=257–260}}

[[Surrender of Japan|Japan surrendered to the Allies]] on 15 August 1945, which created a power vacuum.{{sfn |Friend |2003 |pp=31–32}} Two days later, [[Sukarno]] and [[Mohammad Hatta]] issued the [[Proclamation of Indonesian Independence]], and became president and vice-president later that month.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=260–262}} Dutch attempts to restore colonial rule led to a four-year [[Indonesian National Revolution|military and diplomatic struggle]].{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=261–284}}{{sfn |Friend |2003 |pp=35–38}} Indonesian resistance, intervention by the [[United Nations]], and financial pressure from the [[United States]] forced the Netherlands into negotiations.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=283–284}} On 27 December 1949, the Netherlands transferred sovereignty, excluding western New Guinea, to the federal [[United States of Indonesia]], which was replaced by a unitary republic on 17 August 1950.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=284–285}}{{sfn |Friend |2003 |p=38}}

=== Sukarno era and Suharto's New Order ===
{{Main|Liberal democracy period in Indonesia|Guided Democracy in Indonesia|New Order (Indonesia)}}
{{multiple image
| direction = horizontal
| caption_align = center
| total_width = 260
| image1 = Presiden Sukarno (retouched).jpg
| image2 = Jenderal TNI Soeharto.png
| footer = [[Sukarno]] (''left'') and [[Suharto]] (''right''), Indonesia's first two [[President of Indonesia|presidents]]}}
After the federal system was dissolved, Indonesia adopted a [[Provisional Constitution of 1950|provisional constitution]] and parliamentary system.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=285, 295}} The political situation was unstable throughout the 1950s, and [[1955 Indonesian legislative election|elections in 1955]] failed to produce a durable governing majority.<ref>{{cite book |last=Sulistyo |first=H. |title=Electoral Politics in Southeast and East Asia |publisher=Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung |year=2002 |editor-last=Croissant |editor-first=A. |location=Singapore |pages=75–76 |chapter=Electoral Politics in Indonesia: A Hard Way to Democracy |access-date=25 July 2026 |chapter-url=https://library.fes.de/pdf-files/iez/01361004.pdf |archive-date=12 December 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251212075226/https://library.fes.de/pdf-files/iez/01361004.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref> Meanwhile, regional officers in Sumatra and Sulawesi [[Permesta|rebelled against Jakarta]] as they demanded autonomy and challenged Sukarno's centralising rule.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=308–319}} When the [[Constitutional Assembly of Indonesia|Constituent Assembly]] failed to agree on a permanent constitution,{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=321}} Sukarno [[President Sukarno's 1959 Decree|dissolved it by decree]] in 1959 and restored the 1945 Constitution.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=322–323}} The change began the system known as [[Guided Democracy in Indonesia|Guided Democracy]].{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=322–323}}

The new system concentrated authority in Sukarno, who balanced the military against the growing PKI while restricting party politics.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=322–341}} His campaign against Dutch rule in [[Western New Guinea|western New Guinea]] led to the [[New York Agreement]] in 1962 and Indonesian administration of the territory in 1963.<ref>{{cite web |date=15 August 1962 |title=Agreement between the Republic of Indonesia and the Kingdom of the Netherlands Concerning West New Guinea (New York Agreement) |url=https://peacemaker.un.org/indonesianetherlands-westguinea62 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220126215746/https://peacemaker.un.org/indonesianetherlands-westguinea62 |archive-date=26 January 2022 |access-date=25 July 2026 |website=UN Peacemaker |publisher=United Nations Department of Political and Peacebuilding Affairs}}</ref>{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=328}} After the [[30 September Movement]] killed six generals in 1965, the army blamed the PKI and launched an [[Indonesian mass killings of 1965–66|anti-communist campaign]] with allied civilian groups.{{sfn |Melvin |2018 |p=1}}<ref name="Farid">{{cite journal |last=Farid |first=H. |year=2005 |title=Indonesia's original sin: mass killings and capitalist expansion, 1965–66 |journal=Inter-Asia Cultural Studies |volume=6 |issue=1 |pages=3–16 |doi=10.1080/1462394042000326879}}</ref> At least 500,000 people were killed and an additional hundreds of thousands detained.{{efn|Estimates range from about 200,000 to one million; at least 500,000 is the widely accepted figure.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Cribb |first=R. |year=2002 |title=Unresolved Problems in the Indonesian Killings of 1965–1966 |journal=Asian Survey |volume=42 |issue=4 |pages=550–563 |doi=10.1525/as.2002.42.4.550}}</ref>{{sfn |Robinson |2018 |pp=3}}{{sfn |Bevins |2020 |pp=237-238}}}}<ref name="Farid" /><ref>{{cite web |date=17 October 2017 |title=Indonesia massacres: Declassified US files shed new light |url=https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-41651047 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171118112221/https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-41651047 |archive-date=18 November 2017 |access-date=19 September 2018 |publisher=BBC}}</ref> The campaign destroyed the PKI and shifted power to the army.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Legge |first=John D. |year=1968 |title=General Suharto's New Order |journal=International Affairs |volume=44 |issue=1 |pages=40–47 |doi=10.2307/2613527 |jstor=2613527}}</ref> The ''[[Supersemar]]'' order in 1966 gave army general [[Suharto]] ''de facto'' control over key executive and security powers, an effective transfer of power from Sukarno.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=349}}

Suharto's [[New Order (Indonesia)|New Order]] centralised political and military power, restricted opposition, and used engineered elections to maintain power.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=342–407}} The [[Act of Free Choice]] in 1969 formalised Indonesian rule in western New Guinea but this was opposed by many Papuans, and [[Papua conflict|armed resistance]] has continued since then.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Viartasiwi |first=N. |year=2018 |title=The politics of history in West Papua–Indonesia conflict |journal=Asian Journal of Political Science |volume=26 |issue=1 |pages=141–159 |doi=10.1080/02185377.2018.1445535}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last=Chauvel |first=R. |year=2021 |title=West Papua: Indonesia's last regional conflict |journal=Small Wars & Insurgencies |volume=32 |issue=6 |pages=913–944 |doi=10.1080/09592318.2021.1990491}}</ref> In 1975, Indonesia [[Indonesian invasion of East Timor|invaded former Portuguese colony East Timor]] and [[East Timor (province)|annexed it]] the following year.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=369–370}} In Aceh, [[Hasan di Tiro]] founded the [[Free Aceh Movement]] (GAM) in 1976 and declared independence from Indonesia. The [[Insurgency in Aceh|insurgency]] was largely suppressed by 1979, but it revived in 1989 amid the return of fighters trained in [[Libya]], which prompted a decade-long campaign from the Indonesian military.{{sfn |Barron |2013 |pp=10–11}}

Economic liberalisation, foreign investment, and export-oriented industrialisation contributed to sustained growth, but corporate ownership and wealth became highly concentrated among a small number of family-controlled conglomerates.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Hill |first1=H. |url=https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/p687/html/ch04.xhtml?referer=&page=10#toc-anchor |title=Soeharto's New Order and Its Legacy: Essays in honour of Harold Crouch |last2=Narjoko |first2=D. |date=2010 |publisher=ANU Press |isbn=978-1-921666-47-6 |series=Asian Studies Series |volume=2 |chapter=Managing Industrialisation in a Globalising Economy: Lessons from the Soeharto Era |access-date=29 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180323203150/https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/p687/html/ch04.xhtml?referer=&page=10#toc-anchor |archive-date=23 March 2018 |url-status=live}}</ref> The [[1997 Asian financial crisis|Asian financial crisis in 1997–98]] caused sharp economic downturn and [[May 1998 Indonesia riots|unrest]] in several cities, including Jakarta, [[Medan]], and [[Surakarta]], and culminated in [[Fall of Suharto|Suharto's resignation in May 1998]].{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=399–407}}<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Pincus |first1=J. |last2=Ramli |first2=R. |year=1998 |title=Indonesia: from showcase to basket case |journal=Cambridge Journal of Economics |volume=22 |issue=6 |pages=723–734 |doi=10.1093/cje/22.6.723}}</ref> East Timor later voted for independence in 1999{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |p=413}} and became an independent country in 2002.<ref>{{cite web |date=20 May 2002 |title=East Timor celebrates becoming a nation |url=https://www.theguardian.com/world/2002/may/20/indonesia.easttimor2 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130826033554/https://www.theguardian.com/world/2002/may/20/indonesia.easttimor2 |archive-date=26 August 2013 |access-date=25 July 2026 |publisher=The Guardian}}</ref>

=== ''Reformasi'' era and contemporary Indonesia===
{{Main|Reform era in Indonesia}}
[[File:US Navy 050101-N-1229B-269 An aerial view of Banda Ache from an SH-60F Seahawk assigned to Helicopter Anti-Submarine Squadron Two (HS-2) Golden Falcons.jpg|thumb|Damage following the [[2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami|earthquake and subsequent tsunami]] in [[Banda Aceh]], January 2005]]
The [[Reform era in Indonesia|period after Suharto]] brought constitutional amendments, competitive elections, and decentralisation.<ref name="Butt">{{cite journal |last=Butt |first=S. |year=2010 |title=Regional Autonomy and Legal Disorder: The Proliferation of Local Laws in Indonesia |journal=Singapore Journal of Legal Studies |pages=1–21 |jstor=24870542}}</ref><ref name="Dagg">{{cite journal |last=Dagg |first=Christopher J. |year=2007 |title=The 2004 elections in Indonesia: Political reform and democratisation |journal=Asia Pacific Viewpoint |volume=48 |issue=1 |pages=47–59 |doi=10.1111/j.1467-8373.2007.00329.x}}</ref> The military lost its reserved parliamentary seats and the police became a separate service, although military influence did not disappear.<ref name="Nalle">{{cite journal |last=Nalle |first=Victor I. W. |year=2026 |title=Legislating civil–military relations in post-Reformasi Indonesia |journal=Global Change, Peace & Security |volume=37 |issue=2 |pages=125–142 |doi=10.1080/14781158.2025.2611247}}</ref> Democratic institutions were established, but patronage and influence of oligarchy remained.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Fukuoka |first=Y. |year=2014 |title=Debating Indonesia's Reformasi: Bridging "Parallel Universes" |journal=Journal of Contemporary Asia |volume=44 |issue=3 |pages=540–552 |doi=10.1080/00472336.2014.895026}}</ref> Former general [[Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono]] won the first direct [[2004 Indonesian presidential election|presidential election in 2004]].<ref name="Dagg" /> His administration reached a [[Helsinki Agreement|peace agreement]] with [[Insurgency in Aceh|Aceh separatists]] in 2005 after the previous year's [[2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami|Indian Ocean tsunami]] brought more international attention to the province.<ref name="Parks">{{citation |last1=Barron |first1=P. |title=The Contested Corners of Asia: Subnational Conflict and International Development Assistance |date=7 October 2013 |url=https://asiafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/The-Contested-Corners-of-Asia_The-Case-of-Aceh.pdf |access-date=29 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211022203107/https://asiafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/The-Contested-Corners-of-Asia_The-Case-of-Aceh.pdf |archive-date=22 October 2021 |url-status=live |chapter=The Case of Aceh, Indonesia |publisher=The Asia Foundation |isbn=978-616-91408-1-8 |display-authors=etal}}</ref> It also weakened jihadist networks following [[Terrorism in Indonesia|a series of bombings]].<ref>{{cite report |title=Terrorism in Perspective: An Assessment of 'Jihad Project' Trends in Indonesia |last=Hwang |first=Julie C. |year=2012 |publisher=East-West Center |pages=1–2 |jstor=resrep06463 |number=104}}</ref>

[[Joko Widodo]], elected president in 2014, made infrastructure, social programmes, and state-led development central to his presidency.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Aspinall |first1=E. |last2=Mietzner |first2=M. |year=2014 |title=Indonesian Politics in 2014: Democracy's Close Call |journal=Bulletin of Indonesian Economic Studies |volume=50 |issue=3 |pages=347–369 |doi=10.1080/00074918.2014.980375}}</ref><ref name="Mietzner1">{{cite journal |last=Mietzner |first=M. |year=2025 |title=Flirting with Autocracy in Indonesia: Jokowi's Majoritarianism and its Democratic Legacy |journal=Journal of Current Southeast Asian Affairs |volume=44 |issue=3 |pages=366–384 |doi=10.1177/18681034251318053}}</ref> Electoral competition continued, but the lack of parliamentary opposition,<ref name="Wadipalapa" /> and changes to the [[Corruption Eradication Commission|anti-corruption commission]], limit on dissent, and interference in the 2024 election reduced the quality of democracy.<ref name="Mietzner1" /> Widodo backed [[Prabowo Subianto]] and his son [[Gibran Rakabuming Raka]], whose ticket won the [[2024 Indonesian presidential election|2024 election]].<ref name="Wadipalapa">{{cite journal |last1=Wadipalapa |first1=R. |last2=Nainggolan |first2=P. |last3=Katharina |first3=R. |year=2026 |title=Protecting two presidents: legislative decline in Indonesia's post-2024 election transition |journal=The Journal of Legislative Studies |volume=32 |issue=2 |pages=263–280 |doi=10.1080/13572334.2025.2484488}}</ref> Under Prabowo, the government pursued a fiscally expansive programme,<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Wihardja |first1=Maria M. |last2=Ikhsan |first2=M. |last3=Alatas |first3=V. |year=2025 |title=Times Are Changing: Can Indonesia Stay the Course? |journal=Bulletin of Indonesian Economic Studies |volume=61 |issue=2 |pages=169–204 |doi=10.1080/00074918.2025.2526824}}</ref> while new laws permitted a larger cabinet<ref name="Wadipalapa" /> and a 2025 revision of the military law allowed more active officers to serve in civilian posts.<ref name="Nalle" />

== Geography ==
{{Main|Geography of Indonesia|List of islands of Indonesia}}
[[File:Lake Toba and the surrounding hills.jpg|thumb|[[Lake Toba]] in [[North Sumatra]], the world's largest known [[Cenozoic]] [[caldera]].<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Koulakov |first1=I. |display-authors=etal |date=1 June 2009 |title=P, S velocity and VP/VS ratio beneath the Toba caldera complex (Northern Sumatra) from local earthquake tomography |journal=Geophysical Journal International |volume=177 |issue=3 |pages=1121–1139 |doi=10.1111/j.1365-246X.2009.04114.x |doi-access=free}}</ref> Indonesia lies within one of the most tectonically active regions on Earth.<ref name="Cummins">{{cite journal |last=Cummins |first=P.R. |year=2017 |title=Geohazards in Indonesia: Earth science for disaster risk reduction – introduction |url=https://www.lyellcollection.org/doi/full/10.1144/sp441.11 |journal=Geological Society, London, Special Publications |publisher=The Geological Society of London |volume=441 |issue=1 |pages=1–7 |doi=10.1144/SP441.11 |doi-access=free}}</ref>]]
Indonesia is an equatorial archipelago of coastal plains, mountains, and volcanic islands. It lies between latitudes [[11th parallel south|11°S]] and [[6th parallel north|6°N]] and longitudes [[95th meridian east|95°E]] and [[141st meridian east|141°E]],<ref name="Paramita" /> and is the world's largest [[archipelagic state]], extending {{convert|5120|km|0}} from east to west and {{convert|1760|km|0}} from north to south.{{sfn |Frederick |Worden |2011 |pp=98}} Several straits, including [[Malacca Strait|Malacca]], [[Sunda Strait|Sunda]], and [[Lombok Strait|Lombok]], carry international traffic between the [[Indian Ocean|Indian]] and [[Pacific Ocean]]s, including ships transporting oil, gas, and industrial goods.<ref name="Oegroseno">{{cite book |last1=Oegroseno |first1=A.H. |url=https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/indonesia-beyond-the-waters-edge/indonesias-maritime-boundaries/C950A6EAE31A6E58B0B61F94BC60FD8B |title=Indonesia beyond the Water's Edge: Managing an Archipelagic State |date=21 October 2015 |publisher=ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute |editor-last1=Cribb |editor-first1=R. |pages=49–58 |chapter=Indonesia's Maritime Boundaries |access-date=29 May 2026 |editor-last2=Ford |editor-first2=M. |archive-date=25 June 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250625165358/https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/indonesia-beyond-the-waters-edge/indonesias-maritime-boundaries/C950A6EAE31A6E58B0B61F94BC60FD8B |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Scott |first1=D. |date=1 August 2019 |title=Indonesia Grapples with the Indo-Pacific: Outreach, Strategic Discourse, and Diplomacy |journal=Journal of Current Southeast Asian Affairs |volume=38 |issue=2 |pages=194–217 |doi=10.1177/1868103419860669}}</ref>

The number of Indonesian islands varies by counting method, with published figures ranging from 13,466 named islands to more than 17,500. About 922 are permanently inhabited.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Sukendra |first1=M. |date=December 2017 |title=The Analysis of Geospatial Information for Validating Some Numbers of Islands in Indonesia |journal=Indonesian Journal of Geography |volume=49 |pages=204–211 |doi=10.22146/ijg.12792 |number=2}}</ref><ref name="CIA">{{Cite CIA World Factbook|country=Indonesia|date=3 December 2025|access-date=5 December 2025}}</ref> Its five main islands are [[Sumatra]], [[Java]], [[Sulawesi]], [[Borneo]], and [[New Guinea]].{{sfn |Frederick |Worden |2011 |p=99}} Indonesia occupies most of Borneo, where it is known as [[Kalimantan]], and the western part of New Guinea, commonly called [[Western New Guinea|Papua]]. The remainder of those islands is shared with [[Brunei]] and [[Malaysia]], and with [[Papua New Guinea]], respectively.{{sfn |Frederick |Worden |2011 |p=99}}

Java accounts for less than 7% of Indonesia's land area but is its most populous island, with intensive farming and extensive urban growth.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=de Koninck |first1=R. |last2=Hai |first2=P.T. |date=29 September 2025 |title=Coping with density: reflections on Java (1960-2010) |journal=Singapore Journal of Tropical Geography |publisher=Wiley Online Library |volume=46 |issue=3 |pages=371–385 |bibcode=2025SJTG...46..371D |doi=10.1111/sjtg.70025}}</ref> Forest cover also differs widely, with larger forested areas in Papua and Maluku than in Java and Bali.<ref name="Santoro">{{cite journal |last1=Santoro |first1=A. |last2=Piras |first2=F. |last3=Yu |first3=Q. |date=17 July 2023 |title=Spatial analysis of deforestation in Indonesia in the period 1950–2017 and the role of protected areas |journal=Biodiversity and Conservation |publisher=Springer Nature Link |volume=34 |issue=9 |pages=3119–3145 |doi=10.1007/s10531-023-02679-8 |hdl=2158/1323316 |hdl-access=free}}</ref> Indonesia's terrain includes high peaks, volcanic lakes, major rivers, and coastal plains. At {{convert|4884|m|ft}}, [[Puncak Jaya]] on New Guinea is Indonesia's highest peak,<ref>{{cite web |title=Lorentz National Park |url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/955/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260128085712/https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/955/ |archive-date=28 January 2026 |access-date=4 January 2026 |publisher=UNESCO World Heritage Centre}}</ref> while [[Lake Toba]] on [[Sumatra]] is its largest lake.<ref>{{cite web |title=Toba Caldera UNESCO Global Geoparks |url=https://en.unesco.org/global-geoparks/toba-caldera |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240313102925/https://en.unesco.org/global-geoparks/toba-caldera |archive-date=13 March 2024 |access-date=13 March 2024 |publisher=UNESCO Global Geoparks}}</ref> In [[Kalimantan]], the [[Kapuas River|Kapuas]], [[Barito River|Barito]], and [[Mahakam River|Mahakam]] form river networks that have long linked coastal and inland settlements.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Lubis |first1=M.S. |last2=Susanto |first2=D. |last3=Harjoko |first3=T.Y. |date=25 October 2021 |title=Understanding Riverine Urbanism in Kalimantan through Diachronic Approach: Case Study of Lanting Settlements in Sintang, Indonesia |journal=Journal of Architectural Design and Urbanism |publisher=ISOMAse |volume=4 |pages=12–26 |doi=10.14710/jadu.v4i1.12133 |doi-access=free |number=1}}</ref>

Indonesia occupies a complex plate-boundary zone where the [[Eurasian plate|Eurasian]], [[Indo-Australian plate|India-Australia]], and [[Philippine Sea plate|Pacific-Philippine sea]] [[plate tectonics|tectonic plates]] interact, with subduction zones along these boundaries producing frequent earthquakes and volcanic activity.<ref name="HallR">{{cite encyclopedia |year=2009 |title=Indonesia, Geology |encyclopedia=Encyclopedia of Islands |publisher=University of California Press |url=https://earthjay.com/earthquakes/20190714_halmahera/hall_2009_geology_Indonesia_Islands.pdf |last=Hall |first=R. |editor-last1=Gillespie |editor-first1=R.G. |pages=454–460 |doi=10.1525/9780520943728-104 |editor-last2=Clague |editor-first2=D.A. |access-date=3 June 2026 |archive-date=16 April 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240416020628/https://earthjay.com/earthquakes/20190714_halmahera/hall_2009_geology_Indonesia_Islands.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="Cummins" /> The resulting volcanic belt contains roughly 400 volcanoes, around 130 of which are considered active.<ref name="Hidayat">{{cite journal |last1=Hidayat |first1=A. |display-authors=etal |date=2020 |title=Eruption on Indonesia's volcanic islands: a review of potential hazards, fatalities, and management |journal=IOP Conference Series: Earth and Environmental Science |publisher=IOP Publishing Ltd |volume=485 |issue=1 |article-number=012061 |bibcode=2020E&ES..485a2061H |doi=10.1088/1755-1315/485/1/012061}}</ref> This tectonic setting also exposes the archipelago to major earthquake and tsunami hazards. The [[2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami|Sumatra earthquake in 2004]] generated a tsunami that caused widespread destruction in [[Aceh]].<ref name="Putra">{{cite journal |last1=Putra |first1=R.R. |display-authors=etal |date=19 October 2012 |title=Seismic Hazard Analysis for Indonesia |journal=Journal of Natural Disaster Science |volume=33 |issue=2 |pages=59–70, 62 |doi=10.2328/jnds.33.59}}</ref> The archipelago has also experienced eruptions of exceptional magnitude. No larger explosive event is known from the past 2.5 million years than the [[Youngest Toba eruption|Toba super-eruption]] about 74,000 years ago,<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Ge |first1=Y. |last2=Gao |first2=X. |year=2020 |title=Understanding the overestimated impact of the Toba volcanic super-eruption on global environments and ancient hominins |journal=Quaternary International |volume=559 |pages=24–33 |doi=10.1016/j.quaint.2020.06.021}}</ref> and the [[1815 eruption of Mount Tambora|eruption of Tambora]] in 1815 was followed by global cooling and the [[Year Without a Summer]] in 1816.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Gertisser |first1=R. |last2=Self |first2=S. |year=2015 |title=The great 1815 eruption of Tambora and future risks from large-scale volcanism |journal=Geology Today |volume=31 |issue=4 |pages=132–136 |doi=10.1111/gto.12099}}</ref>

=== Climate ===
{{Main|Climate of Indonesia|Climate change in Indonesia}}
[[File:Koppen-Geiger Map IDN present.svg|thumb|upright=1.2|[[Köppen climate classification|Köppen-Geiger climate classification]] map of Indonesia<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Beck |first1=Hylke E. |last2=Zimmermann |first2=Niklaus E. |last3=McVicar |first3=Tim R. |last4=Vergopolan |first4=Noemi |last5=Berg |first5=Alexis |last6=Wood |first6=Eric F. |author6-link=Eric Franklin Wood |date=30 October 2018 |title=Present and future Köppen-Geiger climate classification maps at 1-km resolution |journal=Scientific Data |volume=5 |article-number=180214 |bibcode=2018NatSD...580214B |doi=10.1038/sdata.2018.214 |pmc=6207062 |pmid=30375988 |doi-access=free}}</ref>|alt=]]
Indonesia's equatorial position and [[monsoon|monsoon winds]] give it a tropical climate. Most lowland areas are warm and humid throughout the year, while higher terrain is cooler and seasonal temperature differences are small.<ref name="ClimateRiskProfile">{{cite report |url=https://climateknowledgeportal.worldbank.org/sites/default/files/2021-05/15504-Indonesia%20Country%20Profile-WEB_0.pdf |title=Climate Risk Profile: Indonesia (2021) |publisher=The World Bank Group and Asian Development Bank |access-date=5 August 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210525174717/https://climateknowledgeportal.worldbank.org/sites/default/files/2021-05/15504-Indonesia%20Country%20Profile-WEB_0.pdf |archive-date=25 May 2021 |url-status=live |work=Climate Change Knowledge Portal}}</ref> Seasonal changes in the monsoon generally bring wetter conditions from November to April and drier conditions from May to October, but their timing and intensity vary by region.<ref name="ClimateRiskProfile" /><ref name="Assyakur">{{cite journal |last1=As-syakur |first1=A.R. |display-authors=etal |date=2 September 2013 |title=Indonesian rainfall variability observation using TRMM multi-satellite data |journal=International Journal of Remote Sensing |publisher=Taylor & Francis Online |volume=34 |issue=21 |pages=7723–7738 |bibcode=2013IJRS...34.7723A |doi=10.1080/01431161.2013.826837}}</ref> Much of the country has a [[Tropical rainforest climate|tropical rainforest climate]], with tropical monsoon and savanna climates across parts of the islands.<ref name="Paramita">{{cite book |last1=Paramita |first1=B. |title=Climate Change and Cooling Cities |last2=Matzarakis |first2=A. |date=1 September 2023 |publisher=Springer, Singapore |isbn=978-981-99-3675-5 |pages=283–296 |chapter=Urban Biometeorology of Tropical Climate: Af, Am, Aw, a Propensity of 34 Provincial Cities in Indonesia |doi=10.1007/978-981-99-3675-5_16}}</ref> Seasonal rainfall helps set rice-planting calendars and influences droughts, floods, and fires.<ref name="Naylor">{{cite journal |last1=Naylor |first1=R.L. |display-authors=etal |date=8 May 2007 |title=Assessing risks of climate variability and climate change for Indonesian rice agriculture |journal=Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences |volume=104 |issue=19 |pages=7752–7753 |bibcode=2007PNAS..104.7752N |doi=10.1073/pnas.0701825104 |pmc=1876519 |pmid=17483453 |doi-access=free}}</ref><ref name="Karuniasa">{{cite journal |last1=Karuniasa |first1=M. |last2=Pambudi |first2=P.A. |year=2022 |title=The analysis of the El Niño phenomenon in the East Nusa Tenggara Province, Indonesia |journal=Journal of Water and Land Development |publisher=Polish Academy of Sciences |pages=180–185 |doi=10.24425/jwld.2022.140388 |number=52}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Spessa |first1=A.C. |display-authors=etal |date=6 March 2015 |title=Seasonal forecasting of fire over Kalimantan, Indonesia |journal=Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences |publisher=European Geosciences Union |volume=15 |issue=3 |pages=429–442 |bibcode=2015NHESS..15..429S |doi=10.5194/nhess-15-429-2015 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

Rainfall varies across the archipelago. Western Sumatra, Java, and the interiors of Kalimantan and Papua are among the wetter areas, while regions nearer [[Australia]], including [[Lesser Sunda Islands|Nusa Tenggara]], are generally drier.<ref name="Zaini">{{cite journal |last1=Zaini |first1=A.Z.A. |last2=Vonnisa |first2=M. |last3=Marzuki |first3=M. |last4=R. |first4=Ramadhan |date=25 November 2023 |title=Seasonal Variation of Rainfall in Indonesia under Normal Conditions without ENSO and IOD Events from 1981-2021 |journal=Journal of Research in Science Education |publisher=Postgraduate University of Mataram |volume=9 |pages=9899–9909 |doi=10.29303/jppipa.v9i11.4569 |number=11}}</ref> The surrounding oceans, island geography, monsoon winds, and topography all affect where and when rain falls.<ref name="Assyakur" /> In drier parts of the country, [[El Niño–Southern Oscillation|El Niño]] events can reduce rainfall and lengthen dry periods, causing water shortages and crop losses.<ref name="Karuniasa" /><ref name="Naylor" />

Indonesia is expected to face rising temperatures, changes in rainfall, higher sea levels, and greater exposure to heat and flooding.<ref name="ClimateRiskProfile" /><ref>{{cite report |url=https://files.cmcc.it/g20climaterisks/Indonesia.pdf |title=G20 Climate Risk Atlas. Impacts, policy and economics in the G20 |last1=Mercogliano |first1=P. |last2=Reder |first2=A. |last3=Ellena |first3=M. |year=2021 |doi=10.25424/cmcc/g20_climaterisk |access-date=25 January 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220527113556/https://files.cmcc.it/g20climaterisks/Indonesia.pdf |archive-date=27 May 2022 |url-status=live |display-authors=etal}}</ref> These changes affect agriculture, water supply, public health, coastal settlements, and wildfire management.<ref name="ClimateRiskProfile" /><ref name="Case">{{cite web |date=14 November 2007 |title=Climate Change in Indonesia: Implications for Humans and Nature |url=http://awsassets.panda.org/downloads/inodesian_climate_change_impacts_report_14nov07.pdf |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180219103237/http://awsassets.panda.org/downloads/inodesian_climate_change_impacts_report_14nov07.pdf |archive-date=19 February 2018 |access-date=18 November 2018 |publisher=WWF |vauthors=Case M, Ardiansyah F, Spector E}}</ref> [[Sea level rise|Sea-level rise]] is especially relevant to coastal areas, where much of Indonesia's population and infrastructure is located.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Neumann |first1=B. |display-authors=etal |date=11 March 2015 |title=Future Coastal Population Growth and Exposure to Sea-Level Rise and Coastal Flooding - A Global Assessment |journal=PLOS ONE |volume=10 |article-number=e0118571 |doi=10.1371/journal.pone.0118571 |pmc=4367969 |pmid=25760037 |doi-access=free |number=3}}</ref><ref name="ClimateRiskProfile" /> Poorer households and communities with weaker infrastructure are likely to have less capacity to adapt.<ref>{{cite report |url=https://smeru.or.id/sites/default/files/publication/climatechangecbms.pdf |title=Impact of Climate Change on Households in the Indonesian CBMS Area |last1=Wahyu |first1=Y.F.M. |date=November 2012 |publisher=The SMERU Research Institute |pages=1, 3-4, 9 |access-date=28 April 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170329060830/https://smeru.or.id/sites/default/files/publication/climatechangecbms.pdf |archive-date=29 March 2017 |url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Biodiversity and conservation ===
{{Main|Fauna of Indonesia|Flora of Indonesia|Environment of Indonesia|Conservation in Indonesia}}
{{See also|Protected areas of Indonesia|List of national parks of Indonesia|List of biosphere reserves of Indonesia}}
<!----Galleries of images are generally discouraged in summary articles as they cause undue weight to one particular section and may cause accessibility problems.--->
[[File:202306 Varanus komodoensis.jpg|thumb|The [[Komodo dragon]] (''Varanus komodoensis'') is one of Indonesia's officially designated national animals under a presidential decree in 1993.<ref>{{cite act|title=Keputusan Presiden (Keppres) Nomor 4 Tahun 1993 tentang Satwa dan Bunga Nasional|trans-title=Presidential Decree Number 4 of 1993 concerning National Animals and Flowers|type=Presidential Decree|date=1993|institution=[[President of Indonesia|President of the Republic of Indonesia]]|via=[[Audit Board of Indonesia]]'s Legal Documentation and Information Network}}</ref>]]

Indonesia is among the 17 countries identified as [[megadiverse countries|megadiverse]] by [[Conservation International]]. Its many islands and geological past have helped produce a highly diverse flora and fauna.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.cbd.int/countries/profile?country=id|title=Indonesia - Country Profile|publisher=Convention on Biological Diversity|access-date=23 July 2025|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250519002820/https://www.cbd.int/countries/profile?country=id|archive-date=19 May 2025}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal|last1=von Rintelen|first1=K.|display-authors=etal|title=A review of biodiversity-related issues and challenges in megadiverse Indonesia and other Southeast Asian countries|journal=Research Ideas and Outcomes|date=11 September 2017|volume=3|article-number=e20860|doi=10.3897/rio.3.e20860|doi-access=free}}</ref> Within the archipelago, [[Wallacea]] is a transition zone between Asian and Australian fauna and one of the world's largest centres of endemism.<ref>{{cite journal|url=https://academic.oup.com/bioscience/article/72/11/1118/6763234|last1=Struebig|first1=M.J.|display-authors=etal|title=Safeguarding Imperiled Biodiversity and Evolutionary Processes in the Wallacea Center of Endemism|journal=BioScience|date=November 2022|volume=72|issue=11|pages=1118–1130|doi=10.1093/biosci/biac085|doi-access=free|access-date=29 May 2026}}</ref> In 2015, an estimated 83% of Southeast Asia's old-growth forest was in Indonesia.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Estoque|first1=R.C.|last2=Ooba|first2=M.|last3=Avitabile|first3=V.|last4=Hijioka|first4=Y.|last5=DasGupta|first5=R.|last6=Togawa|first6=T.|last7=Murayama|first7=Y.|date=23 April 2019|title=The future of Southeast Asia's forests|journal=Nature Communications|language=en|volume=10|issue=1|page=1829|doi=10.1038/s41467-019-09646-4|doi-access=free|pmid=31015425|pmc=6478739|bibcode=2019NatCo..10.1829E}}</ref> Mangrove forests, coral reefs and seagrass beds are among Indonesia's coastal ecosystems.<ref>{{cite journal|url=https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/299010.pdf|last1=Hutomo|first1=M.|last2=Moosa|first2=M.K.|title=Indonesian marine and coastal biodiversity: Present status|journal=Indian Journal of Marine Sciences|date=March 2005|volume=34|issue=1|pages=88–97|access-date=6 May 2026|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250623000746/https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/299010.pdf|archive-date=23 June 2025|url-status=live}}</ref>

Large [[Palm oil production in Indonesia|oil-palm]] and timber plantations were estimated to account for more than two-fifths of national deforestation between 2001 and 2016.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Austin|first1=K.G.|last2=Schwantes|first2=A.|last3=Gu|first3=Y.|last4=Kasibhatla|first4=P.D.|date=1 February 2019|title=What causes deforestation in Indonesia?|journal=Environmental Research Letters|volume=14|issue=2|page=024007|doi=10.1088/1748-9326/aaf6db|bibcode=2019ERL....14b4007A|doi-access=free}}</ref> Land-use change also causes forest fragmentation, with agricultural expansion and infrastructure development among the pressures associated with habitat destruction and other effects on biodiversity.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Gunawan|first1=H.|display-authors=etal|title=A review of forest fragmentation in Indonesia under the DPSIR framework for biodiversity conservation strategies|journal=Global Ecology and Conservation|volume=51|date=June 2024|article-number=e02918|doi=10.1016/j.gecco.2024.e02918|doi-access=free|bibcode=2024GEcoC..5102918G}}</ref> Peat-swamp forests are important carbon stores and habitat for threatened taxa, but are especially vulnerable to logging, fire and land conversion.<ref>{{cite journal|url=https://academic.oup.com/bioscience/article-abstract/61/1/49/304606|last1=Posa|first1=M.R.C.|display-authors=etal|date=1 January 2011|access-date=28 April 2026|title=Biodiversity and Conservation of Tropical Peat Swamp Forests|journal=BioScience|volume=61|issue=1|pages=49–57|doi=10.1525/bio.2011.61.1.10|bibcode=2011BiSci..61...49P}}</ref> Surveys of 15 reefs in three regions found lower coral diversity where reefs were exposed to land-based pollution and anchor damage.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Edinger|first1=E.N.|display-authors=etal|date=August 1998|title=Reef degradation and coral biodiversity in indonesia: Effects of land-based pollution, destructive fishing practices and changes over time|journal=Marine Pollution Bulletin|pages=617–630|volume=36|issue=8|doi=10.1016/S0025-326X(98)00047-2|bibcode=1998MarPB..36..617E}}</ref>

Indonesia has designated 554 protected areas covering 27 million hectares, equivalent to 14% of its land area.<ref name="Nugraha">{{cite journal|last1=Nugraha|first1=R.T.|display-authors=etal|title=Evaluating the effectiveness of protected area management in Indonesia|journal=Oryx|publisher=Cambridge University Press|date=18 March 2024|volume=58|issue=4|pages=474–484|doi=10.1017/S003060532300145X|doi-access=free}}</ref> Assessments conducted in 2015, 2017 and 2019 found improved management scores, but identified poaching, illegal logging, human settlements, tourism and non-timber cultivation as the main threats to protected areas.<ref name="Nugraha" /> In Papua, research around the [[Cyclops Mountains]] protected area found limited consultation with nearby communities and implementation problems linked to scarce resources.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Barnes|first1=P.A.|display-authors=etal|title=The gap between policy and practice for human rights in conservation: a case study in Papua Province, Indonesia|journal=Oryx|date=9 May 2023|volume=57|issue=3|pages=360–369|doi=10.1017/S0030605323000066|doi-access=free}}</ref>

== Government and politics ==
{{Main|Politics of Indonesia}}
{{See also|Government of Indonesia}}
[[File:Ruang MPR.jpg|thumb|A presidential inauguration by the MPR in the [[MPR/DPR/DPD building|Parliament Complex]] Jakarta, [[First inauguration of Joko Widodo|2014]]|alt=]]

Indonesia is a unitary republic with a presidential system under the [[Constitution of Indonesia|1945 Constitution]].<ref name="UUD45">{{cite web |title=The 1945 Constitution of the Republic of Indonesia |url=http://www.ilo.org/wcmsp5/groups/public/---ed_protect/---protrav/---ilo_aids/documents/legaldocument/wcms_174556.pdf |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171011113409/http://www.ilo.org/wcmsp5/groups/public/---ed_protect/---protrav/---ilo_aids/documents/legaldocument/wcms_174556.pdf |archive-date=11 October 2017 |access-date=11 October 2017 |publisher=International Labour Organization}}</ref><ref name="Hamzah">{{cite journal |last1=Hamzah |first1=Hanim |last2=Narang |first2=Agnesya M. |last3=Yusari |first3=Anggi |date=2021 |title=Legal systems in Indonesia: overview |url=https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/ypfs-documents/11551/ |journal=Documents |access-date=18 March 2025 |archive-date=19 March 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250319002956/https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/ypfs-documents/11551/ |url-status=live }}</ref> The Constitution's five principles of {{lang|id|[[Pancasila]]}} are taught as the basis of Indonesian civic values.<ref name="UUD45" /><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Dewantara |first1=J.A. |date=September 2019 |title=Pancasila as Ideology and Characteristics Civic Education in Indonesia |journal=International Journal for Educational and Vocational Studies |volume=1 |issue=5 |pages=400–405 |doi=10.29103/ijevs.v1i5.1617}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last=Morfit |first=M. |date=1 August 1981 |title=Pancasila: The Indonesian State Ideology According to the New Order Government |journal=Asian Survey |publisher=University of California Press |volume=21 |issue=8 |pages=838–851 |doi=10.2307/2643886}}</ref> After the [[Fall of Suharto|fall of the New Order]] in 1998, four [[amendments to the Constitution of Indonesia|constitutional amendments]] altered the political system by introducing direct presidential elections, creating new state institutions, reducing the MPR's role, and giving regional autonomy a constitutional basis.<ref name="Harijanti">{{cite journal |last1=Dwi Harijanti |first1=S. |last2=Lindsey |first2=T. |date=1 January 2006 |title=Indonesia: General elections test the amended Constitution and the new Constitutional Court |journal=International Journal of Constitutional Law |volume=4 |issue=1 |pages=138–150 |doi=10.1093/icon/moi055 |doi-access=free}}</ref> Indonesia remained a [[unitary state]], while decentralisation laws gave substantial powers to provinces, districts, and cities.<ref>{{Citation |last1=Ardiansyah |first1=F. |title=Forest and land-use governance in a decentralized Indonesia |date=2015 |doi=10.17528/cifor/005695 |hdl=10535/9986 |last2=Marthen |first2=A. |last3=Amalia |first3=N. |doi-access=free |hdl-access=free}}</ref>

The [[President of Indonesia|president]] is [[head of state]], [[head of government]], and commander-in-chief of the [[Indonesian National Armed Forces]], and may serve no more than two five-year terms.<ref name="UUD45" /> The [[People's Consultative Assembly]] (MPR) consists of elected members of the [[House of Representatives (Indonesia)|House of Representatives]] (DPR) and the [[Regional Representative Council]] (DPD).<ref name="UUD45" /> The MPR may amend the constitution, inaugurates the president and vice president, and may dismiss them under constitutional procedures.<ref name="UUD45" /> The DPR makes laws with the president, approves the state budget, and oversees the government. The DPD may propose, review, and advise on bills concerning regional government, but has less authority than the DPR.<ref name="Hamzah" /><ref name="Sinukaban">{{cite journal |last1=Sinukaban |first1=A.J. |date=30 January 2020 |title=The Existence of Regional Representative Boards in the Indonesian Representative Institution System |journal=Journal of Law Science |volume=2 |pages=15–23 |doi=10.35335/jls.v2i1.1607 |doi-access=free |number=1}}</ref>

Indonesia's legal framework follows the [[Civil law (legal system)|civil-law]] tradition, alongside customary (''[[Adat|adat]]'') law and Islamic law governing matters such as marriage and inheritance for Muslims.<ref name="Reni">{{cite web |last1=Reni |first1=Dewi Savitri |last2=Renaldi |first2=Juven |title=The Indonesian Legal System and Legal Research |url=https://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Indonesia1.html |publisher=Hauser Global Law School Program, New York University School of Law |date=November–December 2019 |access-date=22 August 2026}}</ref> [[Judiciary of Indonesia|Judicial power]] is exercised by the [[Supreme Court of Indonesia|Supreme Court]], the courts beneath it, and the [[Constitutional Court of Indonesia|Constitutional Court]].<ref name="UUD45" /> The Supreme Court acts as the final appellate court and reviews regulations below statutes.<ref name="Reni" /> The Constitutional Court reviews statutes for constitutionality and decides disputes over the powers of state institutions, political-party dissolution, and election results.<ref name="UUD45" /> It also has a role in presidential impeachment proceedings.<ref name="UUD45" /> The Religious Courts hear family and inheritance disputes and certain economic cases governed by Islamic law.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Cammack |first1=Mark |last2=Feener |first2=R. Michael |date=2012 |title=The Islamic Legal System in Indonesia |url=https://digitalcommons.law.uw.edu/wilj/vol21/iss1/5/ |journal=Washington International Law Journal |volume=21 |issue=1 |page=13 |access-date=22 August 2026}}</ref> The [[Judicial Commission of Indonesia|Judicial Commission]] proposes candidates for Supreme Court justices and has responsibilities concerning judicial conduct.<ref name="UUD45" />

Since the [[1999 Indonesian legislative election|1999 election]], Indonesia has had a fragmented [[multi-party system]]. No party has won an outright majority in [[Elections in Indonesia|legislative elections]], and presidents have usually governed through broad coalitions that include many parties in the legislature and cabinet.<ref name="Pepinsky">{{cite journal |last1=Pepinsky |first1=Thomas B. |date=December 2025 |title=Cleavages, Institutions, and Democracy in Indonesia: The 2024 Elections in Comparative Perspective |journal=Journal of Current Southeast Asian Affairs |volume=44 |issue=3 |pages=345–365 |doi=10.1177/18681034251349467}}</ref><ref name="Slater">{{cite journal |last1=Slater |first1=Dan |date=March 2018 |title=Party Cartelization, Indonesian-Style: Presidential Power-Sharing and the Contingency of Democratic Opposition |journal=Journal of East Asian Studies |volume=18 |issue=1 |pages=23–46 |doi=10.1017/jea.2017.26 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

Political parties are often grouped into secular-nationalist and Islamic-oriented parties,{{efn|The former includes the [[Indonesian Democratic Party of Struggle]] (PDI-P), the [[Party of the Functional Groups]] ({{lang|id|Golkar}}), and the [[Great Indonesia Movement Party]] ({{lang|id|Gerindra}} Party); and the latter includes the centrist [[National Awakening Party]] (PKB) and the Islamist [[Prosperous Justice Party]] (PKS).}}<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Fossati |first1=Diego |date=August 2019 |title=The Resurgence of Ideology in Indonesia: Political Islam, Aliran and Political Behaviour |journal=Journal of Current Southeast Asian Affairs |volume=38 |issue=2 |pages=119–148 |doi=10.1177/1868103419868400}}</ref> but governing coalitions commonly include both.<ref name="Slater" /> Party competition often relies more on patronage and elite bargaining than detailed policy programmes, although religious issues remain a source of ideological difference.<ref>{{cite report |url=https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320627443_Parties_and_Factions_in_Indonesia_The_Effects_of_Historical_Legacies_and_Institutional_Engineering |title=Parties and Factions in Indonesia: The Effects of Historical Legacies and Institutional Engineering |last1=Fionna |first1=U. |last2=Tomsa |first2=D. |date=2017 |publisher=ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute |location=Singapore |access-date=29 May 2026 |number=1}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Fossati |first1=D. |display-authors=etal |date=February 2020 |title=Ideological representation in clientelistic democracies: The Indonesian case |journal=Electoral Studies |volume=63 |article-number=102111 |doi=10.1016/j.electstud.2019.102111 |hdl=1885/222102}}</ref> Opposition parties have at times joined presidential power-sharing arrangements after elections.<ref name="Pepinsky" /><ref name="Slater" />

Indonesia held its first [[1955 Indonesian legislative election|general election in 1955]]. Since [[2004 Indonesian presidential election|2004]], voters have directly elected the president, the DPR, and the DPD every five years. Members of the DPR are elected through party-based contests, while DPD candidates run as individuals to represent provincial constituencies.<ref name="Sinukaban" /><ref name="Harijanti" /> National elections require large-scale logistics, as ballots and other materials must reach remote islands, mountain areas, and places with limited infrastructure.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Erviana |first1=H.Y. |display-authors=etal |date=December 2024 |title=Election Logistics Distribution Strategy in Indonesia: A Literature Review |url=https://journal.formosapublisher.org/index.php/ijis/article/view/12772 |journal=International Journal of Integrative Sciences |publisher=Formosa Publisher |volume=3 |pages=1375–1386 |access-date=4 November 2025 |number=12 |archive-date=12 November 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251112171246/https://journal.formosapublisher.org/index.php/ijis/article/view/12772 |url-status=live }}</ref>

=== Administrative divisions ===
{{Main|Subdivisions of Indonesia}}
Indonesia is a unitary state divided into [[Provinces of Indonesia|provinces]], [[List of regencies and cities in Indonesia|regencies, and cities]], each with elected regional authorities regulated by law.<ref name="UUD45" /> Provinces are headed by governors and have provincial legislatures (DPRD). Regencies and cities are headed by regents and mayors and have local legislatures.<ref name="UUD45" /> Below regencies and cities are [[Districts of Indonesia|districts]], which are divided into [[Villages of Indonesia|villages]]. Rural villages have self-governing status, while urban villages form part of district administration.<ref>{{cite thesis |last=Syukri |first=M. |title=Participatory Governance in the New Developmental State: Assessing Its Efficacy for Marginal Groups in Indonesia |type=PhD thesis |publisher=The University of Western Australia |doi=10.26182/qt3z-zx90 |year=2021}}</ref>

Regional autonomy after 1998 transferred substantial powers to subnational governments, especially regencies and cities.<ref name="Butt" /> Special arrangements apply to nine provinces. [[Aceh]] may apply aspects of Islamic law,<ref>{{cite journal |last=Miller |first=M.A. |year=2004 |title=The Nanggroe Aceh Darussalam law: a serious response to Acehnese separatism? |journal=Asian Ethnicity |volume=5 |issue=3 |pages=333–351 |doi=10.1080/1463136042000259789}}</ref> the six provinces in [[Western New Guinea#Current government|Papua]] have special autonomy,<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Emilianus |first1=J.E. |last2=Lumbuun |first2=T.G. |last3=Latif |first3=A. |last4=Sinaga |first4=P. |date=2024 |title=Protection of local wisdom of papua's original people by the papua people's assembly in Indonesia |journal=International Journal of Judicial Law |publisher=All Law Journal |volume=3 |issue=4 |pages=10–15 |doi=10.54660/IJJL.2024.3.4.10-15}}</ref> and [[Special Region of Yogyakarta|Yogyakarta]] retains a hereditary sultanate.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Wiszowaty |first1=M.M. |last2=Wahyuni |first2=I. |date=27 December 2023 |title=Monarchy in the Republic – Sultanate of Yogyakarta in the Republic of Indonesia |journal=Constitutional Law Review |publisher=Adam Marszałek Publishing House |volume=76 |pages=321–336 |doi=10.15804/ppk.2023.06.23 |number=6}}</ref> [[Jakarta]] has autonomy due to its role as the national capital.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Okamoto |first1=M. |date=1 April 2014 |title=Jakartans, Institutionally Volatile |journal=Journal of Current Southeast Asian Affairs |publisher=All Law Journal |volume=33 |issue=1 |pages=7–27 |doi=10.1177/186810341403300102 |doi-access=free}}</ref> [[Nusantara]] in [[East Kalimantan]] is planned as the future national capital.<ref>{{cite web |title=Undang-undang (UU) Nomor 3 Tahun 2022 tentang Ibu Kota Negara |trans-title=Law Number 3 of 2022 concerning the National Capital === Foreign relations and military ===
{{Main|Foreign relations of Indonesia|Indonesian National Armed Forces}}
[[File:ASEAN HQ 1.jpg|thumb|Jakarta hosts the [[ASEAN Headquarters|headquarters]] of [[ASEAN]].<ref>{{cite book |last1=Nair |first1=Deepak |title=Learning Diplomacy |date=2016 |isbn=978-981-4762-70-0 |pages=1–28 |chapter=Learning Diplomacy: Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar and Vietnam Diplomats in ASEAN |doi=10.1355/9789814762700-003}}</ref>]]

Indonesia follows an "independent and active" (''<span lang="id" dir="ltr">bebas aktif</span>'') foreign policy, a doctrine formulated by Vice President [[Mohammad Hatta]] in 1948.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Laksamana |first1=E. |last2=Alexandra |first2=L. |date=August 2023 |title=Hatta and Indonesia's Independent and Active Foreign Policy: Retrospect and Prospect |journal=Contemporary Southeast Asia |publisher=ISEAS - Yusof Ishak Institute |volume=45 |issue=2 |pages=327–330 |doi=10.1355/cs45-2k |jstor=27241198}}</ref> In practice, the doctrine has meant avoiding military alliances and rival power blocs while using diplomacy to pursue national interests.<ref name="Anwar">{{cite journal |last=Anwar |first=D.F. |date=April 2018 |title=Indonesia's Vision of Regional Order in East Asia amid U.S.-China Rivalry |journal=Asia Policy |publisher=National Bureau of Asian Research |volume=13 |pages=57–63 |doi=10.1353/asp.2018.0024 |number=2}}</ref><ref name="Shekhar">{{cite book |last=Shekhar |first=V. |url=https://www.nbr.org/publication/indonesias-great-power-management-in-the-indo-pacific-the-balancing-behavior-of-a-dove-state/ |title=Indonesia's Great-Power Management in the Indo-Pacific, The Balancing Behavior of a "Dove State" |date=26 October 2022 |publisher=The National Bureau of Asian Research |volume=17 |page=53 |access-date=23 January 2025 |number=4 |archive-date=17 March 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250317151238/https://www.nbr.org/publication/indonesias-great-power-management-in-the-indo-pacific-the-balancing-behavior-of-a-dove-state/ |url-status=live }}</ref> As a [[middle power]], Indonesia relies on regional diplomacy, multilateral forums, and flexibility in dealing with larger powers.<ref name="Umar">{{cite journal |last=Umar |first=A.R.M. |date=July 2023 |title=The rise of the Asian middle powers: Indonesia's conceptions of international order |url=https://academic.oup.com/ia/article/99/4/1459/7216732 |journal=International Affairs |volume=99 |issue=4 |pages=1459–1476 |doi=10.1093/ia/iiad167 |access-date=29 May 2026 |archive-date=9 February 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240209125505/https://academic.oup.com/ia/article/99/4/1459/7216732 |url-status=live }}</ref>

[[File:Taruna akmil.jpg|thumb|[[Indonesian Military Academy]] cadets]]
As a founding member of the [[Association of Southeast Asian Nations]] (ASEAN), Indonesia places the organisation at the centre of its regional diplomacy, including in managing relations with major Indonesia has a [[mixed economy]] in which the private sector and the government both have large roles.{{sfn |Goldstein |2024 |pp=49-51}}<ref>{{cite report |url=https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099062124085019387/pdf/P1795561b546840d6197b5181ca07a690ed.pdf |title=Indonesia Economic Prospects, June 2024: Unleashing Indonesia's Business Potential |author=World Bank |date=June 2024 |publisher=World Bank |page=27 |access-date=29 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240807004504/https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099062124085019387/pdf/P1795561b546840d6197b5181ca07a690ed.pdf |archive-date=7 August 2024 |url-status=live}}</ref> It is the only [[G20]] member state in Southeast Asia,<ref>{{cite web |title=G20 Members |url=https://g20.org/about-g20/g20-members/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150210155549/https://g20.org/about-g20/g20-members/ |archive-date=10 February 2015 |access-date=20 January 2025 |publisher=G20}}</ref> has the region's largest economy by GDP, and ranks among the [[List of countries by GDP (nominal)|top 20 in nominal terms]] and the [[List of countries by GDP (PPP)|top 10 by purchasing power parity]]. Services and industry account for the largest shares of gross domestic product, while agriculture remains a major source of employment.<ref>{{cite web |date=2022 |title=Indonesia: Economy |url=https://globaledge.msu.edu/countries/indonesia/economy/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250122054227/https://globaledge.msu.edu/countries/indonesia/economy |archive-date=22 January 2025 |access-date=23 March 2025 |publisher=Global Edge}}</ref>

=== Tourism ===
{{Main|Tourism in Indonesia}}
[[File:Borobudur-Nothwest-view.jpg|left|thumb|upright=1.15|[[Borobudur]] in [[Central Java]], part of the [[Borobudur Temple Compounds]] [[World Heritage Site]]<ref>{{cite web |title=Borobudur Temple Compounds |url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/592/ |access-date=5 May 2026 |publisher=UNESCO World Heritage Centre |archive-date=30 May 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210530122312/https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/592/ |url-status=live }}</ref>]]

Tourism is part of Indonesia's service economy, generating about {{currency|14 billion|USD|passthrough=yes}} in foreign-exchange earnings and recording 11.6 million international visitor arrivals in 2023.<ref name="Dávid">{{cite journal |last1=Dávid |first1=L.D |last2=Rahmat |first2=A.F |last3=Priatmoko |first3=S. |date=9 October 2024 |title=Main trends in the tourism industry in Indonesia between 2020–2023 |journal=Journal of Infrastructure, Policy and Development |publisher=EnPress Publisher, LLC |volume=8 |issue=11 |page=8162 |doi=10.24294/jipd.v8i11.8162 |doi-access=free}}</ref> It supports jobs and businesses in accommodation, food services, transport, and related activities.<ref name="Ollivaud">{{cite report |title=Making the most of tourism in Indonesia to promote sustainable regional development |last1=Ollivaud |first1=P. |last2=Haxton |first2=P. |date=13 February 2019 |publisher=OECD |doi=10.1787/c73325d9-en |page=4, 11, 14-23 |doi-access=free |series=OECD Economics Department Working Papers |article-number=1535}}</ref> International tourism is concentrated in [[Bali]] and other major gateways, while domestic travellers account for most tourism spending.<ref name="Ollivaud" /><ref>{{cite report |url=https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/11/oecd-tourism-trends-and-policies-2022_71dc1773/a8dd3019-en.pdf |title=OECD Tourism Trends and Policies 2022 |date=2022 |publisher=OECD |pages=317–321 |access-date=29 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240702195600/https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/11/oecd-tourism-trends-and-policies-2022_71dc1773/a8dd3019-en.pdf |archive-date=2 July 2024 |url-status=live}}</ref> Expanding tourism beyond established destinations depends on better infrastructure, workforce skills, investment conditions, and environmental planning.<ref name="Ollivaud" />

Indonesia has natural, cultural, and historical tourism sites across the archipelago. Its [[List of World Heritage Sites in Indonesia|UNESCO World Heritage Sites]] include [[Komodo National Park]] and the [[Yogyakarta|Cosmological Axis of Yogyakarta]], while sites on the tentative list include [[Bunaken National Park]] and the [[Raja Ampat Islands]].<ref name="UNESCOWHC">{{cite web |title=Indonesia - UNESCO World Heritage Convention |url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/statesparties/id |access-date=21 July 2025 |publisher=UNESCO |archive-date=7 July 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170707173059/https://whc.unesco.org/en/statesparties/id/ |url-status=live }}</ref> [[Bali]] is the country's main destination for foreign tourists.<ref name="Ollivaud" /> Historical and urban heritage tourism includes Dutch colonial sites in [[Kota Tua Jakarta|Jakarta]] and [[Dutch architecture in Semarang|Semarang]].<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Maulina |first1=A. |display-authors=etal |date=10 June 2024 |title=Oud Batavia as a heritage site within Jakarta: Tourist revisit intentions |journal=Tourism |publisher=University of Łódź |volume=34 |pages=135–145 |doi=10.18778/0867-5856.34.1.13 |doi-access=free |number=1}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Yuliati |first1=D. |display-authors=etal |date=2 March 2023 |title=Preservation of the Old City of Semarang, Central Java, Indonesia, and its development as a cultural tourism asset |journal=Cogent Social Sciences |publisher=Taylor & Francis |volume=9 |issue=1 |article-number=2170740 |doi=10.1080/23311886.2023.2170740 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

== Infrastructure ==
=== Transport ===
{{Main|Transport in Indonesia}}
[[File:Whoosh High-speed Train G1224, in Bojongkoneng, Ngamprah.jpg|thumb|Opened in 2023, ''Whoosh'' links Jakarta and [[Bandung]] and is the first high-speed railway in Southeast Asia.<ref name="Zufarihasan" /> |alt=Jakarta and Bandung are connected by Whoosh, the first of its kind in Southeast Asia and the Southern Hemisphere.]]

Transport in Indonesia serves a vast archipelago, while population and economic activity remain concentrated mainly on Java.<ref name="Rothenberg" /><ref name="Mandaluyong">{{cite report |url-status=live}}</ref> Roads carry most passenger and freight traffic; on many smaller and less developed islands, inter-island shipping and air transport provide access to major markets.<ref |archive-date=8 February 2026 |access-date=25 April 2026 |publisher=Oxford Business Group}}</ref> High logistics costs, port bottlenecks, and uneven infrastructure make goods movement expensive, |access-date=29 May 2026}}</ref>

Land transport is most developed along the main population and economic corridors, especially on Java.<ref name="Oxford" /> In cities, formal public transport often operates alongside smaller public and paratransit modes, including ''bajaj'', ''becak'', ''angkot'', minibuses, and motorcycle taxis.<ref name="Ingham">{{cite report |url=https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/500471623303164919/pdf/Indonesia-Sustainable-Urbanization-Multi-Donor-Trust-Fund-Component-3-1-Development-of-Urban-Transport-Support-Platform.pdf |title=Indonesia Sustainable Urbanization Multi-Donor Trust Fund |last1=Ingham |first1=D.J. |date=May 2019 |publisher=World Bank |page=9, 25 |access-date=29 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260601150444/https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/500471623303164919/pdf/Indonesia-Sustainable-Urbanization-Multi-Donor-Trust-Fund-Component-3-1-Development-of-Urban-Transport-Support-Platform.pdf |archive-date=1 June 2026 |url-status=live |display-authors=etal}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Permana |first1=A.S. |display-authors=etal |date=26 September 2018 |title=Dual Formal and Informal Transport Modes towards Quasi-Seamless Transit in a Developing City |journal=International Journal of Built Environment and Sustainability |volume=5 |issue=3 |pages=115–144 |doi=10.11113/ijbes.v5.n3.307}}</ref> Public transport in many cities has not kept pace with urban growth, leading to heavy reliance on motorcycles and cars, while app-based ride-hailing has become part of daily travel.<ref name="Ingham" /><ref name="Oxford" /><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Muchlisin |first1=M. |display-authors=etal |date=18 September 2025 |title=Adoption and frequency of motorcycle and car-based ride-hailing use across income groups in Yogyakarta, Indonesia |journal=Transportation Research Part A: Policy and Practice |volume=201 |article-number=104671 |bibcode=2025TRPA..20104671M |doi=10.1016/j.tra.2025.104671 |doi-access=free |number=104671}}</ref>

|url=https://pair.australiaindonesiacentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/PAIR_Maximising-the-Effectiveness-of-the-South-Sulawesi-Line.pdf |title=Maximising the Effectiveness of the South Sulawesi |archive-date=14 October 2024 |url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="Oxford" /> In dense urban regions, commuter and rapid-transit systems include the [[KRL Commuterline|Greater Jakarta commuter Maritime and air transport connect islands and long-distance routes beyond the main land corridors. Air transport serves domestic and international travel, with [[Soekarno–Hatta International Airport]] as the country's main international gateway and [[Ngurah Rai International Airport|Ngurah Rai]] and [[Juanda International Airport]] among other major airports.<ref name="Oxford" /> Maritime transport is important to inter-island trade and logistics, with the [[Port of Tanjung Priok]] serving as the country's principal port and handling more than half of Indonesia's container traffic.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Suprata |first1=F |last2=Natalia |first2=C |last3=Sugioko |first3=A |date=April 2020 |title=Analysing the cause of idle time in loading and unloading operation at Indonesian international port container terminal: Port of Tanjung Priok case study |journal=IOP Conference Series: Materials Science and Engineering |volume=847 |issue=1 |article-number=012090 |bibcode=2020MS&E..847a2090S |doi=10.1088/1757-899X/847/1/012090 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

=== Energy ===
{{Main|Energy in Indonesia}}
[[File:PLTB-Sidrap.jpg|thumb|Sidrap wind farm, Indonesia's first wind power plant,<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Maulidia |first1=M. |display-authors=etal |date=31 December 2019 |title=Sidrap: A Study of the Factors That Led to the Development of Indonesia's First Large-Scale Wind Farm |journal=Case Studies in the Environment |volume=3 |issue=1 |pages=1–12 |bibcode=2019CSEnv...3.1453M |doi=10.1525/cse.2018.001453}}</ref> in [[Sidrap Regency]], [[South Sulawesi]]]]
Indonesia produces more primary energy than it consumes.{{efn|In 2023, Indonesia produced {{convert|19.2|e15Btu|TWh|lk=on|order=flip|abbr=off}} and consumed {{convert|10.5|e15Btu|TWh|order=flip|abbr=off}} of energy.<ref name="EIA">{{cite web |date=25 August 2025 |title=Overview: Indonesia |url=https://www.eia.gov/international/overview/country/IDN |access-date=24 December 2025 |publisher=U.S. Energy Information Administration |archive-date=14 February 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260214120842/https://www.eia.gov/international/overview/country/IDN |url-status=live }}</ref>}} In 2023, industry and transport accounted for 40% and 36% of final energy consumption, respectively.<ref name="IEA">{{cite web |date=2023 |title=Indonesia |url=https://www.iea.org/countries/indonesia/efficiency-demand |access-date=25 April 2026 |publisher=International Energy Agency |archive-date=27 April 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260427050311/https://www.iea.org/countries/indonesia/efficiency-demand |url-status=live }}</ref> Electricity supply is dominated by the state-owned [[Perusahaan Listrik Negara|State Electricity Company]] (''<span lang="id" dir="ltr">Perusahaan Listrik Negara</span>'', PLN), which distributes power to most households and relies heavily on coal-fired generation.<ref name="Apriliyanti">{{cite journal |last1=Apriliyanti |first1=I.D. |last2=Nugraha |first2=D.B. |last3=Kristiansen |first3=S. |last4=Overland |first4=I. |date=December 2024 |title=To reform or not reform? Competing energy transition perspectives on Indonesia's monopoly electricity supplier Perusahaan Listrik Negara (PLN) |journal=Energy Research & Social Science |volume=118 |article-number=103797 |bibcode=2024ERSS..11803797A |doi=10.1016/j.erss.2024.103797 |doi-access=free}}</ref> Extending the grid is difficult in sparsely populated and island areas, while reliability problems are reported even in places connected to the grid.<ref name="Veldhuis">{{cite journal |last1=Veldhuis |first1=A.J. |last2=Reinders |first2=A.H.M.E. |date=December 2015 |title=Reviewing the potential and cost-effectiveness of off-grid PV systems in Indonesia on a provincial level |journal=Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews |volume=52 |pages=757–769 |bibcode=2015RSERv..52..757V |doi=10.1016/j.rser.2015.07.126}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Zuhri |first1=K. |display-authors=etal |date=2019 |title=Inequality in Indonesia's Electricity Access |url=https://www.gssrr.org/JournalOfBasicAndApplied/article/view/10649/5528 |journal=International Journal of Sciences: Basic and Applied Research |volume=48 |pages=237–249 |access-date=29 May 2026 |number=7}}</ref><ref name="Kunaifi">{{cite journal |last1=Kunaifi |last2=Reinders |first2=A. |date=6 January 2018 |title=Perceived and Reported Reliability of the Electricity Supply at Three Urban Locations in Indonesia |journal=Energies |publisher=MDPI |volume=11 |issue=1 |page=140 |doi=10.3390/en11010140 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

Indonesia had 70.8 gigawatts (GW) of [[Electricity generation|installed power generation]] capacity in 2023.<ref name="EIA" /> Coal is the largest source of primary energy consumption and electricity generation, with natural gas and petroleum also making large contributions.<ref name="EIA" /><ref name="Siregar">{{cite journal |last1=Siregar |first1=Yudha Irmansyah |date=November 2024 |title=Pathways towards net-zero emissions in Indonesia's energy sector |journal=Energy |volume=308 |article-number=133014 |bibcode=2024Ene...30833014S |doi=10.1016/j.energy.2024.133014 |doi-access=free}}</ref> Renewables account for a smaller share of supply,<ref>{{cite web |date=2023 |title=Indonesia - Energy Supply |url=https://www.iea.org/countries/indonesia/energy-mix |access-date=24 January 2025 |publisher=International Energy Agency |archive-date=15 April 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240415212757/https://www.iea.org/countries/indonesia/energy-mix |url-status=live }}</ref> although Indonesia has large hydropower, solar, geothermal, wind, ocean, and bioenergy resources.<ref name="EnergyADB">{{cite report |url=https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/publication/178039/ino-paper-09-2015.pdf |title=Summary of Indonesia's Energy Sector Assessment |date=December 2015 |publisher=Asian Development Bank |access-date=29 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240504181951/https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/publication/178039/ino-paper-09-2015.pdf |archive-date=4 May 2024 |url-status=live |work=ADB Papers on Indonesia}}</ref><ref name="Gielen">{{cite book |last1=Gielen |first1=D. |url=https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2017/Mar/IRENA_REmap_Indonesia_report_2017.pdf |title=Renewable Energy Prospects: Indonesia, a REmap analysis |last2=Saygin |first2=D. |last3=Rigter |first3=J. |date=March 2017 |publisher=International Renewable Energy Agency |isbn=978-92-95111-18-9 |pages=2, 14, 19, 24 |access-date=28 September 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211006193904/https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2017/Mar/IRENA_REmap_Indonesia_report_2017.pdf |archive-date=6 October 2021 |url-status=dead}}</ref> It is also among the world's leading geothermal producers.<ref name="EnergyADB" />

Indonesia exports energy commodities, including coal and liquefied natural gas,<ref name="OEC" /><ref name="EIA" /> while importing refined petroleum products.<ref name="EIA" /> Although historically a leading LNG supplier, Indonesia has shifted more natural gas toward domestic use and gas-infrastructure expansion.<ref>{{cite report |url=https://www.nbr.org/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/publications/sr81_revolutionizing_lng_oct2019-1.pdf |title=Revolutionizing LNG and Natural Gas in the Indo-Pacific |last1=Choi |first1=J. |last2=Herberg |first2=M.E. |last3=Palti-Guzman |first3=L. |last4=Smith |first4=R. |last5=Tsafos |first5=N. |date=October 2019 |publisher=The National Bureau of Asian Research |pages=26–29 |access-date=29 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250416062747/https://www.nbr.org/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/publications/sr81_revolutionizing_lng_oct2019-1.pdf |archive-date=16 April 2025 |url-status=live |number=81 |work=NBR Special Report}}</ref> Energy policy therefore has to manage both commodity production and reliable power supply across the archipelago.<ref name="Apriliyanti" /><ref name="Veldhuis" /><ref name="Kunaifi" />

Indonesia has set targets to increase renewable energy use and reduce emissions, including a net-zero emissions goal by 2060 or sooner.<ref name="Gielen" /><ref name="Siregar" /> The transition is slowed by coal dependence, uncertain regulations, PLN's financial position, grid limits, and the cost of electrifying remote areas.<ref>{{cite thesis |last1=Roesad |first1=K. |title=Barriers to Renewable Energy Investment in the Indonesian Power Sector |date=2017 |access-date=29 May 2026 |publisher=Australian National University |url=https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstream/1885/154656/1/Roesad%20K%20Thesis%202018.pdf |pages=23-24, 126-172 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200222120009/https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstream/1885/154656/1/Roesad%20K%20Thesis%202018.pdf |archive-date=22 February 2020 |url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Wirawan |first1=Hanni |last2=Gultom |first2=Yohanna M.L. |date=June 2021 |title=The effects of renewable energy-based village grid electrification on poverty reduction in remote areas: The case of Indonesia |journal=Energy for Sustainable Development |volume=62 |pages=186–194 |bibcode=2021ESusD..62..186W |doi=10.1016/j.esd.2021.04.006}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Schmidt |first1=T.S. |last2=Blum |first2=N.U. |last3=Wakeling |first3=R.S. |date=2013 |title=Attracting Private Investments into Rural Electrification—A Case Study on Renewable Energy Based Village Grids in Indonesia |journal=Energy for Sustainable Development |volume=17 |pages=581–595 |doi=10.1016/j.esd.2013.10.001 |doi-access=free |number=6}}</ref>

The [[2020 Indonesian census|2020 census]] recorded 270.2 million people,<ref name="2020census" /> making Indonesia the world's [[List of countries and dependencies by population|fourth most populous is concentrated on Java and in urban centres, while many provinces outside Java and Bali are much less densely populated.{{sfn |Tejasmara |2023 |pp=117-119}}<ref name="Mardiansjah" /> Java is home to Indonesia has a median age of 30.7 years as of 2026<ref>{{cite web |title=Median age of population – Indonesia |url=https://population.un.org/dataportal/data/indicators/67/locations/360/start/2023/end/2026/table/pivotbylocation?df=4651eb49-b919-4e79-93ca-f00036a1e355 |website=Population Division Data Portal |publisher=United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division |access-date=23 August 2026}}</ref> and a large working-age population.{{sfn |Tejasmara |2023 |pp=24-28}} [[Jakarta]] is the country's largest metropolitan area and, under the UN's harmonised urban definition, is listed as the world's most populous city, with nearly 42 million inhabitants as of 2025.{{efn|In 2025, Jakarta's population and civil-registration office recorded 11,010,514 residents with official addresses in DKI Jakarta.<ref name="DisDukCapilJakarta">{{cite web |date=27 November 2025 |title=Memahami Perbedaan Data Penduduk Jakarta: Data PBB vs Data Dukcapil |trans-title=Understanding the Difference in Jakarta Population Data: UN Data vs. Population and Civil Registration Data |url=https://kependudukancapil.jakarta.go.id/2025/11/27/memahami-perbedaan-data-penduduk-jakarta-data-pbb-vs-data-dukcapil/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260329040803/https://kependudukancapil.jakarta.go.id/2025/11/27/memahami-perbedaan-data-penduduk-jakarta-data-pbb-vs-data-dukcapil/ |archive-date=29 March 2026 |access-date=29 March 2026 |publisher=Jakarta Population and Civil Registration Office |language=id}}</ref> The UN figure refers to a greater urban agglomeration centred on Jakarta and including [[Jakarta metropolitan area|surrounding areas]] such as [[Bogor]], [[Depok]], [[Tangerang]], and [[Bekasi]]. The UN's Degree of Urbanization method groups connected settlements using common population-size, density, and contiguity criteria.<ref name="UNWUP2025">{{cite report |url=https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/undesa_pd_2025_wup2025_summary_of_results.pdf |title=World Urbanization Prospects 2025: Summary of Results |year=2025 |publisher=United Nations |location=New York |access-date=1 June 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260503063515/https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/undesa_pd_2025_wup2025_summary_of_results.pdf |archive-date=3 May 2026 |url-status=live |work=Department of Economic and Social Affairs - Population Division}}</ref>}}<ref name="UNWUP2025" /> In the same year, approximately 59% of Indonesians lived in urban areas.<ref>{{cite web |title=Share of people living in urban areas, 2025 |url=https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/share-of-population-urban |access-date=1 June 2026 |publisher=Our World in Data |archive-date=12 August 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210812214024/https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/share-of-population-urban |url-status=live }}</ref> Urbanisation has come from rural-to-urban migration, natural increase in urban areas, and the reclassification of rural settlements. It has also expanded metropolitan regions, especially on Java,{{sfn |Tejasmara |2023 |pp=132-136}}<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Mardiansjah |first1=F.H. |display-authors=etal |date=5 March 2021 |title=New Patterns of Urbanization in Indonesia: Emergence of Non-statutory Towns and New Extended Urban Regions |journal=Journal of Urban and Regional Analysis |volume=12 |issue=1 |pages=11–26 |bibcode=2021EnUrA..12...11M |doi=10.1177/0975425321990384}}</ref><ref name="Mardiansjah">{{cite conference|last1=Mardiansjah|first1=F.H.|display-authors=etal|date=September 2019|url=https://www.researchgate.net/publication/351160462_Urban_Population_Growth_and_the_Growth_of_Towns_and_Cities_in_Indonesia_the_Challenge_of_Non-statutory_Town_Development|title=Urban Population Growth and The Growth of Towns and Cities in Indonesia: The Challenge of Non-Statutory Town Development|conference=55th ISOCARP World Planning Congress|publisher=International Society of City and Regional Planners|location=Jakarta, Bogor|access-date=27 April 2026}}</ref> and increased demand for infrastructure, basic services, and city management.{{sfn |Tejasmara |2023 |pp=132-136}}

About 8 million [[Indonesian diaspora|Indonesians reside overseas]], with large communities in [[Malaysia]], the [[Netherlands]], [[Saudi Arabia]], [[Singapore]], and [[Taiwan]].<ref>{{cite book |last1=Setijadi |first1=Charlotte |title=Harnessing the Potential of the Indonesian Diaspora |date=2017 |isbn=978-981-4786-92-8 |page=7 |doi=10.1355/9789814786928}}</ref> A 2022 [[Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development|OECD]] report put the share of adults who intended to emigrate permanently at 2%, the lowest in ASEAN.<ref name="OECDMigration" /> Indonesian emigration is dominated by temporary labour migration, much of it to nearby or culturally and religiously familiar destinations such as Malaysia and Saudi Arabia.<ref name="OECDMigration">{{cite report |url=https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/a-review-of-indonesian-emigrants_9a45bf41/48a8a873-en.pdf |title=A Review of Indonesian Emigrants |date=21 June 2022 |publisher=OECD Publishing |location=Paris |pages=8-10, 30-31 |isbn=978-92-64-33707-7 |access-date=1 June 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250430001938/https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/a-review-of-indonesian-emigrants_9a45bf41/48a8a873-en.pdf |archive-date=30 April 2025 |url-status=live |series=Talent Abroad}}</ref>

=== Ethnic groups and languages ===
{{Main|Ethnic groups in Indonesia|Native Indonesians|Languages of Indonesia}}
[[File:Indonesia Ethnic Groups Map - EN.svg|upright=1.5|thumb|A map of ethnic groups in Indonesia]]

The [[2010 Indonesian census|2010 census]] listed over 1,300 ''suku bangsa'', or ethnic groups;<ref name="2010census" /> a later study reclassified them into about 600 groups after noting classification difficulties.<ref name="Ananta" /> Austronesian-speaking populations predominate in much of western and central Indonesia; their spread across the islands involved migration, settlement, and mixing with older communities.{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=5–7}}<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Mona |first1=S. |display-authors=etal |date=4 May 2009 |title=Genetic Admixture History of Eastern Indonesia as Revealed by Y-Chromosome and Mitochondrial DNA Analysis |journal=Molecular Biology and Evolution |publisher=Oxford University Press |volume=26 |issue=8 |pages=1865–1877 |doi=10.1093/molbev/msp097 |pmid=19414523 |doi-access=free}}</ref> Melanesian populations live mainly in the eastern islands, including parts of Nusa Tenggara, Maluku, and western New Guinea.{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=5–7}}{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=6}}

The [[Javanese people|Javanese]] are the largest ethnic group, making up about 40% of the population.<ref name="Ananta">{{cite book |last1=Ananta |first1=A. |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=crKfCgAAQBAJ |title=Demography of Indonesia's Ethnicity |publisher=Institute of Southeast Asian Studies |year=2015 |isbn=978-981-4519-87-8 |pages=vi, xv-xviii, 3, 16-38 |display-authors=etal |access-date=12 December 2024}}</ref> They have filled many senior roles in government and the military, in proportions somewhat above their share of the population,{{sfn |Frederick |Worden |2011 |pp=257-258}} although their population share has declined.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Ananta |first1=A. |date=2016 |title=Declining Dominance of an Ethnic Group in a Large Multi-ethnic Developing Country: The Case of the Javanese in Indonesia |journal=Population Review |publisher=Sociological Demography Press |volume=55 |doi=10.1353/prv.2016.0000 |number=1}}</ref> Early Indonesian nationalists did not define the nation through a single ethnic tradition; they sought to accommodate ethnic difference within a united state.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Elson |first1=R.E. |date=1 October 2005 |title=Constructing the nation: Ethnicity, race, modernity and citizenship in early Indonesian thought |journal=Asian Ethnicity |volume=6 |issue=3 |pages=145–160 |doi=10.1080/14631360500226556}}</ref> Other major groups include the [[Sundanese people|Sundanese]], [[Malay Indonesians|Malay]], [[Batak]], [[Madurese people|Madurese]], [[Betawi people|Betawi]], [[Minangkabau people|Minangkabau]], and [[Bugis people|Bugis]].<ref name="Ananta" />{{efn|Indonesia is also home to smaller communities of [[Chinese Indonesians|Chinese]], [[Indian Indonesians|Indian]], and [[Arab Indonesians|Arab]] descent, each with a long-standing presence in the archipelago.<ref>{{cite web |last=Hervandi |first=R. |date=10 May 2011 |title=Reimagining Chinese Indonesians in Democratic Indonesia |url=https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/129254/apb109_2.pdf |access-date=2 May 2026 |website=Asia Pacific Bulletin |publisher=East-West Center |number=109 |archive-date=29 August 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200829071007/https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/129254/apb109_2.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last=Mani |first=A. |title=Rising India and Indian Communities in East Asia |publisher=ISEAS Publishing |year=2008 |editor-last1=Kesavapany |editor-first1=K. |location=Singapore |chapter=Indians in a Rapidly Transforming Indonesia |doi=10.1355/9789812308009-019 |display-editors=etal}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last=Al Qurtuby |first=S. |year=2017 |title=Arabs and "Indo-Arabs" in Indonesia: Historical Dynamics, Social Relations and Contemporary Changes |journal=International Journal of Asia Pacific Studies |volume=13 |issue=2 |pages=45–72 |doi=10.21315/ijaps2017.13.2.3}}</ref>}}

The official language, [[Indonesian language|Indonesian]], is a standardized variety of [[Malay language|Malay]] based on the Riau-Johor literary tradition. Malay had long served as a ''[[lingua franca]]'' in the archipelago before Indonesian nationalists adopted it as a language of unity through the 1928 [[Youth Pledge]]; it gained official status in 1945 under the name ''Bahasa Indonesia''.<ref name="Sneddon">{{cite book |last1=Sneddon |first1=J.N. |url=https://unsw.press/books/the-indonesian-language/ |title=The Indonesian language: its history and role in modern society |date=2003 |publisher=University of New South Wales Press Ltd |isbn=0-86840-598-1 |access-date=1 June 2026 |archive-date=3 June 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260603120953/https://unsw.press/books/the-indonesian-language/ |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="Steinhauer" /> Written in the [[Latin script]], Indonesian has spread through education, government, and mass media, and serves as a common language across ethnic and regional boundaries.<ref name="Steinhauer">{{cite journal |last1=Steinhauer |first1=H. |year=1994 |title=The Indonesian language situation and linguistics: Prospects and possibilities |journal=Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde |volume=150 |issue=4 |pages=755–784 |doi=10.1163/22134379-90003070}}</ref>

Indonesia is also one of the world's most linguistically diverse countries, with more than 700 languages spoken across the archipelago.<ref name="Aji-AF">{{cite conference|last1=Aji|first1=A.F.|display-authors=etal|date=2022|title=One Country, 700+ Languages: NLP Challenges for Underrepresented Languages and Dialects in Indonesia|conference=Proceedings of the 60th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL)|publisher=Association for Computational Linguistics|location=Dublin|pages=7226–7249|doi=10.18653/v1/2022.acl-long.500|doi-access=free}}</ref> Most local languages belong to the [[Austronesian languages|Austronesian family]], while eastern Indonesia includes more than 150 Papuan languages.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=7}} [[Javanese language|Javanese]] is the most widely spoken local language<ref name="Aji-AF" /> and has official regional status in [[Special Region of Yogyakarta|Yogyakarta]].<ref>{{cite act|type=Regional Regulation|index=2|date=2021|legislature=[[List of governors of Yogyakarta|Governor of Special Region of Yogyakarta]]|title=Peraturan Daerah (Perda) Provinsi Daerah Istimewa Yogyakarta Nomor 2 Tahun 2021 tentang Pemeliharaan dan Pengembangan Bahasa, Sastra, dan Aksara Jawa|trans-title=Regional Regulation of the Province of the Special Region of Yogyakarta Number 2 of 2021 concerning the Preservation and Development of the Javanese Language, Literature, and Script|url=https://peraturan.bpk.go.id/Home/Details/162614/perda-no-2-tahun-2021|language=id|access-date=19 March 2021}}</ref> Several local languages also retain, or have historically used, distinct writing traditions.<ref>{{cite conference|last1=Farid Adilazuarda|first1=M.|display-authors=etal|title=NusaAksara: A Multimodal and Multilingual Benchmark for Preserving Indonesian Indigenous Scripts|book-title=Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)|year=2025|eprint=2502.18148}}</ref> Local languages remain part of regional identity and cultural heritage, even as Indonesian dominates national public life.<ref name="Sneddon" /><ref name="Steinhauer" />

Colonial-era European-descended communities were small. The 1930 census counted 246,000 Europeans in the Dutch East Indies, or 0.4% of the population, most of them Dutch nationals.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=van Imhoff |first1=E. |last2=Beets |first2=G. |date=March 2004 |title=A demographic history of the Indo-Dutch population, 1930–2001 |journal=Journal of Population Research |publisher=Springer |volume=21 |issue=2 |pages=47–72 |doi=10.1007/BF03032210}}</ref> Dutch did not become widely spoken as Malay was already the main lingua franca and was promoted by the colonial policy. Dutch-language education was largely limited to Europeans and a small indigenous elite.<ref>{{cite book |last=Salverda |first=R. |url=https://pure.knaw.nl/ws/files/1482566/42_Salverda_Between_Dutch.pdf |title=Dutch: An International Handbook on Linguistic Variation |publisher=De Gruyter Mouton |year=2013 |editor-last1=Hinskens |editor-first1=F. |pages=800–821 |chapter=Between Dutch and Indonesian: Colonial Dutch in time and space |access-date=1 June 2026 |editor-last2=Taeldeman |editor-first2=J. |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240925154629/https://pure.knaw.nl/ws/files/1482566/42_Salverda_Between_Dutch.pdf |archive-date=25 September 2024 |url-status=live}}</ref> Dutch fluency today is limited, although the language is relevant to some civil and commercial codes whose official versions are still in Dutch.<ref>{{cite book |last=Bell |first=G.F. |title=Codification in East Asia |year=2014 |editor-last1=Wang |editor-first1=W.Y. |pages=39-50 |chapter=Codification and Decodification: The State of the Civil and Commercial Codes in Indonesia |doi=10.1007/978-3-319-03446-1_3}}</ref>

=== Religion ===
{{Main|Religion in Indonesia}}
[[File:Religious affiliation by district (kecamatan) in Indonesia (2022).svg|thumb|right|upright=1.5|A map of districts (''kecamatan'') coloured by plurality/majority religious affiliation and what percentage of citizens it represents]]
[[File:Banda Aceh's Grand Mosque, Indonesia.jpg|thumb|[[Baiturrahman Grand Mosque]] in [[Banda Aceh]], northern Sumatra. The region saw one of Southeast Asia's earliest Islamic sultanates, the [[Samudera Pasai Sultanate]].<ref name="Feener" />]]
[[File:Salah Satu Upacara Besar Di Pura Agung Besakih.jpg|thumb|A Hindu prayer ceremony at [[Besakih Temple]] in [[Bali]], the only province where [[Balinese Hinduism|Hinduism]] is the predominant religion<ref name="McDaniel" />]]

Indonesia recognises [[Religion in Indonesia|six religions]]: [[Islam in Indonesia|Islam]], [[Protestantism in Indonesia|Protestantism]], [[Roman Catholicism in Indonesia|Roman Catholicism]], [[Hinduism in Indonesia|Hinduism]], [[Buddhism in Indonesia|Buddhism]], and [[Supreme Council for the Confucian Religion in Indonesia|Confucianism]],<ref name="Marshall">{{cite journal |last=Marshall |first=P. |date=2018 |title=The Ambiguities of Religious Freedom in Indonesia |journal=The Review of Faith & International Affairs |volume=16 |issue=1 |pages=85–96 |doi=10.1080/15570274.2018.1433588 |doi-access=free}}</ref> while the constitution guarantees freedom to choose and practise religion and belief.<ref name="UUD45" /> The state regulates religious affiliation and public worship.<ref name="Marshall" /> Recognised religions receive administrative recognition for religious affiliation, marriages, and houses of worship, while followers of [[Aliran Kepercayaan|indigenous religion]]s have often received weaker recognition or faced discrimination.<ref name="Marshall" /> ''[[Pancasila]]'' makes belief in one God one of the state ideology's five principles and is used to support national unity and religious harmony.<ref name="Głąb">{{cite journal |last=Głąb |first=K.M. |date=2020 |title=The Culture of Pancasila: An Indonesian concept that fuses the impossible |url=https://bibliotekanauki.pl/articles/52155168 |journal=Acta Asiatica Varsoviensia |publisher=Polish Academy of Sciences |pages=5–22 |access-date=8 May 2025 |number=33 |archive-date=16 February 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260216055936/https://bibliotekanauki.pl/articles/52155168 |url-status=live }}</ref> Religious minorities still face discrimination, including local religious bylaws, social pressure, and restrictions on worship.<ref name="Marshall" /><ref name="Sumaktoyo">{{cite journal |last=Sumaktoyo |first=N.G. |date=26 March 2020 |title=A Price for Democracy? Religious Legislation and Religious Discrimination in Post-Soeharto Indonesia |journal=Bulletin of Indonesian Economic Studies |volume=56 |issue=1 |pages=23–42 |doi=10.1080/00074918.2019.1661354}}</ref>

Indonesia has [[Islam by country|more Muslims than any other country]],<ref>{{cite web |date=2024 |title=5 facts about Muslims and Christians in Indonesia |url=https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/03/28/5-facts-about-muslims-and-christians-in-indonesia/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240515154311/https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/03/28/5-facts-about-muslims-and-christians-in-indonesia/ |archive-date=15 May 2024 |access-date=3 June 2026 |publisher=Pew Research Center}}</ref> with 251 million Muslims in 2025, or 87.15% of the population.<ref name="Tempo" />{{efn|[[Sunni Islam|Sunnis]] constitute 99% of the Muslim population.<ref>{{cite report |url=https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2011/01/27/the-future-of-the-global-muslim-population/ |title=The Future of the Global Muslim Population |date=27 January 2011 |publisher=Pew Research Center |page=154 |access-date=3 December 2025 |archive-date=30 September 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230930233955/https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2011/01/27/the-future-of-the-global-muslim-population/ |url-status=live }}</ref> The rest consists of the [[Shia Islam in Indonesia|Shias]] and [[Ahmadiyya in Indonesia|Ahmadis]], who form 1% (1–3 million) and 0.2% (200,000–400,000) of the Muslim population.<ref name="Marshall" /><ref>{{cite web |author=((Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights and Labor)) |date=2017 |title=2016 Indonesia International Religious Freedom Report |url=https://www.state.gov/documents/organization/268976.pdf |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171219044652/https://www.state.gov/documents/organization/268976.pdf |archive-date=19 December 2017 |access-date=19 December 2017 |publisher=U.S. Department of State}}</ref>}} Protestants and Catholics together made up 10.44% of the population,<ref name="Tempo" /> and Christians form majorities in several eastern provinces.<ref>{{cite report |url=https://www.state.gov/reports/2023-report-on-international-religious-freedom/indonesia/ |title=2023 Report on International Religious Freedom: Indonesia |date=2024 |publisher=United States Department of State |access-date=3 June 2026 |archive-date=3 June 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260603120953/https://www.state.gov/reports/2023-report-on-international-religious-freedom/indonesia/ |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="Aritonang">{{cite book |url=https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/45624 |title=A History of Christianity in Indonesia |date=2008 |publisher=Brill |isbn=978-90-474-4183-0 |editor-last1=Aritonang |editor-first1=J.S. |series=Studies in Christian Mission |volume=35 |access-date=6 June 2026 |editor-last2=Steenbrink |editor-first2=K. |archive-date=21 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260521142553/https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/45624 |url-status=live }}</ref> Hindus accounted for 1.66% and are concentrated in Bali,<ref name="McDaniel" /> while Buddhists accounted for 0.69% and are found mainly among [[Chinese Indonesians]], though not exclusively.<ref>{{cite web |last=Suprajitno |first=S. |date=10 October 2011 |title=Negotiating the Cultural and the Religious: The Recasting of the Chinese Indonesian Buddhist |url=https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-7-issue-3-oct-dec-2011-chinese-indonesian-buddhist-cultural-religious/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260602085502/https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-7-issue-3-oct-dec-2011-chinese-indonesian-buddhist-cultural-religious/ |archive-date=2 June 2026 |access-date=1 June 2026 |publisher=BiblioAsia}}</ref>

Many local belief systems predate the recognised religions and continue alongside them. They include beliefs in ancestors, spirits, and powers tied to places such as fields, forests, rivers, and mountains.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Wessing |first1=R. |date=2006 |title=A Community of Spirits: People, Ancestors, and Nature Spirits in Java |journal=Crossroads: An Interdisciplinary Journal of Southeast Asian Studies |volume=18 |pages=11–111 |jstor=40860833 |number=1}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Watts |first1=J. |display-authors=etal |date=23 September 2015 |title=Pulotu: Database of Austronesian Supernatural Beliefs and Practices |journal=PLOS ONE |volume=10 |issue=9 |article-number=e0136783 |bibcode=2015PLoSO..1036783W |doi=10.1371/journal.pone.0136783 |pmc=4580586 |pmid=26398231 |doi-access=free}}</ref> Traditions such as [[Sunda Wiwitan]],<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Mutaqin |first1=Z.Z. |date=7 January 2014 |title=Penghayat, orthodoxy and the legal politics of the state |journal=Indonesia and the Malay World |volume=42 |issue=122 |pages=1–23 |doi=10.1080/13639811.2014.870771}}</ref> [[Kejawèn]],<ref>{{cite book |last1=Geertz |first1=C. |url=https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/R/bo3627129.html |title=The Religion of Java |date=1976 |publisher=University of Chicago Press |access-date=29 December 2025 |orig-date=1960 |archive-date=24 November 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251124100848/https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/R/bo3627129.html |url-status=live }}</ref> and [[Kaharingan]]<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Baier |first=M. |date=2007 |title=The Development of the Hindu Kaharingan Religion: A New Dayak Religion in Central Kalimantan |journal=Anthropos |volume=102 |issue=2 |pages=566–570 |doi=10.5771/0257-9774-2007-2-566 |jstor=40389742}}</ref> continue in different forms within or alongside the recognised religions. In Java and Bali, Islam and Hinduism also absorbed older customs and ritual practices.<ref>{{cite book |url=https://www.routledge.com/The-Politics-of-Religion-in-Indonesia-Syncretism-Orthodoxy-and-Religious-Contention-in-Java-and-Bali/Picard-Madinier/p/book/9781138844506 |title=The Politics of Religion in Indonesia: Syncretism, Orthodoxy, and Religious Contention in Java and Bali |date=2011 |publisher=Routledge |isbn=978-0-415-61311-8 |editor-last1=Picard |editor-first1=M. |edition=1 |access-date=29 December 2025 |editor-last2=Madinier |editor-first2=R. |archive-date=1 September 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250901223435/https://www.routledge.com/The-Politics-of-Religion-in-Indonesia-Syncretism-Orthodoxy-and-Religious-Contention-in-Java-and-Bali/Picard-Madinier/p/book/9781138844506 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Ali |first1=M. |date=2011 |title=Muslim diversity: Islam and local tradition in Java and Sulawesi, Indonesia |journal=Indonesian Journal of Islam and Muslim Societies |volume=1 |issue=1 |pages=1–35 |doi=10.18326/ijims.v1i1.1-35}}</ref>

[[Hinduism]] and [[Buddhism]] were the earliest major religions from outside the archipelago to become established there.<ref name="McDaniel">{{cite journal |last=McDaniel |first=J. |date=16 July 2017 |title=Religious change and experimentation in Indonesian Hinduism |journal=International Journal of Dharma Studies |volume=5 |article-number=20 |page=2 |doi=10.1186/s40613-017-0056-x |doi-access=free |number=20}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last=Acri |first=A. |url=https://bookshop.iseas.edu.sg/publication/2082 |title=Buddhist Dynamics in Premodern and Early Modern Southeast Asia |date=19 May 2017 |publisher=ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute |isbn=978-9-814-51907-6 |editor-last1=Lammerts |editor-first1=D. C. |page=261 |chapter=Revisiting the Cult of "Śiva-buddha" in Java and Bali |access-date=6 June 2026 |archive-date=21 May 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260521072135/https://bookshop.iseas.edu.sg/publication/2082 |url-status=live }}</ref> Indian religious and cultural influence entered through trade, intermarriage, and religious activity, and became part of palace culture and local practice.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Rahman |first1=Taufiq |date=6 June 2014 |title='Indianization' of Indonesia in an Historical Sketch |journal=International Journal of Nusantara Islam |volume=1 |issue=2 |pages=56–64 |doi=10.15575/ijni.v1i2.26}}</ref> Arabic geographers wrote about Southeast Asia by the 9th century, and Chinese records mention Muslim merchants and envoys by the 10th, while larger Muslim communities and polities developed from the late 13th century onward.<ref name="Feener">{{cite book |last1=Feener |first1=R. Michael |title=Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Asian History |date=2019 |isbn=978-0-19-027772-7 |chapter=Islam in Southeast Asia to c. 1800 |doi=10.1093/acrefore/9780190277727.013.40}}</ref> Islamisation unfolded through commerce, religious teachers, and the rise of Muslim port polities and sultanates.<ref name="Reid">{{cite book |last=Reid |first=A. |title=Historia: Essays in Commemoration of the 25th Anniversary of the Department of History, University of Malaya |date=1984 |publisher=Malaysian Historical Society |pages=20, 27 |chapter=The Islamization of Southeast Asia |hdl=1885/143664}}</ref><ref name="de Graaf">{{cite book |last=de Graaf |first=H.J. |title=Islamic States in Java 1500-1700 |date=1 January 1976 |publisher=Brill |isbn=978-90-04-28700-6 |editor-last=Pigeaud |editor-first=T.G.Th. |series=Verhandelingen van het Koninklijk Instituut voor Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde |volume=70 |pages=1–23 |chapter=The First Islamic States of Java 15th and 16th Centuries |doi=10.1163/9789004287006_002}}</ref> Javanese traditions often present the ''[[Wali Sanga]]'' as the leading figures in the Islamisation of Java.<ref name="Afandi">{{cite journal |last1=Afandi |first1=A.J. |date=March 2023 |title=Islam and Local Culture: The Acculturation Formed by Walisongo in Indonesia |journal=Indonesian Journal of Humanities and Social Sciences |volume=4 |issue=1 |pages=103–124 |doi=10.33367/ijhass.v4i1.4135 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

Christianity grew through Catholic and Protestant missions during the Portuguese and Dutch colonial periods,{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=28–29}}<ref name="Aritonang" /> with different histories across regions and denominations.<ref name="Aritonang" /> It became most established in parts of eastern Indonesia while remaining a national minority.<ref name="Aritonang" /> Small Jewish communities also existed in the archipelago, but their numbers have been negligible since Indonesian independence.<ref>{{cite web |last=Kowner |first=R. |date=20 June 2011 |title=Indonesia's Jews |url=https://www.insideindonesia.org/archive/articles/indonesia-s-jews |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260309103816/https://www.insideindonesia.org/archive/articles/indonesia-s-jews |archive-date=9 March 2026 |access-date=26 April 2026 |publisher=Inside Indonesia}}</ref>

=== Education ===
{{Main|Education in Indonesia}}
[[File:No 18 Rektorat Universitas Indonesia.jpg|thumb|[[University of Indonesia]] in Depok|alt=]]
Indonesia's education system includes more than 50 million students and over 250,000 schools.<ref name="WorldBankEducation">{{cite web |date=1 September 2024 |title=World Bank and Education in Indonesia |url=https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/indonesia/brief/world-bank-and-education-in-indonesia |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250304225325/https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/indonesia/brief/world-bank-and-education-in-indonesia |archive-date=4 March 2025 |access-date=24 January 2025 |publisher=World Bank}}</ref> It is managed across ministries responsible for school education, higher education, and religious education,{{efn|The [[Ministry of Primary and Secondary Education]], the [[Ministry of Higher Education, Science, and Technology (Indonesia)|Ministry of Higher Education, Science, and Technology]] and the [[Ministry of Religious Affairs (Indonesia)|Ministry of Religious Affairs]] for Islamic schools<ref name="WorldBankEducation" />}} and follows a 6-3-3-4 structure: six years of elementary school, three years each of junior and senior secondary school, and four years of tertiary education.<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Mukminin |first1=A. |last2=Habibi |first2=A. |last3=Diat Prajoso |first3=L. |last4=Idi |first4=A. |last5=Hamidah |first5=A. |date=21 June 2019 |title=Curriculum Reform in Indonesia: Moving from an Exclusive to Inclusive Curriculum |journal=Center for Educational Policy Studies Journal |volume=9 |issue=2 |pages=53–72 |doi=10.26529/cepsj.543 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

=== Healthcare ===
{{Main|Healthcare in Indonesia}}
[[File:Ciptomangun-hospital.jpg|thumb|[[Dr. Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital]] in Jakarta|alt=]]
After independence, Indonesia began building a public health sector, but hospitals and other services faced severe resource shortages.{{sfn |Mahendradhata |Trisnantoro |Listyadewi |Soewondo |2017 |pp=22}} The number of health facilities and workers later increased, although long travel times, uneven hospital access, and workforce gaps remain in several provinces.{{sfn |Mahendradhata |Trisnantoro |Listyadewi |Soewondo |2017 |pp=1-4, 93, 106, 113, 122, 200, 219, 221, 225-227}}

From the late 1960s, the government expanded basic healthcare through [[Puskesmas|community health centres]] (''puskesmas'') in rural areas.{{sfn |Mahendradhata |Trisnantoro |Listyadewi |Soewondo |2017 |pp=22-23}} Routine immunisation began in the 1970s, and oral polio vaccine was added in 1981 under the expanded immunisation programme.<ref name="Azizatunnisa">{{citation |last1=Azizatunnisa |first1=L. |title=Ensuring sustainability of polio immunization in health system transition: lessons from the polio eradication initiative in Indonesia |date=6 September 2021 |journal=BMC Public Health |volume=21 |issue=1 |page=1624 |doi=10.1186/s12889-021-11642-7 |pmc=8419659 |pmid=34488698 |last2=Cintyamena |first2=U. |last3=Mahendradhata |first3=Y. |last4=A. |first4=Riris Andono |doi-access=free}}</ref> In 2014, Indonesia launched ''[[Healthcare in Indonesia#Universal health coverage|Jaminan Kesehatan Nasional]]'' (JKN), a national health-insurance programme managed by the [[BPJS Kesehatan|Social Security Agency on Health]] (''BPJS Kesehatan'').<ref name="Susilo">{{cite journal |last1=Susilo |first1=D. |display-authors=etal |date=December 2025 |title=Can Indonesia achieve universal health coverage? Organisational and financing challenges in implementing the national health insurance system |journal=SSM - Health Systems |volume=5 |article-number=100138 |doi=10.1016/j.ssmhs.2025.100138 |pmc=12678622 |pmid=41358189}}</ref> JKN is one of the world's largest [[Single-payer healthcare|single-payer health-insurance systems]] and covered 98% of the population in 2024,<ref name="Susilo" /> but differences remain in service access, health-worker distribution, referrals, and specialist care.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Supriyatiningsih |first1=W. |display-authors=etal |date=21 September 2021 |title=Availability and Accessibility of Primary Care for the Remote, Rural, and Poor Population of Indonesia |journal=Frontiers in Public Health |volume=9 |article-number=721886 |bibcode=2021FrPH....921886W |doi=10.3389/fpubh.2021.721886 |pmc=8491579 |pmid=34621720 |doi-access=free}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last1=Meliala |first1=A. |url=https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/in-sickness-and-in-health/addressing-regional-disparities-in-access-to-medical-specialists-in-indonesia/170C51BCC171841F2B15A7672FE50D79 |title=In Sickness and In Health Diagnosing Indonesia |last2=Rarasati |first2=S. |publisher=ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute |year=2022 |editor-last1=Witoelar |editor-first1=F. |pages=71–87 |chapter=Addressing regional disparities in access to medical specialists in Indonesia |access-date=27 April 2026 |editor-last2=Utomo |editor-first2=A.}}</ref>

Health expenditure was 2.69% of GDP in 2022.<ref>{{cite web |title=Current health expenditure (% of GDP) |url=https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SH.XPD.CHEX.GD.ZS?locations=ID |access-date=8 May 2025 |publisher=World Bank}}</ref> Services are delivered through ''puskesmas'', hospitals, and private providers.{{sfn |Mahendradhata |Trisnantoro |Listyadewi |Soewondo |2017 |pp=116}} Life expectancy rose from 54.9 years in 1973 to 71.1 years in 2023,<ref>{{Cite web |title=Life expectancy |url=https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/life-expectancy?country=~IDN |access-date=21 January 2025 |website=Our World in Data}}</ref> while child mortality declined from 15.5 deaths per 100 live births in 1972 to 2.1 deaths in 2022.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Child mortality rate |url=https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/child-mortality-igme?tab=line&country=~IDN |access-date=21 January 2025 |website=Our World in Data}}</ref> Indonesia has also maintained polio-free status, although polio immunisation still requires sustained national and local support.<ref name="Azizatunnisa" />

== Culture ==
{{Main|Culture of Indonesia}}
{{See also|National Intangible Cultural Heritage of Indonesia|Public holidays in Indonesia}}
Indonesia's cultures differ across islands, languages, and communities. Older populations and later Austronesian migrations contributed to this diversity, with Papuan and Melanesian populations prominent in the east.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=6}}{{sfn |Taylor |2003 |pp=7}} Sea trade and migration linked the islands with India, Arabia, Persia, China, mainland Southeast Asia, and later Europe; these contacts brought religions, scripts, court arts, and colonial rule, often first through ports and trading towns before spreading across the regions.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=5, 8–9}}{{sfn |Vickers |2013 |pp=2}}

Indonesia has no single uniform cultural tradition. Regional arts and customs vary by local history, language, and community, while national forms use Indonesian and reach audiences across the country.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Yampolsky |first=P. |year=1995 |title=Forces for Change in the Regional Performing Arts of Indonesia |journal=Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde |volume=151 |issue=4 |pages=700–725 |doi=10.1163/22134379-90003035}}</ref> These traditions include performance, visual art, ritual, clothing, food, and literature.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=51–67, 127–147, 151–177}} Modern popular culture includes cinema, television drama, music, celebrity media, and reality shows, along with imported Asian dramas and global formats adapted for Indonesian audiences.{{sfn |Heryanto |2008 |pp=32–34, 119–121}}

Indonesia has 16 items on the [[UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage Lists]], including [[wayang]] puppet theatre, [[batik]], [[angklung]], the [[saman dance]], and [[pencak silat]]. Recent joint nominations have added [[pantun]], [[kebaya]], and [[kolintang]] to the list.<ref>{{cite web |title=Indonesia – Intangible heritage, cultural sector |url=https://ich.unesco.org/en/state/indonesia-ID?info=elements-on-the-lists |access-date=21 January 2025 |publisher=UNESCO}}</ref>

=== Art and architecture ===
{{Main|Indonesian art|Architecture of Indonesia}}
{{Further|Indonesian painting}}
[[File:Raden Saleh - Diponegoro arrest.jpg|thumb|upright=1.1|''[[The Arrest of Pangeran Diponegoro]]'' (1857) by [[Raden Saleh]]]]

Indonesian visual arts include textiles, puppetry, masks, carving, temple painting, and modern painting.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=52–67}} Many are part of ritual and court life, mark status or regional identity, and have changed with trade, colonial rule, nationalism, tourism, and urban art communities.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=52–67, 189, 196, 200–203, 210–211}}

Among regional traditions, Balinese painting includes classical [[Kamasan]] and [[Wayang]]-style narrative forms.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Campbell |first1=S. |date=2014 |title=Kamasan Art in Museum Collections: 'Entangled' Histories of Art Collecting in Bali |journal=Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde |publisher=Brill |volume=170 |pages=250–280 |doi=10.1163/22134379-17002001}}</ref> Indonesian architecture ranges from everyday houses to ritual houses (''[[rumah adat]]''), whose layout, posts, roofs, heirlooms, and construction rites can mark ancestry, status, and local belief.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=83–105}} Regional forms include the Javanese ''[[Pendopo|pendapa]]'', [[Dayak people|Dayak]] longhouses, Minangkabau ''[[Rumah Gadang]]'', and Toraja ''[[Tongkonan]]''.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=91–100}}

Sculptural traditions include megalithic monuments in parts of Java, Sumatra, Sulawesi, Nias, Sumba, Flores, and other regions.<ref name="Steimer-Herbet" /> Ngaju Dayak ''hampatong'' figures have commemorative and protective functions, while Asmat artefacts are made for aesthetic and spiritual purposes.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Maiullari |first=M. |date=1 January 2004 |title=Hampatongs in the Daily Life of the Ngaju Dayaks |url=https://www.thejamart.com/pdfs/photo/2004%20BRB%20vol%2035.pdf |url-status=live |journal=Borneo Research Bulletin |publisher=The Free Library |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260603023606/https://www.thejamart.com/pdfs/photo/2004%20BRB%20vol%2035.pdf |archive-date=3 June 2026 |access-date=3 June 2026}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last=de Hontheim |first=A. |date=12 October 2010 |title=Imagination behind Shape: The Invisible Content of Asmat Artefacts |journal=Anthropological Forum |volume=20 |issue=3 |pages=235–249 |doi=10.1080/00664677.2010.515292}}</ref> Ancient Indonesian sculpture from the 8th to 15th centuries includes Hindu and Buddhist stone, bronze, and gold works.<ref>{{cite book |last=Fontein |first=J. |url=https://openlibrary.org/books/OL20330025M/Sculpture_of_Indonesia |title=The Sculpture of Indonesia |date=1 October 1990 |publisher=Abrams, Inc |isbn=978-0-894-68141-7 |access-date=29 December 2025}}</ref> Surviving temple architecture from the same period includes [[Borobudur]] and [[Prambanan]].<ref>{{cite web |title=Borobudur Temple Compounds |url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/592 |access-date=21 January 2025 |publisher=UNESCO World Heritage Centre}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=Prambanan Temple Compounds |url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/642 |access-date=25 December 2025 |publisher=UNESCO World Heritage Centre}}</ref>

=== Music, dance and clothing ===
{{Main|Music of Indonesia|Dance in Indonesia|National costume of Indonesia}}
[[File:COLLECTIE TROPENMUSEUM Katoenen wikkelrok met geometrisch patroon TMnr 5713-2.jpg|thumb|right|upright|An Indonesian [[batik]]]]

Indonesian music and dance include court, village, ritual, and popular forms. [[Gamelan]] traditions are centred especially in Java and Bali, while [[angklung]] and other bamboo ensembles are used in western Java, Banyumas, and other regions. Drums, gongs, lutes, singing, and dance music also appear across the archipelago.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=189-195}} Islamic musical forms include [[Qanbūs|gambus]] and [[qasida]],<ref>{{cite book |title=Divine Inspirations |date=2011 |isbn=978-0-19-538541-0 |editor1-last=Harnish |editor1-first=David |pages=207–240 |doi=10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195385410.001.0001 |editor2-last=Rasmussen |editor2-first=Anne}}</ref> while popular genres include [[Kroncong|keroncong]]{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=195}} and [[dangdut]], which grew from Melayu music and draws on Indian, Arabic, and Western pop and rock elements.{{sfn |Heryanto |2008 |pp=13}}

Dance traditions serve different uses across regions. Mask dances appear in Javanese court drama and Balinese ritual performance, while Dayak mask dances are performed in agricultural rituals and community ceremonies.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=56–57, 196–203}} Javanese court dance includes forms such as ''[[bedhaya]]'' and ''[[serimpi]]'', while other local dances accompany theatre, music, trance, healing, harvest, and rites of passage.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=196–203}} Younger performers also adapt global forms, including K-pop cover dance in Bali and hip-hop communities in Yogyakarta.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Oh |first1=C. |date=10 September 2024 |title=Authenticities of K-pop Cover Dance Influencers in/from Bali, Indonesia |journal=Dance Chronicle |publisher=Taylor & Francis Online |volume=47 |issue=3 |pages=407–432 |doi=10.1080/01472526.2024.2384976}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Dahlan |first1=A.F. |last2=Adi |first2=I.R. |date=April 2025 |title=Ideology, Values and Norms in Hip-hop Community Wijilan Yogyakarta: An Ethnographic Study |journal=Rubikon: Journal of Transnational American Studies |publisher=Gadjah Mada University |volume=12 |pages=57–85 |doi=10.22146/rubikon.v12i1.101571 |doi-access=free |number=1}}</ref>

Clothing traditions differ by region and are used in ceremonies, weddings, formal occasions, and markers of local identity.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=120, 140-147}} ''[[Batik]]'' and ''[[kebaya]]'' are widely used in national and formal dress and are especially prominent in Javanese culture.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=65, 143}} Other regional textiles and clothing traditions include the Batak [[ulos]], Malay and Minangkabau [[songket]], and Sasak [[ikat]], which are used in ceremonies, weddings, exchange, and formal events.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=138-147}}

=== Theatre and cinema ===
{{Main|Cinema of Indonesia|Theatre of Indonesia}}
{{Further|List of highest-grossing films in Indonesia}}
[[File:Wayang Wong Bharata Pandawa.jpg|thumb|left|The ''[[Pandava]]s'' and ''[[Krishna]]'' in an act of the ''Wayang Wong'' performance|alt=]]

Traditional Indonesian theatre uses storytelling, music, movement, puppetry, masks, and actor-dance.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=52, 189, 196, 200-205}} ''[[Wayang]]'' is a major puppet-theatre tradition in Java and Bali; many performances draw on the ''Ramayana'' and ''Mahabharata'', are led by a ''dalang'', and are accompanied by music.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Lis |first=Marianna |year=2019 |title=The Art of Wayang |journal=Art of the Orient |volume=8 |pages=137–150 |doi=10.11588/ao.2019.0.10636 |doi-access=free}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last=Orr |first=Inge C. |year=1974 |title=Puppet Theatre in Asia |url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/i250310 |journal=Asian Folklore Studies |volume=33 |issue=1 |pages=69–84 |access-date=6 May 2026}}</ref> Wayang has been used for ritual, moral instruction, comedy, political comment, and public messaging.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=196, 200-205}}{{sfn |Weintraub |2004 |pp=107-127}}

Other theatrical traditions include ''[[Ludruk]]'', ''[[Ketoprak]]'', ''[[Sandiwara]]'', and ''[[Lenong]]''.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Sedana |first1=I.N. |url=https://www.routledge.com/Routledge-Handbook-of-Asian-Theatre/Liu/p/book/9781138099319 |title=Routledge Handbook of Asian Theatre |last2=Foley |first2=K. |date=2016 |publisher=Routledge |isbn=978-0-415-82155-1 |editor-last=Liu |editor-first=S. |chapter=Traditional Indonesian theatre |access-date=29 December 2025}}</ref> The Minangkabau ''[[Randai]]'' uses music, dance, drama, and martial arts (''[[silat]]'') to perform legends and historical narratives.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Pauka |first=K. |year=1998 |title=The Daughters Take Over? Female Performers in Randai Theatre |journal=The Drama Review |volume=42 |issue=1 |pages=113–121 |doi=10.1162/105420498760308706}}</ref> Balinese masked dance theatre, including ''[[Topeng dance|topeng]]'', has also been staged with modern stories and contemporary performance.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Dibia |first=I Wayan |year=2016 |title=Experimenting the Modern Story "Mr. Tepis" to Balinese Topeng Masked Dance Theatre |journal=Malaysian Journal of Performing and Visual Arts |volume=2 |issue=1 |pages=71–85 |doi=10.22452/mjpva.vol2no1.4}}</ref> In the modern period, theatre groups and networks, including ''Teater Koma'', staged social criticism and political opposition during the late New Order period.<ref>{{cite book |last=Bodden |first=M.H. |url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1j7x85s |title=Resistance on the National Stage: Theater and Politics in Late New Order Indonesia |date=2010 |publisher=Ohio University Press |isbn=978-0-89680-469-2 |series=Research in International Studies, Southeast Asia Series |access-date=29 December 2025}}</ref>

Indonesian cinema began during the Dutch colonial period with ''[[Loetoeng Kasaroeng]]'' (1926),<ref name="deLeonEspena">{{citation |last1=de Leon Espena |first1=D.M. |title=Cinema and politics: the creation of postcolonial self/other and the shaping of strategic cultures in Southeast Asia, 1945-1967 |date=2017 |type=thesis |publisher=Nanyang Technological University |doi=10.32657/10356/69958 |doi-access=free}}</ref> and post-independence filmmaking developed through figures such as [[Usmar Ismail]] and [[Djamaluddin Malik]].<ref name="deLeonEspena" /> During the Sukarno era, film became part of nationalist and anti-colonial politics,<ref name="deLeonEspena" /> while New Order cinema was subject to censorship, state regulation, and official histories.<ref name="vanHeeren">{{cite book |last=van Heeren |first=Katinka |url=https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/34522 |title=Contemporary Indonesian Film: Spirits of Reform and Ghosts from the Past |publisher=KITLV Press |year=2012 |isbn=978-90-6718-381-9 |series=Verhandelingen van het Koninklijk Instituut voor Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde |volume=277 |location=Leiden |access-date=6 May 2026}}</ref> The large film industry of the 1970s and 1980s declined in the early 1990s.{{sfn |Heryanto |2008 |pp=43-56}}

After 1998, low-budget independent films, film communities, festivals, and returning commercial producers revived the industry.{{sfn |Heryanto |2008 |pp=43-56}} ''Kuldesak'' (1999) helped launch this independent film movement, while ''[[Ada Apa dengan Cinta?]]'' (2002) showed the commercial reach of post-Suharto cinema.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Paramaditha |first=I. |date=1 January 2017 |title=Film Studies in Indonesia: An Experiment of a New Generation |journal=Journal of the Humanities and Social Sciences of Southeast Asia |publisher=Brill |volume=173 |issue=2–3 |pages=357–375 |doi=10.1163/22134379-17302006 |doi-access=free}}</ref>{{sfn |Heryanto |2008 |pp=43-56}} Filmmakers addressed sexuality, city life, corruption, religion, and political memory, but state censorship, self-censorship, and pressure from non-state groups still limited expression.<ref name="vanHeeren" /><ref>{{cite journal |last=Paramaditha |first=Intan |year=2011 |title=City and desire in Indonesian cinema |journal=Inter-Asia Cultural Studies |volume=12 |issue=4 |pages=500–512 |doi=10.1080/14649373.2011.603915}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last=Paramaditha |first=I. |url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1xwgkr |title=Southeast Asian Independent Cinema |publisher=Hong Kong University Press |year=2011 |isbn=9789888083602 |editor-last=Baumgärtel |editor-first=T. |location=Hong Kong |pages=69–88 |chapter=Cinema, sexuality and censorship in post-Soeharto Indonesia |access-date=3 June 2026}}</ref>{{sfn |Heryanto |2008 |pp=43-44}} The [[Indonesian Film Festival]] (''Festival Film Indonesia''), first held in 1955, is the country's main national film-awards event.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Undiana |first1=Nala Nandana |last2=Abdullah |first2=Aceng |last3=Agustin |first3=Herlina |last4=Wirakusumah |first4=Teddy Kurnia |year=2025 |title=Identity of the National Film Festival in Indonesia |journal=Revista Guillermo de Ockham |volume=23 |issue=1 |pages=157–173 |doi=10.21500/22563202.7068 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

=== Literature and mass media ===
{{Main|Indonesian literature|Mass media in Indonesia}}
[[File:Pramudya Ananta Tur Kesusastraan Modern Indonesia p226.jpg|thumb|right|upright|[[Pramoedya Ananta Toer]]]]

Indonesian literature includes oral storytelling, inscriptions, court chronicles, religious texts, poetry, prose, and modern writing in Indonesian and regional languages.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=67-78}} Early writing used Indian-derived scripts and later Malay and Islamic literary forms, including [[Kawi script|Kawi]], [[Jawi alphabet|Jawi]], ''[[syair]]'', ''[[pantun]]'', ''[[hikayat]]'', and ''[[babad]]''.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=67-70}} Well-known examples include ''[[Hikayat Hang Tuah]]'' in the Malay tradition and ''[[Babad Tanah Jawi]]'' in the Javanese tradition.<ref>{{cite book |last=Braginsky |first=V. |url=https://brill.com/display/title/23610 |title=The Heritage of Traditional Malay Literature: A Historical Survey of Genres, Writings and Literary Views |publisher=KITLV Press |year=2004 |isbn=90-6718-214-1 |location=Leiden |access-date=29 April 2026}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |title=Babad Tanah Jawi, The Chronicle of Java: The Revised Prose Version of C. F. Winter Sr. |publisher=Leiden University Press |year=2022 |editor-last=Remmelink |editor-first=W. |location=Leiden |translator-last=Remmelink |translator-first=W. |doi=10.1515/9789400604230}}</ref>

Modern Indonesian writing grew in the early 20th century through newspapers, Malay/Indonesian-language publishing, and the colonial publishing house ''[[Balai Pustaka]]''.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=70-72}}{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=232-233}} Sumatran, especially Minangkabau, writers were prominent in early modern literature,<ref>{{cite book |last=Joy Freidus |first=A. |url=https://researchrepository.rmit.edu.au/discovery/fulldisplay/alma9922306786401341/61RMIT_INST:RMITU |title=Sumatran Contributions to the Development of Indonesian Literature, 1920–1942 |publisher=Asian Studies Program, University of Hawaii |year=1977 |access-date=22 September 2015}}</ref> while later figures such as [[Chairil Anwar]], [[Pramoedya Ananta Toer]], and [[Ayu Utami]] became prominent at different points in modern Indonesian literature.{{sfn |Ricklefs |2001 |pp=264}}{{sfn |Heryanto |2008 |pp=32-34}}

Indonesian mass media has operated under state rules, commercial ownership, and changing communications technology.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Sen |first1=K. |url=https://catalogue.nla.gov.au/catalog/1828755 |title=Media, Culture and Politics in Indonesia |last2=Hill |first2=D.T. |publisher=Oxford University Press |year=2000 |isbn=978-0-19-553703-1 |location=Melbourne |access-date=3 June 2026}}</ref> During the New Order, newspapers and broadcasters worked under licensing, censorship, and television policies that promoted development and official ideas of national culture.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Kitley |first1=P. |url=https://www.dymocks.com.au/television-nation-and-culture-in-indonesia-by-philip-kitley-9780896802124 |title=Television, Nation, and Culture in Indonesia |date=2000 |publisher=Ohio University Press |edition=1 |series=Research in International Studies, Southeast Asia Series |access-date=7 August 2025}}</ref> In the ''Reformasi'' era, the 1999 Press Law forbade censorship, press bans, and publication permits, but criminal and civil cases, political pressure, and attacks by private groups continued.<ref>{{citation |last1=Wiratraman |first1=H.P. |title=Press freedom, law and politics in Indonesia: a socio-legal study |date=11 December 2014 |publisher=Leiden University Scholarly Publication |isbn=978-94-6203-733-5 |hdl=1887/30106}}</ref>

Internet use began in the early 1990s and grew quickly after 2000;<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Jennifer Yang |first1=H. |date=12 January 2010 |title=The Internet in Indonesia: Development and Impact of Radical Websites |journal=Studies in Conflict & Terrorism |publisher=Routledge |volume=33 |issue=2 |pages=171–191 |doi=10.1080/10576100903400605 |hdl=10356/79840 |hdl-access=free}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=Share of the population using the Internet - Indonesia |url=https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/share-of-individuals-using-the-internet?tab=chart&country=~IDN&mapSelect=~IDN |access-date=18 July 2025 |publisher=Our World in Data}}</ref> by 2023, Indonesia had over 210 million internet users, helped by affordable smartphones and wider mobile-broadband coverage.<ref>{{cite report |url=https://lkyspp.nus.edu.sg/docs/default-source/case-studies/the-rise-of-indonesia-s-digital-economy.pdf?sfvrsn=c607020a_0 |title=The Rise of Indonesia's Digital Economy |last=Taojun |first=X. |date=March 2025 |publisher=Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy |access-date=8 May 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250508031750/https://lkyspp.nus.edu.sg/docs/default-source/case-studies/the-rise-of-indonesia-s-digital-economy.pdf?sfvrsn=c607020a_0 |archive-date=8 May 2025 |url-status=live |work=LKYSPP Case Study Library}}</ref> Mainstream outlets now use social-media distribution, shared newsrooms, shorter online formats, and cost-cutting.<ref>{{citation |last1=Syarief |first1=S. |title=Indonesian Mainstream Media in the Digital Age: Corporate Convergence, Low-quality News and Staff Exploitation |date=18 April 2024 |work=ISEAS Perspective |volume=29 |number=29 |url=https://www.iseas.edu.sg/?p=63771 |access-date=4 November 2025 |publisher=ISEAS - Yusof Ishak Institute}}</ref>

=== Cuisine ===
{{Main|Indonesian cuisine}}
[[File:Nasi ramas rendang.JPG|thumb|left|''[[Nasi Padang]]'' with ''[[rendang]]'', ''[[gulai]]'', and vegetables, an example of [[Padang cuisine|Minangkabau cuisine]]|alt=]]

Indonesian cuisine differs by island and region. Local crops, fish, spices, religion, trade, and migration all affect what people cook and eat.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=127-130, 132-137}} Food traditions use older local ingredients and techniques, along with later Indian, Chinese, Middle Eastern, Portuguese, Dutch, and other foreign influences.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=127-130}}<ref name="Wijaya">{{cite journal |last1=Wijaya |first1=S. |date=18 September 2019 |title=Indonesian food culture mapping: a starter contribution to promote Indonesian culinary tourism |journal=Journal of Ethnic Foods |publisher=Springer Nature Link |volume=6 |article-number=9 |doi=10.1186/s42779-019-0009-3 |doi-access=free |number=9}}</ref>

Rice is the main [[staple food]] across much of the archipelago, usually consumed with side dishes of meat, fish, vegetables, and condiments.<ref name="Wijaya" /> Common ingredients and seasonings include chilli, [[coconut milk]], shrimp paste, peanuts, garlic, shallots, tamarind, fish, and chicken.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=129-132}} Soy-based foods such as ''[[tempeh]]'' and ''[[tofu|tahu]]'' are also common, especially in Java and Bali.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=132}}

Dishes such as ''[[nasi goreng]]'', ''[[gado-gado]]'', ''[[Noodle|mie]]'', and ''[[Satay|sate]]'' are consumed in many parts of the country.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=131-133}} Regional cuisines include [[Padang cuisine|Minangkabau]] dishes such as ''[[rendang]]''.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=132}}<ref name="Wijaya" /> ''[[Tumpeng]]'', a Javanese ceremonial rice dish, is used in rites of passage, thanksgiving, and public celebrations.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=A.P. Jati |first1=I.R. |date=8 July 2014 |title=Local wisdom behind Tumpeng as an icon of Indonesian traditional cuisine |journal=Nutrition & Food Science |publisher=Emerald Publishing |volume=44 |pages=324–334 |doi=10.1108/NFS-11-2013-0141 |number=4}}</ref>

=== Sports ===
{{Main|Sports in Indonesia|Indonesian martial arts}}
[[File:Pencak Silat Betawi 1.jpg|thumb|upright|A demonstration of ''[[pencak silat]]'', a form of martial arts]]

Indonesia has international sports such as [[Association football|football]], [[badminton]], [[Olympic weightlifting|weightlifting]], and basketball, alongside local games and martial arts.{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=174-177}} Football is one of the country's most followed sports, with active club supporters and public screenings of major matches.<ref name="Prawira">{{cite journal |last1=Prawira |first1=A. |last2=Suhianto |first2=Y. |date=29 November 2024 |title=The Development of the Indonesian Football Industry and its influence Towards National Reputation |url=https://journal.unpar.ac.id/index.php/Sentris/article/view/7193 |journal=Sentris |publisher=Student Study Group for International Issues - Parahyangan Catholic University |volume=5 |pages=89–99 |access-date=21 July 2025 |number=2}}</ref> [[Indonesia national football team|Indonesia]] was the first Asian representative to appear at the [[FIFA World Cup]], taking part in the [[1938 FIFA World Cup|1938 tournament]] as the Dutch East Indies.<ref>{{cite web |date=20 November 2025 |title=Indonesia at the 1938 FIFA World Cup |url=https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/articles/indonesia-first-asian-team |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251207192236/https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/articles/indonesia-first-asian-team |archive-date=7 December 2025 |access-date=6 May 2026 |publisher=FIFA}}</ref>

[[Badminton]] is one of Indonesia's most successful international sports. Indonesia has won both the [[Thomas Cup|Thomas]] and [[Uber Cup]]s, the world team championships of men's and women's badminton.<ref>{{cite web |title=The Historic Journey of the Thomas Cup & Uber Cup |url=https://bwfthomasubercups.bwfbadminton.com/history/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250619125358/https://bwfthomasubercups.bwfbadminton.com/history/ |archive-date=19 June 2025 |access-date=21 July 2025 |publisher=BWF Badminton}}</ref> Most of [[Indonesia at the Olympics|Indonesia's Olympic medals]] have come from badminton and weightlifting.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Muammar |first=E. |date=April 2025 |title=Stagnation of Achievements of Indonesian Athletes at the International Level |journal=Journal Research of Social Science, Economics, and Management |publisher=Publikasi Indonesia |volume=4 |pages=1099–1114 |doi=10.59141/jrssem.v4i9.813 |number=9}}</ref> Basketball also has a long organised history in the country, having appeared at the first [[National Sports Week (Indonesia)|National Sports Week]] in 1948 before the national basketball association was founded in 1951.<ref>{{cite web |title=History of Basketball in Indonesia |url=http://www.nblindonesia.com/v1/index.php?page=abouten |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160908220708/http://www.nblindonesia.com/v1/index.php?page=abouten |archive-date=8 September 2016 |access-date=8 September 2016 |publisher=National Basketball League Indonesia}}</ref>

Local sports and games are used in ceremonies, harvest events, contests, and public entertainment. Examples include ''[[sepak takraw]]'', bull racing (''[[karapan sapi]]'') in [[Madura]],{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=176}}<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Debby |first1=A.S. |last2=Setiawan |first2=A.R. |date=25 December 2023 |title=Karapan Sapi (Cow Race) Cultures in Madura: Economics Values and Owners' Prestige |journal=International Journal of Religious and Cultural Studies |volume=5 |pages=111–128 |doi=10.34199/ijracs.2023.10.04 |doi-access=free |number=2}}</ref> and ritual combat traditions such as ''caci'' in [[Flores]] and ''[[pasola]]'' in [[Sumba]].{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=174-175, 219}} ''[[Pencak silat]]'' is an Indonesian martial art{{sfn |Forshee |2006 |pp=174}} and was included as an official event at the 2018 Asian Games, where Indonesia won all fourteen gold medals contested in the sport.<ref>{{cite web |date=30 August 2018 |title=Indonesia Sweeps Pencak Silat Gold Medals at Asian Games |url=https://setkab.go.id/en/indonesia-sweeps-pencak-silat-gold-medals-at-asian-games/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250423055121/https://setkab.go.id/en/indonesia-sweeps-pencak-silat-gold-medals-at-asian-games/ |archive-date=23 April 2025 |access-date=6 May 2026 |publisher=Cabinet Secretariat of the Republic of Indonesia}}</ref>
